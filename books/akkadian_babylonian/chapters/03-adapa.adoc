== Adapa and the South Wind

=== The text and how it survives

_Adapa and the South Wind_ survives in Akkadian on six fragmentary witnesses across two recensions: a single Middle Babylonian copy from Amarna in Egypt (Fragment B = VAT 348 = EA 356, Vorderasiatisches Museum Berlin, 14th century BCE) and five Neo-Assyrian copies from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh (Fragments A, A1, C, D, and E, with MLC 1296 in the Pierpont Morgan Library and K.15072, K.8743 + duplicates in the British Museum, 7th century BCE).footnote:[Izre'el 2001, *Adapa and the South Wind: Language Has the Power of Life and Death*, pp. 9–11 (manuscript catalogue); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 525, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 525

The standard critical edition is Shlomo Izre'el's _Adapa and the South Wind: Language Has the Power of Life and Death_ (Mesopotamian Civilizations 10, Eisenbrauns 2001), which edits each fragment separately, with synoptic alignment where the Amarna and Nineveh witnesses parallel one another, and provides English translation, philological commentary, and recensional analysis.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, full volume; English translation pp. 9–73.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-73
The accessible English translation cited throughout for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "Adapa," in _Before the Muses_, third edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 525–530.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 525–530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 525-530
A third translation, by Stephanie Dalley in _Myths from Mesopotamia_ (revised edition, Oxford World's Classics 2008), pp. 182–188, serves as a triangulation reference.footnote:[Dalley 2008, pp. 182–188.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 182-188

Fragment B — the Amarna copy — is the longest single witness and provides the bulk of the south-wind incident, Ea's instructions, and the heaven-audience scene; its reverse is partly damaged, and the conclusion of the audience and Adapa's return are partly broken.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and Fragment B edition; Foster 2005, pp. 525–528.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
The Nineveh fragments (A, A1, C, D, E) are individually shorter but preserve, between them, both the cosmological/wisdom prologue (Fragment A's hymn to the sage's intelligence) and a substantively different framing of the heaven-audience climax (Fragment C is an expanded parallel to Fragment B), as well as an alternate ending in which Adapa is honoured by Anu (Fragment D).footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Nineveh fragments edition; Foster 2005, pp. 528–530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Nineveh fragments edition
Neither recension alone is complete; both are needed for the full narrative. Izre'el's edition presents each fragment separately, with synoptic alignment where the Amarna and Nineveh witnesses parallel one another, rather than producing a single composite recension.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 (recensional methodology); Foster 2005, p. 525, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11

There is no Old Babylonian Akkadian _Adapa_. The Sumerian-language tablet from Tell Haddad (ancient Me-Turan), published by Cavigneaux and Al-Rawi 1993, is sometimes cited in older literature as an Old Babylonian witness; it is in fact a Sumerian composition and belongs to the Sumerian literary tradition rather than to the Akkadian _Adapa_ corpus.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. xv–xviii (history of scholarship); ETCSL 1.7.5 (the Sumerian Adapa from Tell Haddad), cited as comparative material only.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. xv-xviii
// EVIDENCE: source_id=etcsl ; loc=ETCSL 1.7.5 (comparative)

Adapa is integrated into the _apkallu_ (antediluvian-sage) tradition by the Late Babylonian period, where he is reckoned the first of seven sages who brought civilization to humanity before the flood.footnote:[Kvanvig 2011, *Primeval History*, pp. 107–115 (Adapa and the apkallu lineage); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 71–73; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Apkallu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 107-115
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 71-73
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Apkallu
The _apkallu_ lineage that names Adapa as the first of seven sages is a Late Babylonian overlay on the original story; it is not present in the Amarna copy, where Adapa is simply Ea's priest at Eridu.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and commentary; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 107–115, on the stratification.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 107-115

=== Setting: Adapa, priest of Ea at Eridu

Adapa is the priest of Ea at Eridu, the oldest city in Mesopotamian tradition and Ea's principal cult-centre.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B opening; Foster 2005, p. 526; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Adapa.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B opening
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Adapa
In his role as Ea's priest, Adapa daily fishes the Persian Gulf to provide the temple with food offerings.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (south-wind episode opening); Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B

The Nineveh recension introduces Adapa with a hymn to his exceptional wisdom (preserved in Fragment A): he is _atra-ḫasīs_ (the same epithet as the flood hero), perfected with great intelligence; Ea has given him wisdom but not eternal life.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment A (Nineveh, MLC 1296 — the cosmological/wisdom prologue); Foster 2005, p. 526; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 105–110.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment A
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 105-110

Adapa's name appears in variant readings: Adapa (the standard, attested across all Akkadian fragments) and U-an / U-anna (the form in the Late Babylonian _apkallu_ genealogy, where the first sage is "U-an Adapa").footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 11–13 (name variants); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Adapa, Apkallu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 11-13
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Adapa

=== The Amarna recension (Fragment B): the south wind and Ea's warning

In the Amarna recension, Adapa goes out fishing one day in his boat.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (south-wind episode); Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
The south wind (Akkadian _šūtu_) rises against him and capsizes his boat into the freshwater Apsû.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B; Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
Adapa is enraged. He pronounces a curse against the south wind, and her wing is broken.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (the breaking of the south wind's wing); Foster 2005, p. 526; Dalley 2008, p. 185 ("South Wind's wing was broken" — singular event).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 185
The south wind ceases blowing for seven days. The cosmic balance is disrupted; the marshland breeze that cools the alluvium is gone.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (the seven-day cessation); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B

Anu in the heavens notices the absence and demands an explanation. He summons Adapa to his court to answer for the breaking of the south wind's wing.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (Anu's summons); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
Ea, anticipating the summons, instructs Adapa in detail before he ascends to heaven.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (Ea's instruction scene); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B

Ea's instructions specify ritual and rhetorical preparations: dress in mourning clothes; let your hair go unkempt; at the gate of heaven Tammuz and Gizzida will challenge you, mourning their own seasonal absence from the land of the living — say that you mourn for them, and they will intercede for you.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (Ea's instructions); Foster 2005, p. 527; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tammuz, Ningishzida.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tammuz
Tammuz (also called Dumuzi in the Akkadian tradition) and Gizzida (the Akkadian form of the Sumerian Ningišzida — an underworld-vegetation god) stand as Anu's gatekeepers in this scene; they have descended to the underworld in their seasonal pattern and are mourning their own exile from the world above.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B; Foster 2005, p. 527; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tammuz, Ningishzida.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B

Ea's central instruction concerns what Anu will offer Adapa once the audience proper begins. Anu will offer "the bread of death" (_akala ša mūti_) and "the water of death" (_mê ša mūti_); Ea warns Adapa not to eat or drink them — they will kill him.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (Ea's central warning); Foster 2005, p. 527; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–125 (the bread/water of life/death cruxes).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-125
Ea's instructions are framed primarily as negative warnings about what Adapa must refuse (the bread and the water); the oil and the clothing that Anu will also offer are not prohibited, and Adapa accepts them when they are presented at the audience.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B; Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B

Adapa ascends to heaven by the road that leads up.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (the heaven-ascent); Foster 2005, p. 528.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
At Anu's gate Adapa meets Tammuz and Gizzida. He gives the answer Ea coached him to give — that he mourns for them, who have gone from the land — and the two gods are favourably impressed. They intercede with Anu on his behalf.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (heaven-gate scene); Foster 2005, p. 528.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B

[LACUNA: Fragment B (Amarna, EA 356), reverse — the conclusion of the heaven audience and Adapa's return are partly broken | source: Izre'el 2001, Fragment B edition and apparatus | scholarly_reconstruction: partial; Fragment C (Nineveh, K.8743) supplies an expanded parallel to the Amarna heaven-audience scene but is not strictly substitutable]

=== The Nineveh recension (Fragments A, A1, C, D, E): cosmology, audience, and an alternate ending

[LACUNA: Fragments A and A1 (Nineveh, MLC 1296 + K.15072), beginning and middle — Adapa's identity and the framing of the south-wind incident are partly lost | source: Izre'el 2001, Nineveh fragments edition | scholarly_reconstruction: yes — Fragment A1 parallels the last extant section of Fragment A; Izre'el reconstructs the SB cosmological prologue]

[RECONSTRUCTION: The cosmological/wisdom prologue of the Nineveh recension — including the hymn to Adapa's intelligence ("perfected him with great intelligence... to him he gave wisdom, he did not give him eternal life") — is reconstructed from the parallel readings of Fragments A and A1. | gap_source: Fragment A (Nineveh, MLC 1296) damaged sections | fill_source: Fragment A1 (Nineveh, K.15072), which parallels the last extant section of Fragment A; Izre'el 2001 collates the two | confidence: medium]

The Nineveh recension preserves a substantial cosmological/wisdom prologue (Fragment A) that the Amarna fragment lacks. The prologue establishes Adapa as a sage perfected with great intelligence by Ea — given wisdom, but not eternal life.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment A (the cosmological/wisdom prologue); Foster 2005, p. 528; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 105–110.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment A
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 105-110
Fragment C (K.8743 in the British Museum) is an expanded parallel to part of Fragment B's heaven-audience scene. After Tammuz and Gizzida intercede, Anu's anger turns to a question: who has revealed the secrets of heaven and earth to this man?footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment C (Nineveh, K.8743 — heaven-audience expansion); Foster 2005, pp. 528–529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment C
Anu's question identifies Ea as the source of Adapa's wisdom. The audience shifts from a court-disciplinary scene to a recognition-and-honour scene.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment C; Foster 2005, p. 529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment C

In a turn that the Amarna recension does not preserve in full, Anu now offers Adapa not the bread of death but "the bread of life" (_akala ša balāṭi_) and "the water of life" (_mê ša balāṭi_) — the food and drink that confer immortality.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment C (the bread-of-life scene); Foster 2005, p. 529; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–125.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment C
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-125
Adapa, faithful to Ea's instructions — instructions Ea had framed as a warning against the bread of _death_ — refuses the bread and the water that Anu now offers him.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment C (the refusal); Foster 2005, p. 529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment C
Adapa accepts the oil and the clothing — and is anointed and dressed.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment C; Foster 2005, p. 529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment C

Anu's reaction is the interpretive crux of the poem. Different fragments preserve different readings between laughter and sorrow. He observes that Adapa has refused immortality, and that the loss is now permanent — for Adapa and for humanity through him.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment C (Anu's reaction); Foster 2005, pp. 529–530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment C

In Fragment B's ending, Adapa is sent back down to the earth without the immortality he was offered. The opportunity is foreclosed; humanity will remain mortal.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment B (the Amarna ending); Foster 2005, p. 530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment B
Fragment D, by contrast, preserves an alternate ending in which Anu honours Adapa: "Anu set a decree to make glorious his lordship forever," and Adapa appears to remain in heaven as the chosen of Anu — the opposite outcome to Fragment B.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, Fragment D (the alternate ending); Foster 2005, p. 530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=Fragment D
The two endings are co-equal recensional variants: the Amarna (Fragment B) loss-of-immortality narrative and the Nineveh (Fragment D) honoured-by-Anu narrative are not reconcilable into a single composite, and Izre'el 2001 presents them side by side rather than choosing one as the primary outcome.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 (recensional methodology); Foster 2005, p. 530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11

=== The interpretive crux: was Ea's warning a deception?

The poem turns on a misreading: Ea warns Adapa against the bread of _death_, but Anu offers the bread of _life_. Whether Ea was protecting Adapa or deliberately misleading him is the central interpretive question of the text.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, p. 121 (commentary on the warning); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–135 (survey of readings); Foster 2005, p. 525, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=p. 121
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-135

One reading, attested in scholarship since the early 20th century, treats Ea as having given a sincere warning that turned out to be wrong — Ea did not know what Anu would offer.footnote:[Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–135 (survey of the sincere-warning reading); Izre'el 2001 commentary.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-135
A second reading, more recent, treats Ea as deliberately deceptive — Ea is the god of wisdom who knows the secrets of heaven, and he chose to keep immortality from Adapa.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, p. 121 (deception reading); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 130–135.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=p. 121
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 130-135
A third reading treats the warning as a misunderstanding by Adapa rather than a deception by Ea: Adapa heard "death" because he was cued for danger, and missed the chance to recognise that Anu's posture had changed.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 121–125 (the misunderstanding reading); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 130–135.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 121-125

[INFERENCE: The poem's didactic function depends on the interpretive ambiguity rather than on its resolution. The text rewards rereading and produces a Mesopotamian commonplace — that human wisdom, even at its highest in a sage like Adapa, is bounded by structural limits. | basis: the text preserves the ambiguity rather than resolving it; Izre'el's commentary p. 121 and Kvanvig 2011 pp. 115–135 both treat the ambiguity as load-bearing; Foster 2005's headnote characterises the poem as a meditation on the limits of knowledge. | risk: the ambiguity-as-feature reading is itself a scholarly judgement; some readers prefer to settle on one of the three resolutions above.]

The recensional history of _Adapa_ is therefore not a story of one prior text descending into many copies but of two substantially different framings preserved in different scribal cultures. The Amarna copy was produced for the Egyptian royal archive; the Nineveh fragments were produced for Aššurbanipal's library; both descend from earlier traditions whose Old Babylonian Akkadian witnesses, if they ever existed, do not survive.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and pp. xv–xviii; Foster 2007, pp. 71–73.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. xv-xviii
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 71-73

=== The poem's place in the corpus

_Adapa_ is one of the Akkadian texts that consistently links the wisdom tradition to the cult of Ea at Eridu. The hero is Ea's priest, and the wisdom that fails him is Ea's wisdom.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 121–125 (commentary on the wisdom theme); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 105–115.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 121-125
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 105-115
In the Late Babylonian tradition, Adapa is integrated into the _apkallu_ list — the seven antediluvian sages who brought the arts of civilization to humanity before the flood. He is reckoned the first of the seven (U-an Adapa), and his story is invoked in apotropaic rituals that name the seven sages.footnote:[Kvanvig 2011, pp. 107–115 (apkallu lineage and ritual context); Foster 2007, pp. 71–73; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Apkallu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 107-115
This _apkallu_-frame is a later overlay. In the Amarna copy, Adapa is simply Ea's priest at Eridu; the seven-sage genealogy is not present.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and commentary; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 107–115.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
