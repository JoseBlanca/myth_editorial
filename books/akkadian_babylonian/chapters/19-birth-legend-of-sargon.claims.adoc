=== Source overview

*The Birth Legend of Sargon* (also called the *Legend of Sargon*, the *Sargon Birth Legend*, or the *Sargon naru*) is an Akkadian pseudo-autobiographical composition in which Sargon of Akkad — Šarru-kīn, "the king is true", founder of the Akkadian Empire ca. 2334–2279 BCE — addresses an unnamed audience from beyond his lifetime, recounting his obscure origins, his exposure as an infant in a basket of reeds upon the river, his rescue by a humble water-drawer, his gardener apprenticeship, the love of the goddess Ištar that elevated him to kingship, and the lands he conquered as king of Akkad. The composition is preserved in the first-millennium BCE Babylonian and Assyrian scribal tradition; the principal manuscript is K.3401, a Neo-Assyrian tablet from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh (7th c. BCE), supplemented by Neo-Babylonian duplicates.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, *Legends of the Kings of Akkade: The Texts*, pp. 36–49 (the definitive critical edition of the *Birth Legend of Sargon*: text, translation, and commentary, treating the Neo-Assyrian K.3401 and the Neo-Babylonian duplicates as a single recensional dossier with the autobiographical opening as the substantively preserved portion). Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, 3rd ed., pp. 912–913 (the principal working English translation). Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 95–98 (the Sargon legends in the late tradition; the *narû* genre context). Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. Sargon, Akkad. Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, passim on the royal-legend tradition.]

The legend is a first-millennium literary composition whose narrative subject is the historical Sargon of Akkad (Šarru-kīn), founder of the Akkadian dynasty and the first Mesopotamian ruler to assemble a territorial empire stretching from the Persian Gulf to upper Mesopotamia and beyond. The historical Sargon reigned approximately 2334–2279 BCE; his reign is attested in Old Akkadian royal inscriptions independent of the later legend. The first-millennium composition grew on this historical foundation; the legend is not a contemporary record but a much later literary elaboration of the historical figure's origins and rise.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 36–49, introduction (the historical Sargon ca. 2334–2279 BCE; the founding of the Akkadian dynasty; the historical context of the legend). Foster 2005, p. 912 (Sargon's historical context). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the legend as first-millennium literary elaboration on the historical foundation). Black & Green 1992, s.v. The composition belongs to the *narû* genre — pseudo-autobiographical royal literature in which a king (most often Sargon or his grandson Narām-Sîn) addresses a future ruler from the inscribed monument that bears his words. The *narû* genre uses the formal apparatus of the royal monumental inscription — first-person voice, dynastic self-presentation, address to a future audience — as the framing device for a literary narrative that is no longer a contemporary royal monument but a much later scribal composition projected back into the voice of the founder-king. The *Birth Legend of Sargon* is one of the principal Akkadian witnesses to the *narû* genre, alongside the *Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn* (chapter 24 of the present volume) and the *King of Battle* (*šar tamḫāri*), all three of which Westenholz 1997 edits in a single critical-edition volume.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 36–49 (the *Birth Legend* as a *narû*-genre composition); Westenholz 1997, the volume as a whole (the *Legends of the Kings of Akkade* dossier comprising the *Birth Legend of Sargon*, the *King of Battle*, and the *Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn* as the principal Akkadian *narû* compositions). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the *narû* genre in the late tradition; the Sargon and Narām-Sîn cycles). Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the *narû* genre in the Akkadian narrative corpus).]

=== The autobiographical opening: "Sargon, mighty king, king of Akkad, am I"

The legend opens with the iconic first-person self-presentation that gives the composition its character. The opening lines, in the standard translation, are rendered: "Sargon, mighty king, king of Akkad, am I." The first-person voice is sustained throughout the surviving text; the composition is formally the king's own address, projected back from the legendary past into the voice of the founder-king. The autobiographical voice is one of the principal literary distinctives of the composition and one of the *narû* genre's defining features.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–39 (the opening lines in transliteration and translation: "Sargon, mighty king, king of Akkad, am I"; the first-person self-presentation). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the autobiographical opening in English translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the *narû* genre's first-person framing).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 38-39 (opening lines)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 912
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 95-98

The opening continues with Sargon's account of his parentage. His mother is described as an *enitu* — a high-priestess of the type whose office in Mesopotamian temple administration involved ritual celibacy; the term is rendered in Westenholz 1997 and Foster 2005 as "high priestess" or "changeling-priestess". His father, in the legend's own phrasing, he did not know: the legend renders the line "my father I knew not". The mother's brothers — Sargon's maternal uncles — are said to love the hills; the phrasing has been variously interpreted, but Westenholz 1997 reads it as locating the maternal family among the highland populations of upper Mesopotamia.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–39 (the mother as *enitu* / high-priestess; the unknown father; the brothers of the father / maternal kin in the hills). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the mother and father in the autobiographical opening). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the priestess-mother trope in the Sargon tradition). Black & Green 1992, s.v. Sargon (the parentage in the legend).]

The legend names Sargon's city as Azupirānu — translated as "Saffron City" or "city of saffron" — and locates it on the banks of the Euphrates. The city is not securely identified with any historically attested site and may be a literary creation of the legend rather than a real geographical referent; Westenholz 1997's commentary treats Azupirānu as a name preserved only in the *Birth Legend* and notes the uncertainty of its location. The Euphrates setting, by contrast, anchors the composition in the Mesopotamian river-valley geography that the rest of the narrative requires.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–40 (Azupirānu as the city named in the legend; its location on the Euphrates; the uncertainty of historical identification). Foster 2005, p. 912 (Azupirānu in the translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the city and the Euphrates setting in the legend).]

The legend specifies that the mother conceived Sargon "in secret" — the phrasing is preserved in the surviving text — and that she bore him in concealment. The element of clandestine conception is the legend's literary framing of an *enitu*'s ritually-irregular maternity: a priestess of the type Sargon's mother is said to be was, in the relevant Mesopotamian temple practice, expected to bear no children. The "in secret" framing thus functions as the legend's narrative motivation for the exposure-on-the-river that follows.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–40 (the conception "in secret"; the *enitu*'s ritual context; the narrative motivation for the exposure). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the secret conception in the translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the *enitu* priestess and the cultural context of the secret birth).]

=== The basket of reeds, the bitumen, and the river

The mother, the legend continues, placed the infant Sargon in a basket of rushes and sealed its lid with bitumen — the natural petroleum-tar of southern Mesopotamia, used in the ancient Near East as a waterproof sealant for reed-bundled craft. The surviving lines render the moment in the king's own voice: she set him in a basket of rushes, sealed the lid with bitumen, and cast him upon the river. The basket-and-bitumen combination is the legend's distinctive material image — an item of everyday Mesopotamian river-craft repurposed as the vessel of a foundling-king.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–40 (the basket of rushes; the bitumen sealing; the casting upon the river — the surviving lines in transliteration and translation). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the basket-and-bitumen scene in the English translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the basket-and-river motif in the Sargon legend). Black & Green 1992, s.v. Sargon (the basket-and-river image as the legend's signature motif).]

The river upon which the infant is cast is the Euphrates — the same river on whose banks the legend has located Azupirānu. The choice of the Euphrates, rather than the Tigris or a more general "river", places the exposure within the alluvial heartland of southern Mesopotamia: the river that runs through the Akkadian-speaking south, past the cities of Sippar, Babylon, Uruk, and ultimately to the southern marshes. The Euphrates setting establishes the narrative's geographical horizon as the Akkadian alluvium.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–40 (the Euphrates as the river of the exposure; the alluvial setting). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the Euphrates in the translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the Euphrates setting in the legend).]

The legend states that the river did not let the basket sink — the phrasing preserves the image of a buoyant rescue rather than a drowning. The river functions in the narrative as a passive cosmic agent: it bears the infant downstream, neither drowning him nor delivering him by its own initiative to safety, but conveying him along its current until he is drawn out by the human agent who is the legend's next character. The river's quasi-protective role is one of the legend's structural details that has invited comparative attention to other exposed-infant narratives in the wider Eurasian tradition.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 38–40 (the river bearing the basket; the buoyant-rescue framing; the river as passive cosmic agent). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the river-and-basket sequence). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the river's narrative role).]

=== Aqqi the water-drawer and the gardener apprenticeship

The legend introduces the figure of Aqqi (also transliterated Akki) — described as a *dāli*, a "drawer of water", a labourer of the irrigation-and-water-supply class. Aqqi, drawing water from the river in the course of his work, raises his bucket and finds the basket containing the infant Sargon. Aqqi lifts the basket from the river — the legend uses the verbal-aspect of the drawing-up that is etymologically reminiscent of his trade — and takes the child as his own.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 40–42 (Aqqi the water-drawer; the *dāli* trade; the drawing-up of the basket from the river; the adoption of the infant). Foster 2005, p. 912 (Aqqi the water-drawer in the translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the Aqqi figure in the Sargon tradition). Black & Green 1992, s.v. Sargon (the water-drawer's role in the legend).]

Aqqi raises Sargon as his own son and appoints him to the gardener's trade — *nukaribbu*, the orchard-and-vegetable cultivator whose work in Mesopotamian agriculture sits within the irrigated landscape that Aqqi's own water-drawing trade supplies. The gardener apprenticeship is the legend's specific framing of Sargon's pre-royal humble origin: not a herdsman (the Cyrus and Romulus parallels' typical alternative), not a warrior, but a tender of irrigated crops within the alluvial agricultural system of southern Mesopotamia.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 40–42 (Aqqi raising Sargon as his son; the gardener — *nukaribbu* — appointment; the irrigated-agriculture context). Foster 2005, p. 912 (the gardener apprenticeship in the translation). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the gardener trade as the legend's distinctive humble-origin specification).]

The Aqqi-and-Sargon relation is one of the legend's most carefully constructed structural devices: a low-status urban labourer of the irrigation system finds, rescues, and apprentices the future founder-king of the Mesopotamian world-empire. The legend uses the specific occupational category — *dāli* the water-drawer, *nukaribbu* the gardener — to anchor the autobiography in the recognisable working life of an Akkadian-speaking urban-and-alluvial society, before the narrative's reorientation toward divine election and royal accession.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 40–42 (the *dāli* and *nukaribbu* occupational specifications; the social-stratum framing; the structural function in the narrative). === Ištar's love and the rise to kingship

The legend's pivotal turn from humble origin to royal accession is mediated by the goddess Ištar — the queen of heaven, goddess of love and war and the planet Venus, the deity whose patronage of kings is one of the most-attested divine-royal relations of the Akkadian tradition. The surviving lines render the moment in the king's own voice: "Ištar granted me her love" — the goddess's love (the specific Akkadian noun is *râmu*, the relational love that the goddess directs toward favoured kings) — and from that grant of love the kingship follows.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 42–44 (Ištar's grant of love; the goddess's patronage of Sargon; the *râmu* lexical context; the divine-election framing). Foster 2005, p. 913 (Ištar's love in the translation: "Ishtar granted me her love"). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (Ištar's patronage of Sargon in the legendary tradition). Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ištar (Ištar as patroness of kings; her role in the Sargon legend).]

The legend states that Sargon then exercised kingship — the specific verbal phrase preserved in the surviving lines indicates the formal taking up of the royal office. The duration of the kingship is given as a specific number of years, but the precise figure is partly damaged in the surviving witnesses; Foster 2005 (p. 913) and Westenholz 1997 (pp. 42–44) render the figure with the lacuna indicated. The legend's framing of the accession as the direct consequence of Ištar's love — not as the outcome of inheritance, election, or conquest — is the composition's load-bearing theological claim: the founder-king holds his office because the goddess of love-and-war chose him.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 42–44 (the exercise of kingship; the partly-damaged years-of-reign figure; the theological framing of the accession as divine grant). Foster 2005, p. 913 (the exercise of kingship in the translation; the damaged years-figure). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the divine-election theology in the Sargon legend). Bottéro 2001 (Ištar's love and the royal-legitimation function in Akkadian royal literature).]

The accession-by-divine-love framing places the *Birth Legend* within the wider Akkadian royal-legitimation theology in which the king holds his office as the gift of a patron deity rather than as a hereditary entitlement. The legend's distinctive feature is that the accession follows not the standard royal-genealogy line (father-king, son-king) but the inverse: from unknown father and ritually-irregular mother, through the river, the water-drawer, and the gardener trade, to Ištar's love and the kingship. The composition thus articulates a self-made-king typology in which divine election operates independently of dynastic descent.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 42–44 (the self-made-king typology; the inverse-genealogy framing; the divine election independent of descent); pp. 36–49 (the legend's place in the Akkadian royal-legitimation tradition). === The conquest catalogue and the closing autobiographical address

The legend continues from the accession into a catalogue of the lands Sargon conquered as king. The surviving lines preserve a partial conquest catalogue — specific cities and territories named in the conquering verbal-aspect that Akkadian royal inscriptions characteristically use — but the catalogue is damaged across the witnesses and is not preserved in full. The conquest catalogue is the legend's bridge from the autobiographical-rise narrative to the formal royal-inscription apparatus that the *narû* genre adopts as its closing framing.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 44–48 (the partial conquest catalogue; the surviving lands named in the legend; the damage across the witnesses). === The historical Sargon and the legendary tradition

The historical Sargon of Akkad — Šarru-kīn, "the king is true" — reigned approximately 2334–2279 BCE and founded the Akkadian Empire, the first Mesopotamian polity to assemble a territorial empire across southern, central, and upper Mesopotamia and into the surrounding regions. His historical reign is attested in Old Akkadian royal inscriptions, in the year-name and date-formula traditions, and in the contemporary administrative record; the historical figure stands at the head of the dynasty that gave Akkadian its name as a language and as a literary tradition.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, introduction (the historical Sargon ca. 2334–2279 BCE; the founding of the Akkadian Empire; the Old Akkadian inscriptions attesting the historical reign). Black & Green 1992, s.v. Sargon, Akkad (the historical Sargon and the dynasty; the city of Akkad / Agade). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the historical Sargon as the foundation of the legendary tradition). Bottéro 2001 (the Akkadian dynasty and its literary legacy).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Sargon, Akkad

// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on Akkadian dynasty

The legendary tradition about Sargon — of which the *Birth Legend* is one composition — develops over the centuries following his historical reign, in a process whose end-point is the first-millennium scribal corpus from which K.3401 and the Neo-Babylonian duplicates derive. Westenholz 1997 establishes that the Akkadian-language legendary corpus about Sargon is a layered tradition: contemporary Old Akkadian inscriptions, Old Babylonian literary compositions (some preserving narratives about Sargon's reign), and the first-millennium *narû* compositions that include the *Birth Legend*, the *King of Battle*, and the *Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn* (the cycle involving Sargon's grandson). The first-millennium *Birth Legend* sits at the late end of this tradition — roughly a millennium and a half after the historical reign — and represents a fully literary elaboration rather than a contemporary record.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 36–49 (the layered Sargon legendary tradition; the chronological depth from Old Akkadian inscriptions to first-millennium *narû* compositions; the *Birth Legend*'s position at the late end). === The narû genre and the legend's literary function

The *Birth Legend of Sargon* is one of the principal Akkadian *narû* compositions. The *narû* genre is the Akkadian literary form in which a king addresses a future audience from the inscribed monument that bears his words; the composition adopts the first-person voice, dynastic self-presentation, and didactic address that characterise the Mesopotamian royal-monumental tradition, but transposes them from the contemporary stone monument to the much later literary tablet. The *Birth Legend* uses the *narû* framing for an autobiographical narrative of the founder-king's origins; the *Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn* (chapter 24) uses the same framing for a didactic narrative of the founder-king's grandson's failure and repentance. The two compositions together — both edited by Westenholz 1997 in the same critical-edition volume — constitute the principal Akkadian *narû* dossier.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 36–49 (the *Birth Legend* as a *narû* composition); the *Legends of the Kings of Akkade* volume as a whole (the *Birth Legend*, the *King of Battle*, and the *Cuthaean Legend* as the principal Akkadian *narû* dossier). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the *narû* genre and its compositions). Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the *narû* genre in the Akkadian narrative corpus). Bottéro 2001 (the *narû* tradition in Akkadian literature).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 36-49 (narû genre)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 95-98
// EVIDENCE: source_id=noegel-2005 ; loc=pp. 233-245
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on narû tradition

The literary function of the *Birth Legend* operates at three levels. At the royal-legitimation level, the composition asserts the founder-king's divine election independent of dynastic descent, articulating the theological framework within which a non-hereditary or irregular succession is legitimised; the *Birth Legend* is the paradigmatic Akkadian statement of the divinely-elected-from-obscurity king. At the literary level, the composition is a self-contained pseudo-autobiography with an iconic opening, a tightly-constructed narrative of exposure-rescue-apprenticeship-election, and the formal *narû* framing. At the didactic level, the composition frames the founder-king as an exemplar of the divine-favour-grants-kingship pattern, available for emulation or admiration by the implied future audience.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 36–49 (the *Birth Legend*'s royal-legitimation, literary, and didactic functions). Foster 2007, pp. 95–98 (the divine-favour-from-obscurity pattern in the Sargon legend). Bottéro 2001 (the *narû* genre's didactic function). Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the literary structure of the *narû* compositions).]

=== The legend's place in the corpus

The *Birth Legend of Sargon* occupies a distinctive position in the Akkadian narrative corpus. As one of the principal *narû* compositions, it sits within the Akkadian royal-legitimation tradition alongside the *Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn* (chapter 24) and the *King of Battle* (also edited by Westenholz 1997 in the same volume); as a first-person autobiography, it stands among the few Akkadian compositions to adopt sustained first-person royal voice; as the legend of the founder-king of the Akkadian dynasty, it carries unusual symbolic weight within the tradition that gave Akkadian its name.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 36–49 (the *Birth Legend*'s position in the Akkadian narrative corpus); the volume as a whole (the *narû* dossier as edited by Westenholz).
