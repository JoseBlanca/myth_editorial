== The Descent of Ishtar

A short Standard Babylonian poem of one hundred thirty-eight lines, preserved on a Neo-Assyrian tablet from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh and a parallel manuscript from the chief singer's library at Aššur, narrates Ištar's descent into the underworld. The goddess of love and war passes through seven gates, surrendering at each one of the ornaments that mark her divine office; she is held captive below; she is retrieved by a being Ea fashions for the purpose; she names her own consort, Tammuz, as the substitute who will take her place in the Land of No Return; and the poem closes with what reads as a ritual instruction for the seasonal Tammuz wailing rites.footnote:[Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, pp. 498–505 (the principal current English translation, with introduction); Dalley 2008, *Myths from Mesopotamia*, pp. 154–162 (a second triangulation translation).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 498-505
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 154-162

The principal Akkadian witness is the Nineveh tablet K.162 in the British Museum (Aššurbanipal's seventh-century BCE royal library), with parallel Neo-Assyrian fragments. An additional manuscript was recovered from the library of the chief singer (the *nargallu*) at Aššur, on an early Neo-Assyrian tablet. The witnesses agree on the seven-gate structure and the substitution ending; minor textual differences appear principally in the closing ritual lines.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 498 (Nineveh witness K.162; Aššur duplicate from the chief singer's library); Dalley 2008, p. 154 (manuscript history; Neo-Assyrian date).]

=== The underworld

The composition opens by naming the underworld: *Kurnugê* — "the Land of No Return", "the dark house", the dwelling of Erkalla — the Mesopotamian underworld where the dead reside. The text describes the conditions in unusually direct images: those who enter are clothed in feathers like birds, dust is their food, clay their bread; they see no light and dwell in darkness.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 499 (opening description of *Kurnugê*; the dust-as-food and feathered-clothing imagery); Dalley 2008, pp. 154–155 (the underworld description); Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. underworld.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 499
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 154-155
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. underworld

The underworld is governed by Ereškigal, the queen of the netherworld and Ištar's elder sister. The gates are hers, the laws are hers, the dead are her subjects. The composition's load-bearing theological pairing is the relationship between the two sisters — Ištar of the heavens, Ereškigal of the underworld — whose offices divide the cosmos between them.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 499 (Ereškigal as queen of *Kurnugê* and Ishtar's elder sister); Dalley 2008, pp. 154–155 (the sisterly relationship and divided cosmic offices); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ereškigal.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 499
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 154-155
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ereškigal

Ereškigal's vizier is Namtar — a deity of fate and disease, herald and executor of his queen's commands. Namtar will appear at the climactic moment of the narrative. The vizier's role is structural: he carries decrees between Ereškigal and the divine world above.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 502 (Namtar as Ereškigal's vizier); Dalley 2008, pp. 158–159 (Namtar's role at the retrieval scene); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Namtar.]

=== Ištar's descent and threat at the gate

Ištar — queen of heaven, goddess of love and war — sets her mind to the descent. The text gives no programmatic reason; it simply opens with her resolve to enter the underworld and her arrival at its outer gate.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 499 (the absent motivation; Ishtar's resolve and arrival); Dalley 2008, pp. 154–155 (the opening movement); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on the underworld theology.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 499
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 154-155
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on underworld theology

Standing before the gate, Ištar issues a threat to its gatekeeper: if he does not open it, she will smash it down, shatter the bolt, break the doorpost, and raise up the dead so that they outnumber the living and devour them. The threat is rhetorically explicit; the gatekeeper is given no option but to admit her.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 499–500 (Ishtar's threat to the gatekeeper; the dead-outnumber-the-living motif); Dalley 2008, p. 155 (the threat and its rhetorical pattern).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 499-500 (Ishtar's threat)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 155

The gatekeeper carries the threat to Ereškigal. Ereškigal turns pale at the news and reflects on what has come; she instructs the gatekeeper to admit Ištar according to "the ancient ordinances" — the laws under which any visitor passes through the seven gates of the underworld and is stripped progressively at each.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 500 (Ereškigal's response and the "ancient ordinances"); Dalley 2008, p. 156 (the queen's instruction to apply the laws of the underworld).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 500
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 156

=== Seven gates, progressive stripping

The structural centrepiece of the composition is the descent through the seven gates. At each gate, the gatekeeper takes from Ištar one item of the clothing or jewelry that marks her divine office, in a fixed sequence preserved through all the witnesses. At the first gate her great crown is taken; at the second, the rings of her ears; at the third, the chains of her neck; at the fourth, the ornaments of her breast; at the fifth, the girdle of birthstones at her waist; at the sixth, the bangles of her hands and feet; at the seventh, the proud garment of her body. By the time Ištar reaches Ereškigal, she stands naked.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 500–501 (the seven-gate stripping sequence; one item per gate); Dalley 2008, pp. 156–157 (the gate-by-gate enumeration); Black & Green 1992, s.v. underworld (on the seven-gates motif).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 500-501 (seven-gate stripping)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 156-157
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. underworld

At each gate Ištar protests, and at each gate the gatekeeper answers with the same formulaic refrain — "Enter, my lady; thus are the rites of the Mistress of Earth", or the close equivalent in Foster's translation. The refrain is repeated through all seven gates; its rhetorical insistence is a defining feature of the poem's structure.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 500–501 (the gatekeeper's formulaic refrain); Dalley 2008, pp. 156–157 (the rhetorical refrain pattern).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 500-501 (gatekeeper's refrain)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 156-157

[INFERENCE: The seven-gate stripping has been read as both a ritual sequence (the goddess's progressive denudation as she crosses the boundaries between worlds) and as a structural mythologem of progressive loss-of-status (a queen of heaven entering the underworld must surrender, gate by gate, the ornaments that mark her divine authority). Both readings are consistent with the text. The ritual reading aligns with the closing Tammuz instruction; the loss-of-status reading aligns with the formulaic refrain that asserts the underworld's "rites" against Ishtar's protest. | basis: the formulaic refrain explicitly invokes "the rites of the Mistress of Earth" at each gate (Foster 2005, pp. 500–501); the closing ritual instruction (Foster 2005, p. 504) embeds the narrative in cult; the parallel Sumerian *Inanna's Descent* (ETCSL 1.4.1) similarly stages the descent as a ritual passage. The structural reading is supported by the ornaments named — crown, earrings, neck-chains, breast-ornaments, girdle, bangles, garment — which together constitute the iconographic markers of Ishtar's divine office. | risk: the readings are interpretive frameworks imposed by modern editors; the surviving Akkadian text does not preserve a programmatic statement about why the stripping is sevenfold or what the items signify individually. The seven-gate motif is widely attested in Mesopotamian underworld literature, but its specific symbolic interpretation is not textually self-explained.]

=== Captivity and the plague on earth

When Ištar reaches Ereškigal, naked, the queen of *Kurnugê* turns sixty diseases — through Namtar — upon Ištar's body. Ištar is held captive in the depth of the underworld, struck by sixty afflictions.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 501 (Ereškigal's command; the sixty diseases through Namtar); Dalley 2008, p. 157 (the captivity and afflictions).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 501
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 157

While Ištar is held below, fertility ceases above. The bull no longer mounts the cow; the donkey no longer mounts the jenny; the man no longer impregnates the woman; the man sleeps in his chamber, the woman in her own. The cessation of generation on earth is the cosmic consequence of Ištar's captivity: with the goddess of love removed from the world above, the world above ceases to reproduce.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 501–502 (cessation of fertility on earth during Ishtar's captivity); Dalley 2008, pp. 157–158 (the fertility-failure motif).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 501-502
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 157-158

The vizier of the great gods, Papsukkal, observes the cessation and reports to the divine assembly. His face darkens, he tears his garments and lets his hair grow tangled, and he carries the news to Ea — god of wisdom, the freshwater *Apsû*, and incantation, patron of craft and cunning, father of Marduk — that something must be done. Papsukkal's distress is the trigger of the next narrative move.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 502 (Papsukkal's report to Ea); Dalley 2008, p. 158 (Papsukkal as vizier of the great gods); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Papsukkal.]

=== Ea creates Aṣušu-namir

Ea devises the response. He creates from his own wisdom a being called Aṣušu-namir — "his appearance is bright", "the bright-faced one" — an asexual figure not bound by the rules that govern gods or humans. The creation is the kind of expedient Ea regularly authors in the late-Babylonian dossier: a contrived being who can act where no ordinary divine emissary can succeed.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 502 (Ea's creation of Aṣušu-namir; the asexual being); Dalley 2008, p. 158 (the name etymology and the creation-as-stratagem); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ea (Ea as inventor of expedient beings).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 502
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 158
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ea

Ea instructs Aṣušu-namir to descend to *Kurnugê*, to win Ereškigal's favour, and then — through a request linked to the water of life that Ereškigal keeps in her own keeping — to extract from her a sworn promise that, once named, binds her to release Ištar. (The technical Akkadian term for the vessel or container of the water of life appears in Foster's translation in transliteration; this chapter follows Foster's narrative-level summary rather than asserting a specific lemma at the lexical level.) The deception works on the underworld's rule that what a sovereign formally promises cannot be retracted: when Aṣušu-namir extracts a sworn promise from Ereškigal and then names the water of life as the object of the promise, Ereškigal is bound.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 503 (Ea's instructions to Aṣušu-namir; the water-of-life stratagem and the binding-promise mechanism); Dalley 2008, p. 159 (the deception and the binding promise).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 503
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 159

[INFERENCE: The Aṣušu-namir stratagem turns on the Akkadian theology of binding speech — once a deity's formal word is given, it has objective force and cannot be retracted, even by the speaker. Ereškigal's curse on Aṣušu-namir, when it follows, expresses her recognition that she has been outmanoeuvred by precisely this principle: she cannot rescind her promise, and so she damns the messenger instead. | basis: the binding-speech theology is widely attested in the Akkadian narrative tradition (Black & Green 1992, s.v. fate, *šīmtu*); the ritual-decree literature regularly turns on irrevocable speech-acts; the parallel Sumerian *Inanna's Descent* uses a similar promise-and-curse structure in its retrieval scene. The text does not name "binding speech" as the principle, but the narrative logic of Ereškigal's curse on Aṣušu-namir presupposes it. | risk: the structural reading is the chapter's editorial framing; the Akkadian text does not preserve a programmatic statement about why the promise binds Ereškigal. The reading is supported by parallel attestations of the theology in the Akkadian tradition but is not textually self-explained in *The Descent of Ishtar* itself.]

=== Ereškigal's curse and the sprinkling of the water of life

Ereškigal recognises the deception. She utters a powerful curse upon Aṣušu-namir: the messenger's food shall be the city's leftovers, the messenger's drink shall be the city's drainwater, the messenger shall dwell at the threshold among the city's outcasts. The curse is a fate of perpetual marginality, the lot of those who exist at the edges of social order. But the curse, fierce as it is, does not undo the promise: Ereškigal must release Ištar.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 503–504 (Ereškigal's curse upon Aṣušu-namir; the marginal-fate imagery); Dalley 2008, pp. 159–160 (the curse-content and its irrevocability).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 503-504 (Ereškigal's curse)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 159-160

Ereškigal commands Namtar to bring the water of life and sprinkle it upon Ištar. Namtar performs the sprinkling; Ištar is revived. She is then released to ascend.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 504 (Namtar sprinkles the water of life; Ishtar revived); Dalley 2008, p. 160 (the revival and the order to ascend).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 504
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 160

=== The ascent: progressive restoration

Ištar's ascent reverses the descent, gate by gate. At each gate, the gatekeeper returns to her one item of the clothing and jewelry that was stripped from her on the way down, in reverse order: the proud garment first, then the bangles, the girdle of birthstones, the breast-ornaments, the neck-chains, the earrings, and finally the great crown. By the seventh gate she stands once again clothed in the full ornament of her divine office. (The ascent restores in reverse the items listed at the descent; the chapter draws this reversal from the structural symmetry of Foster's translation rather than from a verbatim quotation of the ascent passage.)footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 504 (the ascent through the seven gates; restoration in reverse sequence); Dalley 2008, pp. 160–161 (the gate-by-gate restoration).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 504 (the ascent and restoration)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 160-161

But Namtar's escort comes with a condition: a substitute must be brought in Ištar's place. The underworld must not be left empty; the laws of *Kurnugê* require that when one passes out of it, another must enter in their stead.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 504 (the substitution requirement; the laws of *Kurnugê*); Dalley 2008, p. 161 (the substitute-condition for the ascent).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 504 (substitution requirement)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 161

=== Tammuz substituted

The substitute Ištar names is Tammuz — the shepherd-god, her own consort. The text presents Tammuz as having neglected his lover during her descent: instead of mourning her in the standard postures of grief, he has dressed in fine clothes and played the pipe in the company of singers. When Ištar emerges and sees him so dressed, she designates him as the substitute, and Namtar's demons seize him.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 504–505 (Tammuz's neglect during Ishtar's captivity; his designation as substitute; Namtar's demons take him); Dalley 2008, pp. 161–162 (the substitution scene); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tammuz.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 504-505 (Tammuz substituted)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 161-162
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tammuz

Tammuz's sister Belili — wearing her own ornaments, lapis lazuli on her arm — is described as casting them aside in grief at the news. Belili's mourning frames Tammuz's removal in the wider household of the dying-god cult; the figure of the bereaved sister is a load-bearing element of the closing ritual instruction.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 505 (Belili tearing off her ornaments at the news of Tammuz's removal); Dalley 2008, p. 162 (Belili's grief).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 505
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=p. 162

=== Closing ritual: the Tammuz festival

The composition closes with what reads as a ritual instruction. The closing lines move from narrative into prescription: on a specific day each year — the day of Tammuz's annual descent — the cult community is to lament the dying shepherd-god, the lapis-lazuli pipe is to be sounded for him, and Belili is to take part in the wailing. The closing prescription is partly damaged in some witnesses, but the etiological hook is clear: the narrative of Ištar's descent and Tammuz's substitution authorises a recurring cult observance.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 505 (closing ritual instruction; the Tammuz wailing rite); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 26–29 (the *Descent of Ishtar* and the Tammuz cult in late-period transmission); Bottéro 2001, on the seasonal Tammuz wailing rites in Babylonian and Assyrian practice.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 505
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 26-29
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on Tammuz wailing rites

[INFERENCE: The closing ritual instruction makes *The Descent of Ishtar* an etiological narrative for a specific cult practice — the seasonal Tammuz wailing rite — rather than a freestanding underworld myth. The composition's preservation in first-millennium Akkadian libraries is consistent with this ritual function: the text was kept and recited because it grounded a recurring observance, not because it was admired purely as literature. | basis: the closing prescription is part of the surviving text (Foster 2005, p. 505); Foster 2007 (pp. 26–29) connects the composition to the late-period Tammuz cult; the Tammuz wailing rite is independently attested across the Akkadian first-millennium dossier (Bottéro 2001, passim). The ritual function of the composition is supported textually by the closing prescription and contextually by the parallel cult attestations. | risk: the ritual-aetiology reading places greater weight on the closing prescription than on the narrative body, and modern readers may receive the composition primarily as a literary work; the etiological function is not the only valid reading, but it is supported by the text's own framing and by the cult-context evidence.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The Descent of Ishtar* is one of the most-studied compositions in the Akkadian narrative tradition. It is widely cited in introductions to Mesopotamian religion, in studies of the dying-god typology, and in cross-cultural comparative work on underworld and seasonal-vegetation myths. Its compactness — 138 lines — and its tightly-structured narrative arc make it the illustrative type for the Akkadian underworld-descent genre.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 498 (the composition's anthological prominence); Bottéro 2001, on the *Descent of Ishtar* as a representative underworld myth; Foster 2007, pp. 26–29.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 498
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 26-29

Within the Akkadian corpus, the closest internal parallel is *Nergal and Ereškigal* (chapter 14), which shares the underworld setting and the figure of Ereškigal but tells a substantively different story — Nergal's reluctant marriage to the queen of *Kurnugê* — rather than a descent-and-substitution narrative. The two compositions form a paired underworld dossier: *The Descent of Ishtar* gives the goddess's descent and seasonal-substitution structure; *Nergal and Ereškigal* gives the underworld's complementary marital and political structure.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 498 and pp. 506 ff. (*Descent of Ishtar* and *Nergal and Ereškigal* as paired underworld compositions); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ereškigal.]
