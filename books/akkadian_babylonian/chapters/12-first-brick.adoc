== The First Brick

A short late-Babylonian Akkadian incantation, preserved on multiple cuneiform tablets and edited in modern critical form by W. G. Lambert, frames the act of temple renovation as the human echo of an originating divine labour-force. The text — known to modern scholarship by its editorial title *The First Brick* — has Ea fashion the freshwater deep and then create from clay seventeen named craft-pantheon deities, plus a king and mankind, who together perform the work of rebuilding existing temples. Marduk, the dominant figure of the late-Babylonian creation dossier, does not appear in it. *The First Brick* belongs to the dossier's Ea-centred minority register, and it gives the temple-renovation rite a cosmogonic genealogy.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 376–383 (text, translation, and commentary; chapter 33 in the volume's "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" section, Part III).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383

The composition has no preserved ancient title. *The First Brick* is editorial — taken from the surviving content's foregrounding of the *libittu maḫrītu* ("first/former brick") in the temple-renovation ritual. Lambert's 2013 edition establishes both the title and the composition's place in the late-Babylonian creation dossier; Michael Streck's 2014 review of Lambert in the *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* independently confirms the title and the Part-III placement.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 376 (modern editorial title; chapter 33 placement among the "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" of Part III); Streck 2014, "Review of W. G. Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths*", *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 77 (2014), 371–372 (independent confirmation of the title and Part-III placement; named-but-flagged — not currently in sources.yaml).]

=== Renovation, not foundation

The composition's ritual setting needs to be specified carefully, because the title invites the wrong picture. The first brick of *The First Brick* is not the first brick of a wholly-new building. Babylonian and Assyrian temple-renovation rites required the recovery of the *libittu maḫrītu* — literally "the first/former brick" — from the structure of the existing temple before its rebuilding. The recovered brick was preserved through the rebuilding process and reset into the new structure, where it served as a continuity-marker between the old and new temples and ensured that the cult's ritual identity carried across the renovation. Jamie Novotny's study of the libittu maḫrītu rite in the royal inscriptions of Esarhaddon and Assurbanipal documents the practice in operation in seventh-century BCE Assyria.footnote:[Novotny, "Temple Building in Assyria", on the *libittu maḫrītu* rite in the royal inscriptions of Esarhaddon and Assurbanipal (named-but-flagged: J. Novotny, "Temple Building in Assyria: Evidence from Royal Inscriptions", in M. J. Boda and J. Novotny, eds., *From the Foundations to the Crenellations: Essays on Temple Building in the Ancient Near East and Hebrew Bible*, AOAT 366, Münster: Ugarit-Verlag 2010, pp. 109–139); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on the temple-foundation and temple-renovation cult.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on temple-foundation and temple-renovation cult

*The First Brick* gives this rite a cosmogonic genealogy. The practitioners who recover and reset the *libittu maḫrītu* are doing on the human scale what Ea did on the divine scale at the beginning. The composition therefore frames renovation labour as the descendant of an originating divine labour-force.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the cosmogonic-genealogy reading of the renovation rite); Bottéro 2001, on the typological logic of Mesopotamian foundation rites.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on foundation-rite typology

The surviving manuscripts are first-millennium BCE. The temple-renovation ritual context they presuppose is older — the libittu maḫrītu is attested in Assyrian royal-inscription practice from the seventh century BCE, and likely deeper into the Mesopotamian temple-building cult. *The First Brick* sits within the long Mesopotamian tradition of incantation and ritual literature that gives architectural acts a theological grounding.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. brick, foundation rituals (on Mesopotamian foundation-and-renovation ritual practice across periods); Bottéro 2001, on royal piety and the temple-foundation-and-renovation cult.]

=== Ea creates the freshwater deep

The narrative opens with the creation of the freshwater deep. Ea — god of wisdom, the freshwater *Apsû*, and incantation, patron of craft and cunning, father of Marduk — fashions the *Apsû* (the cosmic freshwater deep beneath the earth, his own dwelling) as the first cosmogonic act. Lambert's translation preserves the Sumerian form *Abzû* in the late-Babylonian Akkadian-Sumerian register the manuscripts use, but the underlying cosmic place is the *Apsû* familiar from the wider creation dossier: Ea's dwelling, and the cosmic water-source from which the materials of subsequent creation will be drawn.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea as principal creator deity; the *Apsû* as the cosmogonic starting-point); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ea (Ea as patron of the *Apsû* and of craft); Bottéro 2001, on Ea's role in the Babylonian theology of cosmogony and craft.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ea
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on Ea and craft

This Ea-centred opening is what marks the composition off from the rest of the late-Babylonian creation dossier. *Enūma Eliš*, *Enmešarra's Defeat*, *The Toil of Babylon*, *Uraš and Marduk*, *Damkina's Bond*, and *The Founding of Eridu* are all Marduk-centred. *The First Brick* is not. Marduk is not named in the surviving narrative; the composition belongs to a minority register that the dossier preserves alongside its Marduk-centred majority.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea as principal creator deity; absence of Marduk from the surviving narrative); Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401 (the wider late-Babylonian creation dossier, in which Marduk is the dominant divine actor of the majority of compositions); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian creation dossier.]

=== The clay-creation of the craft-pantheon

After the *Apsû*, Ea fashions a series of named deities from clay. Each is given a specific role in the temple-renovation labour. The clay-creation is the foundational divine act behind the human craft-pantheon: every Mesopotamian artisan whose work maintains the temples has a divine archetype in the cohort Ea now creates.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea's clay-creation of the named deities; the clay-creation as the foundational divine labour-force); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian incantation-cosmogony genre.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on incantation-cosmogony

The first deity created is Kulla — the brick-god, the divine artisan responsible for renovating the *libittu maḫrītu*. Kulla's creation is the cosmogonic centre of the narrative: the brick-god, fashioned from the same clay he will subsequently work, is the divine archetype of the brick-laying artisan whose human counterparts perform the renovation rite.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Kulla as brick-god created by Ea; Kulla's role in renovating the *libittu maḫrītu*); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Kulla.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Kulla

After Kulla, Ea creates Ninildu, Ninsimug, and Ninagal — three further craft-deities tasked with assisting in the construction. They cover the rest of the building trades: Ninildu the divine carpenter, Ninsimug the divine smith, Ninagal the divine metalworker. The trio is named in the surviving lines as a group, each as a participant in the renovation labour.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ninildu, Ninsimug, Ninagal; the construction-craft trio); Black & Green 1992, s.v. craftsmen-deities.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. craftsmen-deities

A further cohort follows — Arazu, Guškinbanda, Ninzadim, Ninkurra, Ašnan, Laḫar, Siris, Ningišzida, Ninšar, Adag, Umunmutamgu, and Umunmutamnag — tasked variously with supplying resources to mankind for the regular offerings the maintained temples require. The composition extends its cosmogonic reach outward from the renovation work itself to the full economy of cult provisioning: the offered substances — grain, beer, wool, vegetables — each have a divine patron-deity whose creation Ea performs alongside that of the construction-craft trio.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the resource-supply cohort of created deities); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian theology of cult provisioning.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on cult provisioning

The last deity Ea creates is Kusu — addressed in the text as "chief priest of the great gods." Kusu's task is to perform the rites of the temple. He is the priestly counterpart to Kulla: where Kulla represents the divine artisan, Kusu represents the divine priest, and together the two cover the full sacerdotal-and-artisanal economy of the renovation ritual.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Kusu as "chief priest of the great gods"; Kusu's role in performing the rites of the temple); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Kusu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Kusu

Alongside the seventeen named craft-pantheon deities, Ea creates a king and mankind. The full institutional and human counterparts of the divine labour-force are thus also part of the cosmogonic act. The king will commission the renovation work; mankind will provide the offerings the maintained temples need; and the divine craftsmen and divine priest will provide the divine archetypes that authorise both. The composition's full cosmogonic scope is not just the divine craft-pantheon but the entire temple-cult economy on which the Babylonian world depended.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea's creation of the king and mankind alongside the craft-pantheon); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian theology of cult-state-mankind integration.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on cult integration

=== The renovation as the divine archetype

The composition closes by tying the cosmogonic narrative back to the ritual present. The deities Ea has created are tasked, collectively, with the temple-renovation work. Their human counterparts — the king who commissions, the artisans who labour, the priests who perform the rites, the populace whose offerings sustain the cult — perform the same work in the ritual now. Each renovation of each Babylonian temple is, in the composition's theology, a recapitulation of the divine labour-force's foundational work; and each *libittu maḫrītu* recovered and reset is a continuity-marker not only between the old and new temple but between the human renovation and the divine creation that authorises it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the closing section: the divine craft-pantheon as the archetype of the human renovation labour); Bottéro 2001, on the typological logic of Mesopotamian renovation rites; Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian incantation-cosmogony theology.]

A note on terminology. The Akkadian common noun *libittu* — "brick" — is the underlying lexical term. The technical phrase used in the composition and in the parallel royal-inscription rites is *libittu maḫrītu* ("first/former brick"). The qualifier *maḫrītu* — from *maḫrû* "first, former, prior" — marks the brick as the original brick of the existing temple, recovered for reuse rather than as a brand-new inaugural brick. The composition's theology depends on this temporal logic: the *libittu maḫrītu* is "first" not because it inaugurates a new building but because it is the original brick of the old building, preserved through its reuse.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (lexical commentary on *libittu maḫrītu*); CAD L, s.v. libittu (the standard Akkadian masonry term); CAD M/I, s.v. maḫrû (the qualifier "first, former, prior").]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The First Brick* is marginal in attestation. Its modest manuscript footprint — a few Late Babylonian tablets and a late Assyrian fragment — places it well below the canonical-recension distribution of *Enūma Eliš* or *Atra-ḫasīs*. Its scholarly significance lies less in liturgical breadth than in its position within the late-Babylonian creation dossier as one of the dossier's Ea-centred minority compositions, alongside the cosmogonic incantation prologues and certain bilingual creation accounts.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (place within the dossier; modest manuscript footprint); Foster 2007, on the late-period Akkadian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

Within that dossier, *The First Brick* contributes the polytheistic / craft-pantheon-creation register: cosmogony staged as the divine creation of a labour-force. It is one of the clearest Akkadian witnesses to the theology in which divine creation is itself a labour of crafts — Ea fashioning artisans from clay rather than (as in *Enūma Eliš*) Marduk splitting a primordial body, or (as in *Atra-ḫasīs*) the lower gods rebelling against forced labour. The cosmogonic breadth of the dossier as a whole is enriched by the inclusion of compositions like *The First Brick* that hold their own ground against the Marduk-centred dominant register.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (craft-pantheon-creation register); Foster 2007, on the diversity of late-Babylonian cosmogonic registers; Bottéro 2001, on the breadth of Babylonian theological thought.]

=== Closing reflection: the diversity of Babylonian creation thought

This is the closing chapter of the cosmogonic cycle of the book. The cycle has moved from *Enūma Eliš* — the dominant Babylonian cosmogony, the *akītu*-festival recitation that frames Marduk's kingship in cosmic-combat terms — through ten further compositions that together register the diversity of Babylonian creation thought beyond the dominant epic.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–401 (the wider Babylonian creation dossier collected in a single volume); Foster 2007, on the diversity of cosmogonic registers in the late-Babylonian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

The dossier, taken as a whole, has shown that Babylonian creation thought is not monolithic. Cosmogony is staged in this corpus as combat (*Enūma Eliš*, chapter 1; *The Slaying of Labbu*, chapter 5; *Enmešarra's Defeat*, chapter 6); as flood-survival and human-creation (*Atra-ḫasīs*, chapter 2); as the failure-narrative of human-divine boundary (*Adapa*, chapter 3); as theogonic genealogy and violent succession (*The Theogony of Dunnu*, chapter 4); as labour-rebellion and divine alienation (*The Toil of Babylon*, chapter 7); as boundary-disputes between gods (*Uraš and Marduk*, chapter 8); as marital bond and cosmic order (*Damkina's Bond*, chapter 9); as city-and-rivers foundation (*The Founding of Eridu*, chapter 10); as etiological incantation against affliction (*The Worm and the Toothache*, chapter 11); and, in this final chapter, as the divine creation of a craft-pantheon for temple renovation.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–401 and the per-chapter critical editions cited throughout; Foster 2007 on the diversity of registers; Bottéro 2001 on the breadth of Babylonian theological thought.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim

[INFERENCE: The breadth of registers preserved in the cosmogonic dossier — combat, flood, theogony, labour-rebellion, boundary-disputes, marital bond, city-foundation, incantation, craft-pantheon-creation — argues against any reading of Babylonian cosmogony as a single epic dressed in different costumes. The compositions are not variants of one another; they are distinct theological registers that the late-Babylonian scribal tradition preserved together because each carried its own load-bearing function (festival recitation, foundation ritual, medical-magical incantation, theogonic education, royal-piety performance, temple-renovation incantation). *Enūma Eliš* is the dominant composition by attestation, liturgical footprint, and length; it is not the dominant composition by theological scope. Compositions like *The First Brick*, with its Ea-centred craft-pantheon-creation register, hold their own ground within the dossier's theological economy — and would be obscured if the dossier were assimilated to a Marduk-centred whole. | basis: the dossier collected in Lambert 2013 (pp. 1–401) preserves the full range of registers; Foster 2007 surveys the late-period dossier and registers the diversity; the per-chapter critical editions and translations cited throughout chapters 1–11 of this book establish each composition's distinctive theological contribution; *The First Brick*'s Ea-centred framing (verified in this chapter's round-2 revision) is itself a witness to the dossier's theological diversity. | risk: the closing-reflection framing is the chapter's editorial reading, written from the vantage of the cosmogonic-cycle's end; the late-Babylonian scribal tradition itself preserves no programmatic statement that the dossier should be read as a register-diverse whole rather than as *Enūma Eliš* and its supplements. The structural reading is supported by the dossier's content but is the modern editorial framing rather than an ancient theological program.]
