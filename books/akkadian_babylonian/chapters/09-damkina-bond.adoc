== Damkina's Bond

Two tablets — a Neo-Assyrian copy held at the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago and catalogued as A 7882, and a Neo-Babylonian copy in the British Museum, BM 27776 — preserve a short Akkadian composition about a divine war and a goddess's intervention. W. G. Lambert's 2013 edition gives the composition the modern editorial title *Damkina's Bond*, after the *il-let-sa* — variously read "bond" or "burden" — that the goddess releases at the narrative's hinge.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 321–325 (text, translation, and commentary) and p. 497 (notes on the text); Oshima 2010, "Damkina shall not bring back her Burden in the future: A new Mythological Text of Marduk, Enlil, and Damkianna" (an independent critical edition published as a Festschrift article in *A Woman of Valor: Jerusalem Ancient Near Eastern Studies in Honor of Joan Goodnick Westenholz*, ed. W. Horowitz, U. Gabbay, and F. Vukosavović, Biblioteca del Próximo Oriente Antiguo 8, Madrid: CSIC, pp. 145–162; identifies the two manuscripts as A 7882 NA and BM 27776 NB).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 + p. 497

The composition has no preserved ancient title; the modern editorial title is taken from the contested term *il-let-sa* in the surviving lines, where Lambert reads "bond" (something displayable, perhaps a clay tablet) and Oshima reads "burden" (a potentially catastrophic plague or natural / celestial disaster). Lambert 2013 pp. 321–325 (with notes at p. 497) is the standard modern critical edition; Oshima 2010 provides an independent edition with the substantive philological disagreement on *il-let-sa*.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 321 (modern title and composition overview); Oshima 2010 (alternative reading and translation as "Damkina shall not bring back her Burden in the future").]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 321

There is no widely-used English-language anthology translation. Neither Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., 2005) nor Dalley's *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., 2008) includes *Damkina's Bond*; Lambert 2013 and Oshima 2010 are the only modern critical editions.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the sole modern critical edition in book form); Foster 2005 and Dalley 2008 (do not contain this composition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=full table of contents
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=full table of contents

The two manuscripts overlap partially. Per Oshima 2010, MS B (BM 27776, Neo-Babylonian) is generally to be preferred over MS A (A 7882, Neo-Assyrian) on philological grounds — the Assyrian copy contains scribal errors. Lambert 2013 collates both manuscripts into a composite text. Substantial portions of the narrative are damaged in both witnesses; the contour of the narrative is recoverable from the composite, but specific actions, divine speech, and the resolution's theological implications are partly lost.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (manuscript condition and composite-text apparatus); Oshima 2010 (the MS B preference and scribal-error discussion).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

[LACUNA: Two manuscripts — A 7882 (Neo-Assyrian, Oriental Institute Chicago) and BM 27776 (Neo-Babylonian, British Museum) — both partly damaged. === Marduk-Enlil rivalry and the secret of Marduk

The composition belongs to the late-Babylonian and first-millennium dossier articulating Marduk's sovereignty over the older Mesopotamian pantheon. Marduk's takeover of cosmic offices is the central theological move of *Enūma Eliš*; *Damkina's Bond* extends that takeover into the specifically political-theological register of subordinating Enlil's establishment at Nippur — the elder pantheon's traditional cult-centre.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the Marduk-Enlil rivalry framing); Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš*'s Marduk-elevation theology, with Marduk receiving cosmic offices in Tablets IV–V); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on Marduk-Enlil rivalry and Babylon-vs-Nippur theological politics.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Marduk-Enlil rivalry

The colophon of the Neo-Babylonian manuscript classes the composition's contents as a secret of Marduk — the Akkadian term used in such colophons is typically *pirištu* — and warns that revealing them to anyone outside the intended audience of ancient scholars was a taboo of Marduk. Future generations are warned not to repeat the transgressions the narrative recounts. The text is thus self-marked, in its surviving paratext, as restricted scholarly material rather than as liturgical or popular reading.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the BM 27776 colophon and its secret-of-Marduk language); Oshima 2010 (the colophon's reading).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (BM 27776 colophon)

The dating of the composition is contested. Lambert 2013 dates it to the Kassite or Isin II dynasty — the mid-second millennium BCE — on the basis that Marduk is already presented as king of the gods (a post-*Enūma Eliš* theological state) but Nabu is still his scribe rather than his son (a pre-late-Babylonian theological state, before Nabu's elevation in the first-millennium pantheon). Oshima 2010 dates the composition to the first quarter of the first millennium — c. 1000–750 BCE — making it later than Lambert's proposal but still squarely within the Marduk-king theological era. Lambert proposes Malgium, an ancient Babylonian city, as the place of origin; Oshima does not press a specific place. The two surviving copies — the Oriental Institute's A 7882 and the British Museum's BM 27776 — entered modern collections through scholarly channels; the tablets themselves circulated through the late-Mesopotamian temple and palace scribal networks.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Kassite / Isin II dating and Malgium origin proposal); Oshima 2010 (the early-first-millennium dating); Foster 2007, on the late-period Akkadian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

=== Marduk arrives at Nippur

The composition opens with Marduk's arrival at Nippur. The surviving lines preserve a flooding or inundation motif: Marduk comes with his army, and the city is overrun. The action is a divine-military invasion of the elder pantheon's cult-centre by the new cosmic king's pantheon — the gods of Babylon advancing on the seat of Enlil and the Eshumesha establishment.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Marduk's arrival at Nippur and the flooding / inundation motif); Oshima 2010 (the same opening sequence in the alternative edition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

In the early stages of the conflict the gods of Eshumesha — Ninurta's temple at Nippur, the constellation of deities associated with Ninurta's cult and the Enlil-pantheon — capture Anunnaki deities. The Enlil side initially gains ground, and Marduk's forces are not yet decisive.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Eshumesha capture of Anunnaki); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Eshumesha (Ninurta's temple at Nippur); RlA s.v. Eshumesha.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (Eshumesha capture)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Eshumesha
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Eshumesha

[INFERENCE: The "Anunnaki" captured by the Eshumesha gods at this point in the narrative are the Marduk-side deities of the Babylon pantheon, not the broader generic "Anunnaki" of standard Mesopotamian theology. The composition's logic — Marduk attacks Nippur, Enlil's gods initially capture some of Marduk's, Damkina intervenes, Marduk reverses the situation and captures the Enlil-side gods — requires that the captured gods at this stage be on Marduk's side. | basis: the narrative shape preserved across the two manuscripts (Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325) requires this reading; the late-Babylonian theological dossier sometimes uses "Anunnaki" in a more restricted sense for a specific divine constellation rather than for all the gods (Black & Green 1992, s.v. Anunnaki). | risk: the Akkadian usage of "Anunnaki" is variable and the surviving lines do not unambiguously identify the captured gods; this is the most plausible reading but rests on the narrative-shape requirement, not on a textually unambiguous identification.]

=== Neretagmil's message to Damkina

A messenger figure now intervenes. Neretagmil — the *sukkal*, vizier or messenger-god, of the god Nāru, the deified river — delivers a message to Damkianna (a late Akkadian variant spelling used in some manuscripts of this composition; the older Sumerian form is Damgalnuna and the standard Akkadian form is Damkina) about the battle. The message prompts Damkina's intervention.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Neretagmil and the message to Damkina); RlA s.v. Damgalnuna and Damkina (the Damkianna / Damkina equivalence).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (Neretagmil's message)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Damgalnuna / Damkina

Damkina is Marduk's mother — the consort of Ea, the wise god of fresh waters, and the mother of Marduk in the Babylonian pantheon. Her position in this narrative is structurally significant: she is on Marduk's side by maternal-genealogical tie, but she also occupies a senior generation in the divine family, and her intervention has the authority of a senior god, not the merely affective authority of a partisan mother.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. Damkina (consort of Ea, mother of Marduk); RlA s.v. Damkina; Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Damkina's structural position in the narrative).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Damkina
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Damkina
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

=== Damkina releases the il-let-sa

Damkina releases an object the surviving Akkadian text refers to as her *il-let-sa*. The act precipitates the conflict's resolution: after the release, Marduk captures the gods of Eshumesha. But the nature of the *il-let-sa* is contested between the two modern editors of the composition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the il-let-sa release in the surviving lines); Oshima 2010 (the il-let-sa term and its alternative reading).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (il-let-sa)

[VARIANT: primary=Lambert 2013's reading "bond" (something displayable, perhaps a clay tablet); on Lambert's reading the il-let-sa release is a positive theological gesture — a binding / pact / token that ends the conflict on Marduk's terms | alt=Oshima 2010's reading "burden" — derived from *e'iltu* (obligation, bond, sin), so etymologically a binding obligation that Damkianna's release sends out as ill-fortune (a plague or natural / celestial disaster); Oshima rejects the alternative derivation from *illatu* ("horde") | chosen: Lambert's "bond" reading, per the brief's locked source (Lambert 2013) and the chapter title *Damkina's Bond* | reason: the chapter follows Lambert 2013 as the locked source-anchor; Oshima's alternative reading is acknowledged in the body and in the comparative-hooks but is not the primary framing. The disagreement is philological — both readings are scholarly proposals on the same Akkadian word, sharing the *e'iltu* / "obligation/bond" lexical landscape but differing in how the released object operates (a positive displayable token in Lambert vs. a catastrophic release of an obligation in Oshima) — and load-bearing for the composition's interpretation, so the alternative is preserved here rather than collapsed silently into the chosen reading.]

[INFERENCE: Whether the *il-let-sa* is read as "bond" (Lambert) or as "burden" (Oshima), the moment functions as the narrative's hinge: Damkina's release of the object determines the conflict's outcome, and Marduk's capture of the Eshumesha gods immediately follows. The two readings give different theological registers — Lambert's is the binding-of-the-cosmos / pact register; Oshima's is the catastrophe-precipitation register — but both place Damkina's intervention as causally decisive. | basis: the narrative shape across the two manuscripts (Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325; Oshima 2010) places the il-let-sa release immediately before the capture of the Eshumesha gods, with the release as the proximate cause of the resolution; both editors agree on the structural placement, even as they disagree on the term's meaning. | risk: the philological disagreement over il-let-sa is unresolved in the secondary literature, and the chapter's framing of the moment as "hinge of the narrative" rests on the structural placement, not on a settled reading of the term itself. Future philological work may settle the il-let-sa question and would then determine which theological register the chapter foregrounds.]

=== Marduk captures the 360 gods of Eshumesha

After Damkina's intervention, Marduk captures the 360 gods of Eshumesha all together. Among them is Enlil — the Mesopotamian high-god, the head of the elder pantheon. The narrative thus articulates Marduk's theological-political sovereignty over the older Mesopotamian divine order: he subordinates not just the elder pantheon's gods individually, but the entire Eshumesha establishment as a unit, including Enlil himself.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Marduk's capture of the 360 Eshumesha gods, with Enlil among them); Oshima 2010 (the same scene); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Enlil (the high-god of the elder Mesopotamian pantheon).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (capture of 360 gods including Enlil)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Enlil

The number 360 is theologically and astronomically loaded. It corresponds to the canonical count of days in the Mesopotamian schematic year — the standard 12 × 30-day year of the astrolabes and of *MUL.APIN* — and, by extension, to the 360° zodiacal scheme of later Babylonian astronomy. The 360 gods of Eshumesha are not merely a round number; they index the schematic-year totality, and Marduk's capture of all of them at once is a totalising theological gesture.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. number symbolism (the 360-day year and the 360-fold divine constellation); Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the 360-god capture as totalising theological gesture); Bottéro 2001, on Mesopotamian numerical-cosmological symbolism.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. number symbolism
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (the 360-god capture)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Mesopotamian numerical-cosmological symbolism

[INFERENCE: *Damkina's Bond* articulates Marduk's theological-political sovereignty over Enlil's establishment at Nippur in a way that is structurally parallel to *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6, Lambert 2013 pp. 281–298) — also a Marduk-subordinates-elder-divine-order narrative ending in capture, judgement, and the redistribution of cosmic offices. The two compositions form a sub-cluster within the late-Babylonian Marduk dossier articulating Marduk's specifically political-theological sovereignty. *Damkina's Bond* differs from *Enmešarra's Defeat* in foregrounding a maternal-feminine intervention (Damkina's il-let-sa release) as causally decisive, where *Enmešarra's Defeat* foregrounds Marduk's direct judgement and the warden-god Nergal's enforcement. | basis: the two compositions sit adjacent in Lambert 2013's Part III dossier (pp. 281–298 *Enmešarra's Defeat*; pp. 321–325 *Damkina's Bond*); both narrate Marduk's subordination of an elder divine order; both end with capture and political-theological redistribution. The structural pairing is articulated in the late-Babylonian theological imagination via the Marduk-elevation logic of *Enūma Eliš* (Lambert 2013 pp. 1–277, esp. Tablets IV–V on the conferring of cosmic offices). | risk: the structural reading is interpretive — the surviving texts do not present themselves programmatically as a paired sub-cluster — but the structural parallel is robust enough that the two compositions are routinely treated together in the secondary literature on the Marduk dossier (Bottéro 2001 on Marduk-Enlil rivalry; Foster 2007 on the late-Babylonian compositional milieu).]

=== Place in the late-Babylonian / first-millennium Marduk dossier

*Damkina's Bond* belongs to the cluster of compositions Lambert 2013 grouped under the heading "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" (Part III, pp. 281–401). The dossier includes *Enūma Eliš* (Chapter 1), *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6), *The Toil of Babylon* (Chapter 7), *Uraš and Marduk* (Chapter 8), *The Slaying of Labbu* (Chapter 5), *The Founding of Eridu* (Chapter 10), *The First Brick* (Chapter 12), *The Theogony of Dunnu* (Chapter 4), and additional shorter compositions and incantation prologues.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401 (full Part III "Further Babylonian Creation Tales"); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian / first-millennium compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on the late-Babylonian milieu

The composition's closest internal parallel is *Enmešarra's Defeat* — a Marduk-subordinates-elder-divine-order narrative with binding, capture, and judgement elements. The two compositions form a Marduk-vs-elder-pantheon sub-cluster within the dossier. An earlier framing of *Damkina's Bond* as paired with *Uraš and Marduk* under a "feminine consortship sub-cluster" rubric depended on a mis-identification of Urash as the female cosmogonic Uraš (corrected at the chapter-8 stage) and is dropped here.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 281–298 (*Enmešarra's Defeat*) and pp. 321–325 (*Damkina's Bond*); the structural pairing follows from the two compositions' shared Marduk-subordinates-elder-pantheon narrative pattern.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-298 and pp. 321-325

The composition is marginal in attestation. Two manuscripts, no liturgical or first-millennium recensional footprint outside the cosmogonic dossier itself, and the Neo-Babylonian colophon's secret-of-Marduk classification together suggest a restricted scholarly transmission rather than a popular liturgical use. There is no evidence that *Damkina's Bond* was ever a widely-recited composition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (limited attestation; secret-of-Marduk colophon); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian temple-scholarly milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

=== Variants and witness

*Damkina's Bond* has two parallel witnesses — A 7882 (Neo-Assyrian) and BM 27776 (Neo-Babylonian) — that overlap partially. Lambert 2013 collates them into a composite text; Oshima 2010 publishes an independent edition. The two manuscripts are not co-equal recensions in the sense of significantly divergent traditions; they are two witnesses to one text with sub-manuscript variation and scribal errors (especially in MS A). The Lambert / Oshima editorial disagreement on the *il-let-sa* term is a philological judgement on a single word's semantic range, not a recension difference.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (two-manuscript transmission; composite text); Oshima 2010 (alternative edition; MS B preference; il-let-sa reading).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Damkina's Bond* is marginal individually but significant within the late-Babylonian and first-millennium Marduk dossier as evidence that the Marduk-Enlil rivalry was articulated narratively as well as theogonically. *Enūma Eliš* establishes Marduk's cosmic kingship through combat with Tiamat and the conferral of cosmic offices in Tablet V; *Damkina's Bond* extends that kingship into the specifically political-theological register of subordinating the Enlil establishment at Nippur — capturing 360 gods of Eshumesha, Enlil included, with Damkina's intervention as the catalyst.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš* Marduk-elevation theology) and pp. 321–325 (*Damkina's Bond* Marduk-vs-Enlil narrative); Bottéro 2001, on Marduk-Enlil rivalry in the Babylonian theological imagination.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277 and pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Marduk-Enlil rivalry

The composition's narrative obscurity outside specialist scholarship reflects both its fragmentary preservation and its restricted-secret transmission profile. It survives because two scribal apparatuses — a Neo-Assyrian one and a Neo-Babylonian one — preserved it as material for the Marduk-theological dossier, with the Neo-Babylonian colophon explicitly classing the text as a secret of Marduk. The dossier's editorial logic is the reason *Damkina's Bond* exists at all: priestly scholars kept the secret-tablet alongside the more public Marduk theogony, and two copyists, in two centuries, transmitted it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (transmission profile; secret-of-Marduk colophon); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian scribal milieu.]
