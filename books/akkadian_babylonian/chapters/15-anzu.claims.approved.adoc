=== Source overview

*Anzû* is an Akkadian myth that narrates the theft of the Tablet of Destinies (*ṭuppi šīmāti*) by the great storm-bird Anzû, the failure of the gods to find a champion who will challenge him, the eventual mission of the warrior-son of Enlil, and the warrior's defeat of Anzû in single combat. The composition is preserved in two substantively different Akkadian recensions: an Old Babylonian recension (ca. 1700 BCE) on three tablets, with the warrior-hero named Ningirsu, and a Standard Babylonian recension (1st millennium BCE) also on three tablets — Tablet I, Tablet II, Tablet III per the Annus 2001 SAACT 3 critical edition — with the warrior-hero named Ninurta. The SB recension is longer and more elaborated than the OB; the recensional difference is one of literary expansion and theological consolidation, not of tablet count.footnote:[Annus 2001, *The Standard Babylonian Epic of Anzu*, SAACT 3 (Helsinki) — the critical edition of the SB recension, comprising Tablet 1, Tablet 2, and Tablet 3; Vogelzang 1988, *Bin šar dadme: Edition and Analysis of the Akkadian Anzu Poem* (Groningen: Styx) — the critical edition of the OB recension and analysis of the recensional history; Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, pp. 555–578 (accessible English translation of both recensions); Dalley 2008, *Myths from Mesopotamia*, pp. 203–227 (second triangulation translation).]

The Old Babylonian recension survives on Susa manuscripts — Sb 9470 (= OB Anzu II) and Sb 14683 (= OB Anzu III), both held at the Louvre — dated to roughly 1700 BCE. The OB recension is structured in three tablets, with substantial breaks throughout; its hero is Ningirsu, the Lagash war-god, the patron-deity of the southern Mesopotamian city-state of Girsu whose warrior identity was inherited by the wider Akkadian tradition. The OB Akkadian text is the earliest attested form of the composition in Akkadian and shows the original framing of the narrative.footnote:[Vogelzang 1988, entire (OB recension; Susa manuscripts Sb 9470 and Sb 14683; three-tablet structure; Ningirsu hero); Foster 2005, pp. 555–562 (the OB recension's witnesses and structure); Dalley 2008, pp. 203–212 (manuscript history of the OB recension); Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. === Co-equal recensions: a methodological note

The OB and SB recensions of *Anzû* are roughly co-equal in scholarly weight. Neither alone is sufficient: the OB is earlier and shows the original framing; the SB is more elaborated and more widely attested in 1st-millennium libraries. The two recensions tell substantively the same story — same central plot (Anzû's theft of the Tablet of Destinies, the failure of candidate-heroes, the warrior's eventual defeat of Anzû) — but with a critically different hero (Ningirsu in OB; Ninurta in SB) and at a different length (the SB recension is more expansive on the same three-tablet frame as the OB). The chapter therefore presents both recensions in sequence, each attributed to its source, rather than reducing them to a single composite text.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3 (the SB recension as scholarly reference for the 1st-millennium tradition; three-tablet structure); Vogelzang 1988 (the OB recension as scholarly reference for the OB tradition); Foster 2005, pp. 555–578 (the co-equal recensional status; both recensions translated side by side); Foster 2007, pp. 35–39 (recensional history; both recensions as scholarly reference points).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 555-578

The OB→SB recensional substitution of the hero — Ningirsu → Ninurta — corresponds to a wider theological reconfiguration within the Akkadian tradition. Ningirsu (the Lagash war-god) and Ninurta (the Nippur war-god, warrior-son of Enlil) are treated by Black & Green 1992 as originally distinct cult-centre deities, syncretized by the time of Gudea (late third millennium) into a documented identification of Ningirsu as a "local hypostasis" of Ninurta. Whether the two were originally manifestations of a single warrior-god or two distinct deities subsequently merged is a question on which Assyriologists remain divided; Black & Green lean toward two-deities-in-origin, while other scholars read them as a single warrior-figure under local names. The SB recension's substitution of Ninurta for Ningirsu reflects the 1st-millennium cosmopolitan consolidation of the warrior-god identity under the Nippur-centred name — a cult-political shift, not a mere rename — regardless of how the underlying identity question is resolved.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3 (commentary on the Ningirsu/Ninurta substitution); Vogelzang 1988 (on the OB Ningirsu framing); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ningirsu, Ninurta (the two as originally distinct cult-centre deities — Ningirsu at Lagash, Ninurta at Nippur — subsequently syncretized); Foster 2007, pp. 35–39 (the wider 1st-millennium consolidation).]

=== The Old Babylonian recension: opening at the assembly of the gods

The OB recension opens with the gods assembled and the cosmic situation laid out. The narrative establishes the precariousness of cosmic order: the Tablet of Destinies — the cuneiform tablet of cosmic decrees that confers sovereignty on whoever holds it — is held by Enlil, the storm-god and lord of earth. The OB recension introduces Anzû as a great storm-bird who has been brought into the divine household, and elaborates the conditions of his proximity to Enlil.footnote:[Vogelzang 1988, OB Tablet I (the opening assembly and the establishment of the cosmic situation; Enlil's possession of the Tablet of Destinies; Anzû's entry into the divine household); Foster 2005, pp. 555–558 (the OB opening); Dalley 2008, pp. 203–206 (the OB framing).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 555-558
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 203-206

In the OB recension, Anzû's opportunity to seize the Tablet of Destinies comes when Enlil sets it aside to bathe. While Enlil is in his bath, Anzû watches; when Enlil is unguarded, Anzû takes the Tablet and flies away with it to his mountain.footnote:[Vogelzang 1988, OB Tablet I (the bath scene; Anzû seizes the Tablet of Destinies while Enlil bathes); Foster 2005, pp. 557–559 (the OB theft scene); Dalley 2008, pp. 205–207 (the bath-theft motif).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 557-559
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 205-207

With the Tablet of Destinies gone, the cosmic decrees are suspended: the gods' authority is bound up in the Tablet, and its absence in Anzû's possession means that no decree can be made and no cosmic action can be authorised. The OB narrative dramatises the disruption: Enlil, having lost the Tablet, is no longer the source of decrees; the divine assembly must convene to address the crisis.footnote:[Vogelzang 1988, OB Tablet I–II (the disruption of cosmic decree following the theft); Foster 2005, pp. 558–561 (the disruption motif); Dalley 2008, pp. 206–209 (the suspended-decree situation).]

In the OB recension, the divine assembly searches for a champion who will face Anzû and recover the Tablet. The candidate-heroes are sought among the warrior-gods of the pantheon; the OB account of which gods are approached is partly damaged, but the structural pattern — successive candidates considered and the task refused or declined — is preserved.footnote:[Vogelzang 1988, OB Tablet II (the search for a champion; candidate-heroes approached); Foster 2005, pp. 558–561 (the OB candidate-search); Dalley 2008, pp. 207–210 (the assembly's deliberation).]

=== The Standard Babylonian recension: opening with the divine council

The SB recension opens with a divine-council frame that is significantly more elaborate than the OB opening. The gods are assembled; the cosmic situation is laid out; the narrative establishes that Anzû has been brought into proximity to Enlil and the Tablet of Destinies through the prior decision of the gods to entrust him with a position of honour. The SB framing thus situates the theft within a divine-political configuration rather than presenting it as a sudden act of opportunism.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3, SB Tablet I (the divine-council opening; the prior elevation of Anzû); Foster 2005, pp. 562–566 (the SB opening); Dalley 2008, pp. 212–215 (the SB framing).]

In the SB recension, Anzû seizes the Tablet of Destinies from Enlil when Enlil sets it aside to bathe — the same bath-theft motif as the OB recension, preserved across the recensional reformulation. With the Tablet in Anzû's possession, the cosmic decrees are suspended and the divine order is thrown into crisis.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3, SB Tablet I (Anzû's theft during Enlil's bath); Foster 2005, pp. 564–567 (the SB theft scene); Dalley 2008, pp. 215–217 (the SB bath-theft).]

The SB recension elaborates the divine assembly's search for a champion. Anu, the sky-god and formal head of the divine hierarchy, presides over the search; the assembly addresses candidate-heroes in turn. The SB candidate-search names Gerra explicitly as the second refuser; the figure is the divine personification of fire, and his refusal underscores that the warrior-gods of the pantheon and the elemental deities alike are unwilling to face Anzû while the Tablet of Destinies remains in the storm-bird's possession.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3, SB Tablet I (Gerra/Gibil approached second and declines); Foster 2005, pp. 566–568 (Gerra's refusal in the SB sequence); Dalley 2008, pp. 216–218 (the second refuser).]

=== Sharur the sentient mace

A distinctive feature of the SB recension — and, fragmentarily attested in the OB recension per Vogelzang 1988 — is Sharur (*Šaruru*), the sentient war-mace of the warrior-hero. Sharur is not an inert weapon but a speaking instrument that advises the hero, carries messages between the warrior and Ea, and participates in the strategic deliberation before and during the combat. === The first combat-engagement and Anzû's counter-attack

Ninurta — in the SB recension — and Ningirsu — in the OB recension — ascend to Anzû's mountain and engage the storm-bird in battle. The first phase of the combat goes badly for the hero: Anzû uses the Tablet of Destinies' authority to declare the warrior's weapons void. In a striking scene preserved across both recensions, Anzû speaks: the warrior's arrow shall return to its reed-bed, the warrior's bowstring shall return to its sheep, the bow itself shall return to its tree, the bronze shall return to its mountain. The decree-power of the Tablet of Destinies is turned against the warrior, dissolving his weapons before they reach Anzû.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3, SB Tablet II (Anzû's counter-decree; the arrow-to-reed-bed, bowstring-to-sheep, bow-to-tree imagery); Vogelzang 1988 (the OB parallel passage, partly damaged); Foster 2005, pp. 571–574 (the counter-decree scene in both recensions); Dalley 2008, pp. 221–224 (the counter-decree scene); Black & Green 1992, s.v. === Ea's counter-strategy and the second combat

With his weapons dissolved by Anzû's decree, the warrior is in apparent defeat. The SB recension narrates that Sharur, the sentient mace, is dispatched to Ea for counsel; Ea responds with the tactical solution. The warrior is to raise the south wind against Anzû, pluck the storm-bird's pinions while he is grounded by the wind, and strike the killing-blow before Anzû can issue another decree.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3, SB Tablets II–III (Sharur to Ea; Ea's tactical counsel; the south-wind-and-pinions strategy); Foster 2005, pp. 573–576 (Ea's counter-strategy); Dalley 2008, pp. 223–226 (the tactical counsel).]

=== Ritual context and theological function

*Anzû* serves multiple theological functions across its recensions. At the etiological level, the composition articulates the warrior-son of Enlil's establishment as the champion of the gods: Ninurta's defeat of Anzû is the foundational event by which the warrior-god's authority within the divine hierarchy is secured. At the cosmological level, the theft and recovery of the Tablet of Destinies articulates the precariousness of cosmic order and its dependence on heroic action. At the royal-legitimation level, the king's defeat of his enemies is figured in 1st-millennium royal inscriptions on the model of Ninurta's defeat of Anzû.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3, commentary (theogonic function — Ninurta's establishment; cosmological function — the Tablet of Destinies); Foster 2007, pp. 35–39 (royal-legitimation function — the king as Ninurta); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ninurta (Ninurta as warrior-of-the-gods); Bottéro 2001, on divine kingship and royal-legitimation theology.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Anzû* is the principal Akkadian instantiation of the storm-god-defeats-monster combat-myth. With *Enūma Eliš* (chapter 1), where Marduk defeats Tiamat, it forms the paired combat-myth dossier of the Akkadian narrative tradition: *Enūma Eliš* gives the cosmogonic combat (Marduk's slaying of Tiamat creates the structured cosmos); *Anzû* gives the cosmic-restoration combat (Ninurta's slaying of Anzû recovers the cosmic decree-tablet and restores order after its disruption). Both compositions feature the Tablet of Destinies as a load-bearing cosmic artifact, and both close with extended hymnic litanies — Marduk's fifty names in *Enūma Eliš*; a litany of Ninurta's names and epithets in the SB *Anzû* (paralleling the Enūma Eliš structure, with the exact count for SB Anzû not securely established).footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3 (the *Anzû* / *Enūma Eliš* pairing; shared Tablet of Destinies and closing-litany structures); Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths* (the *Enūma Eliš* fifty-names structure); Foster 2007, pp. 35–39 (the paired combat-myth dossier); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tablet of Destinies.]

The composition's geographic and chronological spread is itself notable. The OB Susa witnesses (Sb 9470, Sb 14683 — both Louvre) place the composition at the eastern edge of the Old Babylonian-period scribal world; the SB Nineveh, Sultantepe, Tarbiṣu, and Aššur witnesses place the 1st-millennium recension across the principal Neo-Assyrian library centres. The composition's transmission across a millennium and across a geographic range from southwestern Iran (Susa) to northern Mesopotamia (Aššur) and the Anatolian periphery (Sultantepe) testifies to its standing in the Akkadian scribal canon.footnote:[Annus 2001, SAACT 3 (SB recension witnesses); Vogelzang 1988 (OB recension witnesses; Susa manuscripts Sb 9470 and Sb 14683); Foster 2005, pp. 555–578 (geographic and chronological spread); Foster 2007, pp. 35–39 (Anzû in the late tradition).]
