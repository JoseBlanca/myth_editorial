=== Source overview

The Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgameš* is the principal Akkadian heroic epic, narrating the kingship of Gilgameš at Uruk, his companionship with Enkidu, their joint exploits in the Cedar Forest and against the Bull of Heaven, Enkidu's death, Gilgameš's grief-driven journey in search of immortality across the cosmic ocean to the flood-survivor Uta-napišti, and Gilgameš's return to Uruk with the knowledge that mortality is the boundary inside which a king must build. The composition is preserved on some 80 first-millennium manuscripts from Nineveh, Aššur, Sultantepe, Sippar, Babylon, Uruk, and other Mesopotamian scribal centres, supplemented by Old Babylonian (ca. 1700 BCE) and Middle Babylonian (ca. 1400–1100 BCE) forerunners that attest earlier stages of the Akkadian Gilgameš tradition.footnote:[George 2003, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, vols. 1–2 (Oxford: Oxford University Press) — the definitive critical edition, with Akkadian text, English translation, and commentary; manuscript catalogue and recensional history in vol. 1. Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, pp. 99–220 (accessible English translation; the working English text for this chapter). Dalley 2008, *Myths from Mesopotamia*, pp. 39–153 (second triangulation translation). Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 1–20 (Gilgameš and the cuneiform literary tradition).]

The SB recension is structured in twelve tablets. Tablets I–XI constitute the principal narrative arc — kingship, companionship, hubris, loss, and the return — and form what George 2003 treats as the integral eleven-tablet epic. Tablet XII is a separate compositional unit: a literal Akkadian translation of the second half of the Sumerian *Gilgameš, Enkidu, and the Netherworld* — literal in technique (near-verbatim, word-for-word) and partial only in scope (covering the Sumerian source's second half) — appended editorially to the SB epic by the late tradition. Tablet XII is not narratively continuous with Tablet XI; Enkidu, who has died in Tablet VII, appears alive again in Tablet XII as a participant in the underworld dialogue. The twelve-tablet shape is therefore the canonical SB form, with the understanding that the twelfth tablet is an editorial annex rather than a narrative continuation.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, introduction (the 12-tablet structure; Tablet XII as editorial appendix; the literal Akkadian translation of the second half of the Sumerian *Gilgameš, Enkidu, and the Netherworld* — literal in technique, partial only in scope); Foster 2005, pp. 215–220 (Tablet XII; the editorial-annex framing); Dalley 2008, pp. 39–47 (the 12-tablet shape; the structural distinction between Tablets I–XI and Tablet XII).]

The SB recension is attributed to the Babylonian scholar-priest Sîn-lēqi-unninni, whose name appears in the first-millennium *Catalogue of Texts and Authors* — a cuneiform list that pairs principal compositions of the Akkadian literary corpus with the names of their tradent scholars. The catalogue is preserved on Neo-Assyrian Nineveh manuscripts (7th c. BCE, from Ashurbanipal's library) and on Late Babylonian witnesses from Babylon; its compositional date is conventionally placed in the 10th–7th centuries BCE (the latest works it names belong to the tenth century, while the earliest surviving manuscripts are seventh-century). The catalogue names Sîn-lēqi-unninni as the master-scribe associated with the Gilgameš series; the redactor Sîn-lēqi-unninni it names is himself conventionally placed in the late second millennium BCE (the Kassite period, ca. 1300–1000 BCE), so that the first-millennium catalogue is a later witness preserving the tradition's memory of a late-2nd-millennium tradent. The attribution is named not only in the catalogue but also in colophons of SB Gilgameš manuscripts at Nineveh and other centres, where later scribal families traced their scholarly lineage to Sîn-lēqi-unninni. The redaction itself — the editorial work of producing the SB recension from the older OB and MB material — is reconstructed by inference from the catalogue notice, the colophon traces, and the manuscript dossier.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, introduction (Sîn-lēqi-unninni's attribution in the *Catalogue of Texts and Authors*; the redaction reconstructed by inference from the catalogue notice and the SB manuscript dossier; the catalogue preserved on first-millennium Nineveh and Late Babylonian manuscripts); Foster 2005, p. 99 (Sîn-lēqi-unninni named as the SB recension's tradent); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (the catalogue tradition; named-tradent attestations in late Akkadian literature; the SB-manuscript colophons recording the scholarly lineage); Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. Gilgameš (the Sîn-lēqi-unninni attribution).]

The Old Babylonian forerunners — preserved on the Pennsylvania tablet (Yale Babylonian Collection), the Yale tablet, the Sippar tablet ("the Old Babylonian Tablet III/IV"), the Schøyen tablet, and a small number of additional OB manuscripts — attest a substantially earlier and structurally more compact Akkadian Gilgameš tradition. The OB material lacks the late-recension prologue *ša naqba īmuru* and corresponds, where the texts overlap, to portions of the SB Tablets II–V and X. The Middle Babylonian forerunners — Akkadian fragments from Boğazköy (the Hittite capital), the Megiddo fragment (Late Bronze Palestine), the Ur fragment, and the Nippur fragment — attest the epic's Late Bronze international diffusion across the cuneiform world. George 2003 gives parallel critical editions of the OB and MB material in vol. 1.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1 (OB forerunners — Pennsylvania, Yale, Sippar, Schøyen; MB forerunners — Boğazköy, Megiddo, Ur, Nippur; structurally more compact OB tradition without the late-recension prologue); Foster 2005, pp. 99–101 (OB and MB material; the relationship to the SB recension); Dalley 2008, pp. 39–47 (the forerunner dossier); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (the OB→MB→SB recensional history).]

=== The two prologues: *ša naqba īmuru* and *šūtur eli šarrī*

The SB recension opens with a prologue that is itself a redactional layer: the so-called late-recension prologue, *ša naqba īmuru*, "He who saw the deep". The phrase names Gilgameš at the outset as the one who saw — or knew, or had access to — *naqbu*, the cosmic deep, the underground sources of wisdom; the prologue then introduces the king as the man who travelled to the ends of the earth, returned with knowledge, and inscribed his story on a stone stele for those who came after. The wisdom-literature register of the prologue — the framing of the epic as the record of a journey for knowledge, set down for later readers — is one of the principal redactional moves of the SB recension and is reconstructed by George 2003 as the front of the SB text.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I, prologue (the late-recension prologue *ša naqba īmuru*, "He who saw the deep"; the wisdom-literature framing; the stele-and-foundations imagery); Foster 2005, pp. 101–103 (the late-recension prologue in translation; the framing as wisdom literature); Dalley 2008, pp. 50–52 (the prologue in translation); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (the wisdom-literature framing as an SB redactional layer).]

The body of the SB Tablet I retains, after the late-recension prologue, what George 2003 reconstructs as the original opening of the older Akkadian Gilgameš tradition: *šūtur eli šarrī*, "Surpassing all kings". The original opening is a hymnic introduction to Gilgameš in his royal capacity — surpassing all kings in stature, in strength, in achievement; the named builder of Uruk's walls; the king of the cedar gate and the great rampart. The two openings co-exist within the SB Tablet I: the wisdom-literature framing on the outside, the hymnic royal introduction inside it. The relationship between the two is structural rather than narrative — they do not contradict each other so much as front the epic from two registers.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I (the original opening *šūtur eli šarrī*, "Surpassing all kings", retained inside the late-recension prologue; the structural relationship of the two openings); Foster 2005, pp. 103–106 (the *šūtur eli šarrī* opening in translation; the hymnic royal introduction); Dalley 2008, pp. 52–55 (the inner opening of Tablet I); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (the two openings as redactional layers).]

=== Tablet I: Gilgameš at Uruk, the creation of Enkidu, the meeting

The SB Tablet I introduces Gilgameš, king of the city of Uruk in the southern Mesopotamian alluvium, two-thirds god and one-third man, son of the goddess Ninsun and of the legendary king Lugalbanda. The introduction characterises Gilgameš at the height of his royal power: builder of Uruk's massive wall and the temple precinct of Ištar, surpassing all kings in strength, beauty, and accomplishment; restless and over-vital, exhausting his subjects with the demands of his energy.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I (Gilgameš's introduction; the two-thirds god / one-third man genealogy; Ninsun and Lugalbanda as parents; the builder of Uruk's walls); Foster 2005, pp. 103–110 (Tablet I introduction in translation); Dalley 2008, pp. 50–58 (the opening characterisation); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Gilgameš (the two-thirds god, one-third man formula).]

Gilgameš's restlessness manifests as oppression of his city. He drives the young men of Uruk in athletic contests and military exercises beyond their capacity; he claims first-night privileges over the brides of the city. The people of Uruk cry out to the gods for relief, and the gods — convened to address the complaint — turn the problem over to the mother-goddess Aruru, the creator-goddess who first formed Gilgameš, with the request that she now create a counterpart who will match Gilgameš in strength and so divert his energy from the city.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I (Gilgameš's oppression of Uruk; the people's complaint; the gods' assembly; Aruru commissioned to create a counterpart); Foster 2005, pp. 106–112 (the oppression and the divine response); Dalley 2008, pp. 53–60 (the complaint and the commission to Aruru).]

Aruru forms Enkidu out of clay, in the wilderness, as Gilgameš's match. Enkidu is wild: covered with hair from head to foot, eating grass with the gazelles, drinking from the watering-hole with the wild beasts; he interferes with the trappers, freeing the animals from their snares and filling in the pit-traps. A trapper who has seen him reports the disturbance to his father, who advises him to take the matter to Gilgameš at Uruk. Gilgameš, when the trapper reaches the city, instructs the trapper to take the temple-prostitute Šamḫat to the watering-hole and have her seduce Enkidu, on the theory that civilization through sexual encounter will sever the wild man from his animal companions.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I (Aruru's creation of Enkidu in the wilderness; the wild characterisation; the trapper's complaint; Gilgameš's instruction to take Šamḫat); Foster 2005, pp. 110–117 (the creation of Enkidu and the trapper episode); Dalley 2008, pp. 58–63 (Enkidu's wilderness phase; the trapper-and-prostitute solution); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Enkidu (the wild-man motif; the civilizing-through-sexuality framing).]

Šamḫat is taken to the watering-hole. She uncovers herself; Enkidu approaches; they couple for six days and seven nights. When Enkidu rises to rejoin the animals, the gazelles flee from him and the wild beasts reject him — sexual experience has changed his nature, and the wilderness no longer accepts him as one of its own. Šamḫat then instructs him: she gives him bread and beer (the staples of human civilization), clothes him, anoints him with oil, brings him to the encampment of the shepherds, and finally leads him toward Uruk. The civilizing of Enkidu through Šamḫat is presented as a transformation through specific cultural acts — the sexual encounter, the staple foods, the clothing, the oil — and is the structural complement of Gilgameš's oppression: the wilderness counterpart is brought into the city.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I and Tablet II opening (the seven-day encounter; the gazelles' rejection; the staple-foods civilizing sequence; the journey to Uruk); Foster 2005, pp. 117–122 (the civilizing of Enkidu); Dalley 2008, pp. 62–66 (the civilizing sequence and the journey to Uruk); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Enkidu (the staple-foods civilizing motif).]

Tablet I closes with two dreams of Gilgameš, reported to his mother Ninsun for interpretation. In the first, a heavy meteor falls from heaven that Gilgameš cannot lift but loves like a wife; in the second, an axe lies in the street of Uruk that he embraces. Ninsun interprets both as the coming companion: the mighty one whom Gilgameš will not be able to lift away and whom he will love. The dream-pair frames Enkidu's arrival as a divinely intended companionship rather than as the relief from oppression that the gods had originally commissioned; Gilgameš's grief over Enkidu's eventual death is foreshadowed in the very first tablet's report of how Enkidu enters the king's life.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet I close (the two dreams; Ninsun's interpretation; the companion-foreshadowing); Foster 2005, pp. 119–122 (the dreams and their interpretation); Dalley 2008, pp. 64–66 (the dream-pair and the framing of Enkidu's arrival); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Gilgameš (the dream-pair as a structural anticipation of the companion).]

=== Tablet II: Enkidu enters Uruk, the wrestling-match, the proposal to attack Ḫumbaba

Šamḫat leads Enkidu to Uruk. As they enter the city, the citizens gather to look at him; Enkidu is appraised as Gilgameš's match in physical stature. === Tablet IV: the seven-day-march journey and the dream sequences

Tablet IV narrates the journey to the Cedar Forest. The structure is a series of seven-day marches: each march covers a distance the text gives in stage-units; each night the heroes dig a well, set up camp, prepare offerings to Šamaš, and Gilgameš sleeps and dreams. Each dream is reported to Enkidu, who interprets it as a favourable omen for the impending confrontation with Ḫumbaba. The repeated structure — march, well, offering, dream, interpretation — is one of the most formally patterned sections of the SB epic and frames the approach to the Cedar Forest as a sequence of escalating omens.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet IV (the seven-day-march framework; the dream sequences; Enkidu's interpretations); Foster 2005, pp. 138–148 (Tablet IV; the march-and-dream sequence); Dalley 2008, pp. 80–87 (the dream sequences and the approach to the Cedar Forest); Black & Green 1992, s.v. === Tablet IX: the journey begins — wandering, Mt Māšu, the Scorpion-People

Tablet IX opens with Gilgameš's departure from Uruk. He has refused to return to his city; he is dressed in lion-skins, his hair unkempt, his face hollow with mourning. He has set himself a new task: to find Uta-napišti, the flood-survivor who was granted immortality by the gods, and to learn from him the secret of life. The departure-as-wanderer is the SB recension's structural pivot: the king of Uruk becomes the wanderer of the cosmic ocean, the maker-of-a-name becomes the seeker-of-the-secret.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet IX opening (Gilgameš's departure as wanderer; the goal of finding Uta-napišti; the structural pivot); Foster 2005, pp. 188–192 (Tablet IX opening); Dalley 2008, pp. 98–101 (the departure and the wanderer-quest); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Gilgameš, Uta-napišti.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=vol. 1, Tablet IX opening (departure as wanderer)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 188-192
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 98-101
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Gilgameš, Uta-napišti

Gilgameš journeys across the wilderness and reaches Mt Māšu — the twin-peaked mountain at the edge of the world, the cosmic boundary that the sun-god Šamaš passes through each morning and evening. The mountain is guarded by the Scorpion-People (*girtablilû*), monstrous beings whose gaze can kill. They challenge Gilgameš; recognising his partly divine nature, they let him pass. === Tablet X: Šiduri, Ur-šanabi, the crossing of the Waters of Death

Tablet X is the journey across the cosmic ocean. Gilgameš first encounters Šiduri, the tavern-keeper at the edge of the sea — the alewife who keeps a tavern at the cosmic shore. She sees the wild, mourning king and asks who he is; Gilgameš tells her his story and asks how to reach Uta-napišti. In the SB recension, Šiduri directs him to Ur-šanabi, Uta-napišti's boatman, who alone can ferry him across the Waters of Death.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet X opening (Šiduri the tavern-keeper; her dialogue with Gilgameš; the direction to Ur-šanabi); Foster 2005, pp. 198–204 (Tablet X; Šiduri); Dalley 2008, pp. 105–110 (Šiduri the alewife); Black & Green 1992, s.v. === Tablet XI: the flood narrative; the plant of rejuvenation; the return

Tablet XI is the SB epic's flood narrative. Gilgameš asks Uta-napišti how he, alone of all humanity, came to be granted immortality; Uta-napišti answers with the story of the flood, in which the gods resolved to destroy humanity, in which Ea warned him through the wall of his reed-hut, in which he built a great cubical boat, loaded it with his family and the animals, and survived the seven-day flood that wiped out humanity. After the flood, the great gods convened; Enlil, seeing that Uta-napišti had survived, was angry; Ea defended him; the gods, persuaded, granted Uta-napišti and his wife immortality and settled them at the mouth of the rivers, at the far edge of the world.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet XI (the flood narrative; the cubical boat; the seven-day flood; the post-flood divine assembly; Uta-napišti's apotheosis to immortality); Foster 2005, pp. 212–220 (Tablet XI; the flood story); Dalley 2008, pp. 114–125 (the flood narrative as Uta-napišti tells it); Lambert & Millard 1969, *Atra-Ḫasīs*, passim (the *Atra-ḫasīs* original of the flood story; Tablet XI as the SB Gilgameš's incorporation of the Atra-ḫasīs material); Black & Green 1992, s.v. === Tablet XII: the editorial appendix

Tablet XII is the editorial appendix to the SB epic. It is a literal Akkadian translation of the second half of the Sumerian poem *Gilgameš, Enkidu, and the Netherworld* (ETCSL 1.8.1.4) — literal in technique, near-verbatim and word-for-word where it appears, and partial only in that it covers the second half of the Sumerian source rather than the whole — appended to the SB Gilgameš series by the late tradition. Tablet XII narrates a different episode: Gilgameš loses two cultic objects (a *pukku* and a *mekkû*, often understood as a drum and drumstick or as a ball and mallet, the precise identification debated in the scholarship) into the underworld; Enkidu — alive in this tablet — descends to retrieve them; he is held in the underworld by various transgressions of underworld taboos; eventually his shade returns to Gilgameš and the two converse about the fates of the dead. The dialogue catalogues the postmortem conditions of various categories of human dead, ranked by their station in life and by the offerings made for them.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, Tablet XII (the editorial appendix; the literal Akkadian translation of the second half of *Gilgameš, Enkidu, and the Netherworld*; the *pukku* and *mekkû*; Enkidu's descent and return; the dialogue on the fates of the dead); Foster 2005, pp. 215–220 (Tablet XII in translation); Dalley 2008, pp. 39–47 and pp. 124–135 (Tablet XII; the editorial-annex framing); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Gilgameš, Enkidu (the Tablet XII episode); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (Tablet XII as editorial appendix).]

Tablet XII is not narratively continuous with Tablets I–XI. Enkidu, who has died in Tablet VII and been buried in Tablet VIII, appears alive in Tablet XII as a member of Gilgameš's household. The two tablets thus belong to different narrative timelines; the placement of Tablet XII after Tablet XI is editorial rather than narrative. George 2003 treats Tablet XII as an annex to the eleven-tablet integral epic; the SB redactor (or a later editor) appended the literal (near-verbatim) translation of the Sumerian poem's second half as a thematic supplement on the underworld and the fates of the dead, and the manuscript tradition transmits the twelve-tablet shape as canonical even though the twelfth tablet is compositionally distinct from the first eleven.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, introduction and Tablet XII apparatus (Tablet XII as editorial annex; the non-continuity with Tablets I–XI; the Enkidu-alive-in-XII problem; the canonical-twelve-tablet shape); Foster 2005, pp. 215–220 (Tablet XII as editorial annex); Dalley 2008, pp. 39–47 (the structural distinction); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (Tablet XII in the SB tradition).]

=== The epic's place in the corpus

The SB *Gilgameš* belongs to the heroic-epic genre of the Akkadian literary tradition. With *Anzû* (chapter 15), it is one of the two principal SB heroic epics in the corpus; with *Etana* (chapter 18), *Tukulti-Ninurta* (chapter 26), and the Sargon-and-Narām-Sîn legend cycle (chapters 19–25), it constitutes the wider Akkadian epic and royal-legend dossier. Noegel 2005 (pp. 233–245) treats the Akkadian epic genre as a unified set; within that set, the SB *Gilgameš* is by general consensus the supreme literary product — the most fully developed, the most psychologically interior, the most thematically integrated.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, introduction (the SB Gilgameš in the Akkadian heroic-epic genre); Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (Gilgameš in the Mesopotamian epic genre); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (the SB Gilgameš as the supreme product of the Akkadian literary tradition); Bottéro 2001, on the SB Gilgameš's psychological and theological reach.]

The manuscript dossier of the SB *Gilgameš* — some 80 first-millennium manuscripts across multiple scribal centres, supplemented by the OB and MB forerunners — is among the largest for any Akkadian literary composition. The geographic spread (Nineveh, Aššur, Sultantepe, Sippar, Babylon, Uruk; Boğazköy, Megiddo, Ur, Nippur in the MB stratum) and the chronological depth (OB ca. 1700 BCE to Late Babylonian colophons of the Hellenistic period) together place the composition at the centre of the Akkadian literary canon. The composition was copied, studied, and re-redacted across the full span of Akkadian literary culture; it was the foundation-text of the Akkadian epic curriculum.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, introduction (the manuscript dossier; the geographic and chronological spread); Foster 2005, pp. 99–101 (the manuscript distribution); Foster 2007, pp. 1–20 (the foundation-text status); Black & Green 1992, s.v.
