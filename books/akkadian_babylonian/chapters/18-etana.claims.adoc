=== Source overview

*Etana* is an Akkadian myth that narrates the predicament of Etana, the early king of the Mesopotamian city of Kish, who has been granted kingship by the gods but lacks an heir, and whose recourse for divine intervention is the heavenly *šammu ša alādi* — the plant of birth, growing in heaven, which would give his wife conception of a son. The composition embeds a self-contained beast-fable — the betrayal of the serpent by the eagle, the eagle's punishment in a pit at Šamaš's decree, the eagle's rescue by Etana, and the eagle's reciprocity in carrying Etana up to heaven — that is one of the rare Mesopotamian fables to survive in fully narrative context. The composition is preserved in three substantively distinct Akkadian recensions: an Old Babylonian recension from Sippar (ca. 1700 BCE), a Middle Assyrian recension from Aššur (mid-2nd millennium BCE), and a Standard Babylonian recension from Nineveh (K.2606 + duplicates, 7th c. BCE). None of the three is complete; the conclusion of the heaven-flight is lost in every witness.footnote:[Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, 3rd ed., pp. 533–554 (Old Babylonian, Middle Assyrian, and Standard Babylonian recensions translated in sequence; pp. 533–537 provide the introductory matter on the three-recension dossier and the structural lost ending). Dalley 2008, *Myths from Mesopotamia*, rev. ed., pp. 189–202 (second triangulation translation). Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 43–46 (Etana in the late tradition; the recensional history). Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. Etana, eagle, serpent.]

Etana is named in the Sumerian King List, the cuneiform chronicle of Mesopotamian dynastic succession compiled in Sumerian during the late third and early second millennia BCE, as the king of Kish — the early Mesopotamian city-state in northern Babylonia — "who ascended to heaven". The SKL framing makes Etana one of the few Akkadian mythological figures whose name is integrated into the formal royal-chronological tradition; the legend of the heaven-flight is thus not merely literary but is registered in the cuneiform tradition's own framework for thinking about kingship and dynastic succession. === Co-equal recensions: a methodological note

The three Akkadian recensions of *Etana* — OB Sippar, MA Aššur, and SB Nineveh — are roughly co-equal in scholarly weight. None alone is sufficient: the OB is the earliest and shows the original framing; the MA is fragmentary but supplies parallel material at points where the OB and SB are damaged; the SB is the most fully preserved on the strength of the K.2606 + duplicates dossier and the Saggs 1986 join. The three tell substantively the same story — the same shared narrative spine of the eagle-and-serpent fable, Etana's predicament of childlessness, the eagle's reciprocity, and the heaven-flight — but differ in length, in the elaboration of individual scenes, and in which portions of the narrative arc each preserves. The chapter therefore presents the recensions in sequence (OB → MA → SB) along the shared narrative spine, with each recension attributed to its source and the chapter foregrounding what each recension uniquely preserves.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533–554 (the three-recension dossier; the co-equal recensional weight; the shared narrative spine); Dalley 2008, pp. 189–202 (the same three-recension format); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the recensional history and the co-equal scholarly weighting).]

=== The shared narrative spine

The three recensions share a common narrative spine that the chapter follows below. The spine: an eagle and a serpent swear an oath of friendship on a shared tree, raising their young together; the eagle betrays the oath by eating the serpent's young; the serpent appeals to Šamaš, the sun-god and judge, for justice; Šamaš devises a trap in which the eagle is caught and thrown into a pit, left to die; meanwhile, Etana — the king of Kish, lacking an heir — prays to Šamaš for the plant of birth that grows in heaven, the *šammu ša alādi*; Šamaš directs Etana to the pit, where Etana rescues the eagle, who recovers and out of gratitude offers to carry Etana up to heaven on his back; the heaven-flight episodes — Etana mounted on the eagle, ascending past the gates of the gods (Ištar's heaven, Anu's heaven) — survive only partially in all three recensions; and the resolution is lost in every witness.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533–554 (the shared narrative spine across the three recensions); Dalley 2008, pp. 189–202 (the same spine in the second triangulation); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the narrative arc and its preservation across the recensions); Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the structural shape of the composition).]

=== The Old Babylonian recension: the founding framing

The OB Sippar recension opens by setting the scene: the gods have decreed kingship for the city of Kish — the early Mesopotamian city-state of northern Babylonia whose name in the Sumerian King List is the first city of postdiluvian kingship — and Etana has been chosen and installed as its king. The cosmic situation of the composition is thus established at the outset: a divinely-sanctioned king is in place, holding the office the gods have decreed.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533–537 (the OB opening; the cosmic situation; Kish as the postdiluvian royal seat; Etana's installation as king); Dalley 2008, pp. 189–192 (the OB framing); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the OB recension's opening framing); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Etana (Etana as king of Kish).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 43-46
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Etana

The OB recension presents the eagle-and-serpent fable as the first substantive narrative episode. The eagle and the serpent meet on a shared tree (in the OB framing, a poplar or comparable tree at the foot of the world-mountain; the precise species detail varies across recensions) and swear an oath of friendship by Šamaš — the sun-god, patron of justice and judge of the gods — that each will protect the other's young. The oath is formally sworn between the two animals, and is the structural premise of the fable: oath, betrayal, and divine retribution form the fable's three-part shape.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 535–540 (the OB eagle-and-serpent oath; the shared-tree setting; the formal oath sworn by Šamaš); Dalley 2008, pp. 189–193 (the OB oath scene); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the fable's three-part structure); Black & Green 1992, s.v. eagle, serpent (the deity-pair in the fable).]

In the OB recension, the eagle betrays the oath. When the serpent is away hunting and the serpent's young are unguarded, the eagle eats the serpent's young — in some passages of the OB narrative, after deliberation with his own chick, who counsels against the betrayal but is overruled. The betrayal is presented as a deliberate decision by the eagle, taken in the knowledge that the oath is being broken; the eagle's own chick functions in the narrative as the voice of the doomed-counsel who warns against the act.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 537–540 (the OB betrayal scene; the eagle's deliberation with his chick; the chick's warning against the betrayal; the eagle's decision to eat the serpent's young); Dalley 2008, pp. 191–193 (the betrayal in the OB framing); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the eagle's deliberation as a distinctive narrative element); Black & Green 1992, s.v. eagle.]

The OB recension narrates Etana's predicament alongside the eagle's punishment. Etana, the king of Kish, has been granted kingship by the gods but lacks an heir; his wife has not conceived, and the dynastic succession is in question. Etana appeals to Šamaš for the plant of birth — the *šammu ša alādi*, the heavenly plant whose substance brings conception — that grows in heaven and that he must obtain from the gods. The OB recension thus aligns the eagle's punishment in the pit with Etana's predicament on earth, setting up the recursion of Šamaš as the deity who hears both appeals.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 538–540 (the OB Etana predicament; the appeal to Šamaš; the plant of birth and the heavenly setting); Dalley 2008, pp. 192–194 (Etana's appeal in the OB framing); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (Etana's predicament and the structural alignment with the eagle's punishment); Black & Green 1992, s.v. In the OB recension, Šamaš directs Etana to the pit in which the eagle is confined. Etana descends to the pit and rescues the eagle — feeds him, restores his wing and feather, brings him back from near-death. The eagle, recovered, recognises Etana's reciprocity-giving act and offers in return to carry Etana up to heaven, where the plant of birth is to be found. The structural reciprocity is the narrative's pivot: the eagle's punishment is the precondition for his eventual service to Etana; Etana's act of rescue is the precondition for his heaven-flight.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 538–540 (the OB rescue and reciprocity scene; the eagle's recovery; the offer to carry Etana to heaven); Dalley 2008, pp. 193–195 (the rescue and the reciprocity offer); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the structural reciprocity); Black & Green 1992, s.v. eagle (the eagle's reciprocity).]

=== The Middle Assyrian recension: a fragmentary intermediate witness

The MA Aššur recension preserves portions of the same narrative spine in Middle Assyrian dialect. The MA recension is fragmentary throughout and functions principally as a parallel witness rather than as a free-standing complete text; its surviving portions overlap with the OB and SB recensions at the eagle-and-serpent fable, the rescue scene, and the opening of the heaven-flight, and supply parallel material where the OB or SB recensions are damaged.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 540–544 (the MA Aššur recension; its fragmentary preservation; the parallel-witness function; the overlapping portions with OB and SB); Dalley 2008, pp. 193–197 (the MA recension as parallel witness); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the MA recension's intermediate dialect and the parallel-witness role); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Etana.]

The MA recension's preserved portions of the eagle-and-serpent fable elaborate the betrayal scene — including the eagle's deliberation with his chick, the chick's warning against the betrayal, and the eagle's decision to eat the serpent's young — and the serpent's appeal to Šamaš. The MA recension confirms the fable's three-part shape (oath, betrayal, divine retribution) and the recursive role of Šamaš as the deity who hears both the serpent's appeal and, later, Etana's prayer.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 540–544 (the MA fable; the betrayal scene; the chick's warning; the serpent's appeal; the recursion of Šamaš); Dalley 2008, pp. 193–197 (the MA fable in the second triangulation); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the MA fable's elaboration).]

The MA recension also preserves portions of the rescue scene and the opening of the heaven-flight, where the eagle restores himself in the pit through Etana's intervention and offers reciprocity in return. The MA recension's breaks are extensive enough that its independent narrative cannot be followed as a continuous text, but the surviving fragments confirm the narrative arc as it appears in the OB and SB recensions and support the chapter's treatment of the three recensions as parallel witnesses to a shared composition.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 540–544 (the MA rescue and the opening heaven-flight; the parallel-witness function); Dalley 2008, pp. 193–197 (the MA rescue scene); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the MA recension's coverage of the narrative arc).]

=== The Standard Babylonian recension: the most fully preserved witness

The SB Nineveh recension, preserved on K.2606 (the Aššurbanipal-library tablet) and its duplicate manuscripts, is the most fully preserved of the three recensions and is the standard scholarly base on which the surviving narrative arc is most extensively recoverable. === The heaven-flight: the partly-preserved episodes

The heaven-flight is the composition's signature episode and survives in partly-preserved form across the SB recension (the OB and MA recensions break before the flight's principal episodes are reached, with limited overlap). Etana mounts the eagle's back and is carried upward; the eagle ascends through stages, and at each stage the surviving text gives a measure of the receding world below — the lands of men shrinking to small features, the sea narrowing to a band, the world becoming smaller and stranger as the ascent continues. The flight is staged as an ascent through cosmic levels rather than as a single continuous rise.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 550–554 (the SB heaven-flight; the staged ascent; the receding-earth imagery; the partly-preserved episodes); Dalley 2008, pp. 200–202 (the heaven-flight in the SB framing); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the heaven-flight as the composition's signature episode); Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the staged-ascent structure).]

=== Ritual context, iconography, and theological function

*Etana* serves multiple functions across its recensions. At the royal-legitimation level, the composition presents the model of the king who needs divine intervention to secure dynastic continuity; the heaven-flight grounds royal-divine relations and articulates the conditional dependence of the dynastic line on the gods' grant. At the didactic level, the eagle-and-serpent fable teaches the consequences of betrayal — oath-breaking incurs divine retribution, and reciprocity is the formal restoration of broken relations. At the literary level, the composition combines an embedded beast-fable with a heroic heaven-flight, a structural pairing unusual in Akkadian narrative literature.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533–554 (the royal-legitimation, didactic, and literary functions); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (Etana's functions in the late tradition); Bottéro 2001 (royal-divine relations and the antediluvian / postdiluvian sequence); Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the structural pairing of fable and heroic heaven-flight); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Etana.]

The Etana-on-eagle motif is attested in Mesopotamian iconography from the Old Akkadian period onward — on cylinder seals and seal-impressions from the late third millennium and the early second millennium BCE. The iconographic tradition is older than any of the surviving literary recensions: the seals depict the figure mounted on the great bird and ascending past the disc of the sun, with the iconographic conventions that are recognisably those of Etana. The composition's textual tradition thus inherits an iconographic tradition that already existed; the literary recensions document a narrative whose visual representation predates them.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. === The poem's place in the corpus

*Etana* occupies a distinctive position in the Akkadian narrative corpus. It is one of the few Akkadian mythological compositions whose central figure is named in the Sumerian King List's royal sequence — Etana is the king of Kish "who ascended to heaven", placed in the postdiluvian royal sequence — and the composition's narrative thus operates simultaneously as a piece of literary mythology and as the legendary expansion of an SKL royal-list notice. The integration with the SKL gives the composition a documentary anchor unusual in the Akkadian mythological dossier: Etana is one of the few mythological figures whose existence the cuneiform tradition formally registers in its own royal chronology.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 533 (Etana's SKL integration; the documentary anchor; the legendary expansion of an SKL royal-list notice); Bottéro 2001 (the SKL framing and royal-divine relations); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the SKL integration and the documentary anchor); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Etana (Etana in the SKL).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on royal-divine relations and SKL framing
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 43-46
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Etana

*Etana* is also one of the few Akkadian narrative compositions to embed a fully-developed beast-fable within a heroic narrative frame. The eagle-and-serpent fable preserves the formal three-part structure of the disputation- and fable-genre that becomes more prominent in the wisdom literature of the later Akkadian tradition (the Akkadian disputations between trees, birds, animals, and elements — the wisdom-and-fable cycle that occupies chapters 34–39 of the present volume — develops the genre at greater length). The *Etana* fable is one of the rare cases in which the fable is preserved with full narrative context rather than as a self-contained didactic piece, and its preservation within a heroic-epic frame is one of the structural distinctives of the composition.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533–554 (the *Etana* fable's preservation in a heroic frame; the structural distinctiveness); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the *Etana* fable in the wider context of Akkadian fable-and-disputation literature); Noegel 2005, pp. 233–245 (the embedded fable within heroic narrative); Black & Green 1992, s.v. The composition's transmission across this span testifies to its standing in the Akkadian scribal canon: the *Etana* legend, with its eagle-and-serpent fable and its heaven-flight, is a composition the Mesopotamian scribes copied and recopied across periods and across dialect boundaries.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533–554 (the three-recension chronological and geographic spread; the composition's standing in the Akkadian scribal canon); Dalley 2008, pp. 189–202 (the geographic and chronological spread); Foster 2007, pp. 43–46 (the *Etana* tradition's transmission across the Akkadian scribal world).]
