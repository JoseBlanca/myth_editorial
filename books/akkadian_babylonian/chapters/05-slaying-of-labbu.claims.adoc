=== Source overview

*The Slaying of Labbu* is a short, heavily fragmentary Akkadian combat-myth preserved on a single Neo-Assyrian tablet, published as CT 13, plates 33–34 (British Museum, *Cuneiform Texts from Babylonian Tablets in the British Museum*, vol. 13).footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 361–365 (text, translation, commentary, and manuscript description); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 581, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-365
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 581

The composition has no preserved ancient title. The modern title is editorial, taken from the name of the antagonist, Labbu — Akkadian for "raging" or "lion-like" — who is the dragon-monster the surviving fragment describes.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 361 (modern title and lexical note); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Labbu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 361
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Labbu

The standard critical edition is W. G. Lambert's treatment in *Babylonian Creation Myths* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 16, Eisenbrauns 2013), pp. 361–365.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–365 (full edition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-365

The accessible English translation cited throughout for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "The Slaying of Labbu," in *Before the Muses*, third edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 581–582.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 581–582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 581-582

The single manuscript is Neo-Assyrian in date; no Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, or other parallel manuscripts are known. The composition cannot be cross-checked against any other witness.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (single-manuscript transmission); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-362

[LACUNA: CT 13, 33–34 — extensive breaks throughout the surviving column. The hero's name, the kill scene itself, and the post-combat outcome are all lost in the lacuna. | source: Lambert 2013, pp. 361–365 | scholarly_reconstruction: not available — single manuscript with no parallel Akkadian witnesses for fill]

The surviving fragment preserves only a portion of the narrative: the divine planning council and the start of the combat. The kill itself is broken away, and the post-combat aftermath is not recoverable.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (surviving narrative scope); Foster 2005, p. 581, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-362

=== The setting: Labbu rises from the sea

In the surviving opening, Labbu — a primordial dragon-monster — has emerged from the Sea (Akkadian *tâmtu*) and is terrorising the gods.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 362–363 (the opening preserved lines); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 362-363

The text describes Labbu's monstrous form in some detail: the dragon is enormous, covered in scales, with multiple heads in some readings, and emerges from the saltwater Sea rather than the freshwater Apsû.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 362–363 (Labbu's description); Foster 2005, p. 581; Black & Green 1992, s.v. monsters.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 362-363
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. monsters

The gods are terrified by Labbu's appearance. They convene in council to decide how to respond.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 362–363 (the divine fear); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 362-363

=== The divine council and the dispatch of the champion

The moon-god Sîn appears in the surviving council scene, addressing the assembled gods about the threat Labbu poses.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 363 (Sîn's role in the council); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 363

A divine champion is dispatched against Labbu. The champion's name, however, is broken in the surviving manuscript and cannot be recovered with certainty.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 363–364 (the champion's name lost); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 363-364

[INFERENCE: The lost hero is most commonly identified by scholars as Tišpak, the warrior-god of Ešnunna, on the basis that Tišpak is the dragon-slayer of choice in the Old Babylonian Diyala-region tradition and that the surviving narrative fits his combat profile. Other proposals include Marduk and Nergal, but Tišpak is the conjecture most often advanced. | basis: Lambert 2013 p. 364 commentary discusses the Tišpak conjecture; Black & Green 1992 s.v. Tišpak notes Tišpak's dragon-slaying iconography on Diyala-region cylinder seals; the surviving fragment's combat profile (warrior-god dispatched by divine council against a sea-monster) matches Tišpak's known type. | risk: the hero's name is lost in the manuscript and the Tišpak identification is conjectural rather than textually preserved; alternative scholarly proposals exist.]

The champion is equipped with weapons and divine authority for the combat. The surviving text preserves part of this equipping scene before the manuscript breaks off.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 363–364 (the equipping scene); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 363-364

The combat itself begins on the preserved tablet but is not completed before the text breaks. The surviving lines describe the champion's approach to Labbu and the opening exchanges of the combat.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 364 (the combat opening); Foster 2005, p. 582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 364

=== What is lost

The kill scene is not preserved. Whether the champion uses a particular weapon, addresses Labbu in speech, or relies on cunning rather than force is unrecoverable. The standard Mesopotamian combat-myth pattern (champion-versus-monster culminating in a decisive killing blow) is presumed but not attested in the surviving manuscript.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 364–365 (on what is lost); Foster 2005, p. 582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 364-365

The post-combat aftermath is also lost. Whether Labbu's body becomes a feature of the cosmos (as Tiamat's does in *Enūma Eliš*), whether the champion is exalted to higher status (as Marduk and Ninurta are after their combats), and whether the text closes with a hymnic-theological recitation are all unknown.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 364–365; Foster 2005, p. 582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 364-365

[INFERENCE: The lost ending most likely included a decisive kill and some form of cosmic-political consequence — the standard pattern in the Akkadian combat-myth corpus. | basis: every preserved Akkadian combat-myth (Marduk vs. Tiamat, Ninurta vs. Anzû, Erra's plague-combat) culminates in a decisive kill or victory followed by political consequence; Lambert 2013 p. 365 reads the lost ending as fitting this pattern; Bottéro 2001's combat-myth typology supports the structural reading. | risk: the surviving text does not preserve the ending, and the pattern-completion is therefore an extrapolation from the corpus rather than a textually attested reconstruction.]

=== Variants

The Slaying of Labbu has no parallel manuscripts. CT 13, 33–34 is the only surviving copy of the composition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (single-manuscript transmission); Foster 2005, p. 581.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The Slaying of Labbu* is marginal in attestation but significant as one node in the broader Akkadian combat-myth dossier. Akkadian literature preserves several combat-myths — Marduk versus Tiamat (in *Enūma Eliš*), Ninurta versus Anzû, Erra and Išum's plague-combat, and the Cuthaean Legend's monstrous adversaries — and Labbu sits among them as an alternative combat-cosmogony that survives only in fragmentary form.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 364–365 (Labbu's place in the combat-myth dossier); Foster 2005, p. 581, headnote; Bottéro 2001, on the combat-myth typology.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 364-365
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 581
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=combat-myth typology

The composition's significance lies less in its own narrative — too fragmentary to support deep treatment — than in its presence as evidence that the Akkadian theological imagination ran multiple combat-cosmogonies in parallel. Marduk's defeat of Tiamat in *Enūma Eliš* is the canonical version, but other compositions described other dragon-slayings, with other heroes, in other places.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 365; Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 365

[INFERENCE: The Slaying of Labbu is best read as part of an alternative combat-cosmogony dossier that surrounded the dominant Marduk-Tiamat narrative — evidence that the cosmic-combat motif circulated in multiple recensions in the Akkadian theological imagination, even when only one (the Marduk version) achieved canonical status through the *akītu* festival. | basis: Lambert 2013 pp. 364–365 places Labbu within this dossier; Foster 2005's headnote characterises the composition as one of several "alternative" combat-cosmogonies; Bottéro 2001 on the combat-myth typology. | risk: the structural reading is interpretive; the fragmentary state of the text leaves uncertain whether Labbu was a major rival to Marduk-Tiamat or a peripheral local-cult composition.]
