=== Source overview

*Nergal and Ereškigal* is an Akkadian myth that narrates how Nergal — war-god of the upper world — descends to the underworld, encounters Ereškigal its queen, and becomes by various means her co-ruler. The composition is preserved in three substantially different recensions across nearly a millennium of Akkadian transmission: a brief Middle Babylonian version from Amarna, a long Standard Babylonian version from Sultantepe, and a partial Late Babylonian fragment from Uruk.footnote:[Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, pp. 506–524 (translations of all three recensions); Dalley 2008, *Myths from Mesopotamia*, pp. 163–181 (a second triangulation translation); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 30–34 (recensional history).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 163-181
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 30-34

The earliest witness is the Middle Babylonian tablet EA 357 from the Amarna archive in Egypt, written in Akkadian (the lingua franca of Late Bronze Age diplomacy), dated to the fourteenth century BCE. The Amarna recension is a brief composition of approximately ninety lines preserving a compact etiological version of the myth. EA 357 was found alongside other Akkadian literary tablets in the Amarna archive — *Adapa* (EA 356), *Šar tamḫāri* (the *King of Battle*, EA 359), and miscellaneous lexical and educational material — and the cluster is itself an important witness to Akkadian's Late Bronze Age international circulation as a learned and diplomatic language.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 506 (Amarna recension; EA 357; date and length); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (the Amarna Akkadian-literary cluster); Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. Amarna.]

The principal Standard Babylonian witness is the Sultantepe tablet conventionally cited as STT 28 (with joins and duplicates), excavated at the southeastern Anatolian site of Sultantepe (ancient Ḫuzirina) in 1951 and edited by O. Gurney's edition remains the standard critical reference; Foster 2005 and Dalley 2008 use it as their base text for the long recension.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506, 514–524 (Sultantepe recension; STT 28 + joins; Gurney 1960 edition); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (Sultantepe / Ḫuzirina context); Gurney 1960, "The Sultantepe Tablets (Continued): VII. === Co-equal recensions: a methodological note

The MB Amarna and SB Sultantepe recensions are roughly co-equal in scholarly weight. The Amarna version is shorter (~90 lines) and earlier (14th century BCE); the Sultantepe version is much longer (~750 lines) and later (7th century BCE). Neither dominates the scholarly record. The two recensions tell substantially different versions of the same myth — same characters and outcome, but very different narrative treatment, register, and length. The chapter therefore presents both recensions in sequence, each attributed to its source, rather than reducing them to a single composite text.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 506 (the co-equal recensional status; the substantial differences); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (recensional history; both recensions as scholarly reference points).]

=== The Middle Babylonian Amarna recension

The Amarna recension opens at a divine banquet hosted by Anu, the sky-god. The gods of heaven gather; Ereškigal, queen of the underworld, cannot ascend to attend in person but is to receive her portion. Anu and the assembled gods send Namtar — Ereškigal's vizier — up to fetch her share of the banquet.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–509 (Amarna recension opening; Anu's banquet and Namtar as Ereškigal's emissary); Dalley 2008, pp. 163–164 (the Amarna opening); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Anu, Namtar.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 506-509 (Amarna opening)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 163-164
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Anu, Namtar

When Namtar arrives at the assembly, every god rises to honour him as Ereškigal's representative — every god except one. Nergal, the war-god, fails to stand. The slight is reported back to Ereškigal; she takes offence and demands that the offending god be sent down to her for punishment.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–509 (Namtar's arrival; Nergal's failure to rise; Ereškigal's demand); Dalley 2008, pp. 163–164 (the Nergal-fails-to-stand motif).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 506-509 (Nergal fails to stand)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 163-164

In the Amarna recension, Nergal descends to the underworld to face Ereškigal. The brief composition does not elaborate the descent through gates or the complex stratagems that the later Sultantepe recension develops; instead, it moves quickly to a violent confrontation. Inside Ereškigal's house, Nergal seizes her by the hair and pulls her from her throne. He is on the point of killing her when she pleads for her life — offering him joint kingship of the underworld in exchange. Nergal accepts; the two are married, and he is established as the underworld's co-ruler alongside Ereškigal. The Amarna recension closes with this etiological resolution: a violent confrontation converted into joint rule by Ereškigal's plea.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–509 (Amarna encounter; the hair-seizing scene; Ereškigal's plea for joint rule; Nergal as underworld co-ruler); Dalley 2008, pp. 164–165 (the violent confrontation and the etiological resolution).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 506-509 (Amarna resolution)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 164-165

The Amarna recension's etiological function is explicit at the level of plot: the composition explains how Nergal — by origin a war-god of the upper world, in some other Akkadian compositions identified with Erra and associated with violent death and plague — came to share the throne of the underworld with Ereškigal. The Amarna text supplies a divine-quarrel-resolved-by-marriage frame that establishes the cosmic-political configuration the narrative presupposes.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–509 (etiological function); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Nergal (on Nergal's wider attestation as war-god and his identification with Erra); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on the cult of Nergal.]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Nergal
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on Nergal

=== The Standard Babylonian Sultantepe recension: opening at the divine banquet

The Sultantepe recension opens at the same setting as the Amarna version — Anu's divine banquet — but elaborates the framing significantly. The gods of heaven gather; Ereškigal, queen of the underworld, cannot ascend to attend in person; Namtar is sent up to receive her portion of the banquet. So far the Sultantepe opening parallels the Amarna.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Sultantepe recension opening; the divine banquet); Dalley 2008, pp. 165–166 (Sultantepe opening); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Anu, Namtar.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Sultantepe opening)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 165-166

When Namtar arrives, Nergal again fails to rise to honour Ereškigal's representative. But the Sultantepe recension elaborates the consequences. The slight is reported, Ereškigal demands the offending god, and Nergal — knowing the danger — does not descend immediately. Instead, he goes to Ea, god of wisdom, the freshwater *Apsû*, and incantation, patron of craft and cunning, father of Marduk, for advice and protection.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Sultantepe: Nergal's appeal to Ea); Dalley 2008, pp. 166–167 (Ea's advice).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Ea's advice)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 166-167

=== Ea's stratagem: the fourteen demons and the chair-trick

Ea instructs Nergal in a battery of ritual stratagems for the descent. He sends Nergal down with fourteen apotropaic demons — protectors against the powers of the underworld — and gives instructions for how Nergal is to comport himself when he arrives in *Kurnugê*. Foremost among Ea's instructions is a warning against accepting any of the underworld's offerings: he is not to sit on the chair offered to him, not to eat the food, not to drink the beer, not to wash his feet in the water, and — when Ereškigal removes her clothes to bathe — he is not to lie with her (Foster's translation glosses this last prohibition as a warning against looking upon the bathing queen, while more literal translations render the underlying Akkadian phrase as a prohibition on doing "what men and women do"). Any of these acts will bind him to the underworld permanently.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Ea's stratagem; the fourteen demons; the prohibitions on chair, food, beer, water, and looking); Dalley 2008, pp. 167–168 (Ea's instructions); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ea.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Ea's stratagem)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 167-168
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ea

Nergal descends through the seven gates of the underworld, accompanied by his fourteen demons. The seven-gate descent in the SB recension parallels the seven-gate descent of *The Descent of Ishtar* (chapter 13), but the gates here are not stripping-points: they are entry points through which Nergal and his demonic escort pass. Ereškigal's vizier Namtar receives Nergal at each gate.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (the seven gates; Nergal's descent with demons); Dalley 2008, pp. 168–169 (Namtar at the gates); Black & Green 1992, s.v. underworld.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (seven gates)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 168-169
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. underworld

Within Ereškigal's palace, Nergal applies Ea's instructions: he refuses the chair, the food, the beer, the foot-bath. The stratagem holds — until the moment when Ereškigal removes her clothes to bathe. The bathing-prohibition fails, and the two come together in passionate union for six days and six nights.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (the chair-trick; Nergal's refusals; the bathing scene; the six-day union); Dalley 2008, pp. 169–171 (the courtship climax).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Nergal's refusals; the bathing scene)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 169-171

[INFERENCE: The Sultantepe recension's elaborate prohibitions — chair, food, beer, water, looking — parallel a wider Mesopotamian narrative pattern in which the underworld's offers must be refused on pain of permanent retention. The same pattern underlies the *Adapa* narrative (Ea instructs Adapa not to eat the food and drink offered by Anu, with similar binding consequences) and the *Gilgameš* narrative (Uta-napišti and the reed-bed). === Nergal's flight and return

After six days and six nights of union with Ereškigal, on the seventh day Nergal escapes back to the upper world while she sleeps. The Sultantepe recension narrates his flight with elaborate care: he ascends through the seven gates, past Namtar, and back to the divine assembly. Ereškigal, awakening, finds him gone and reacts with grief and rage.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Nergal's escape; Ereškigal's reaction); Dalley 2008, pp. 171–173 (the flight and Ereškigal's rage).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Nergal's flight)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 171-173

Ereškigal sends Namtar back up to the divine assembly with a different message: she demands not punishment but Nergal's return. If Nergal is not sent back to her, she threatens to raise the dead so that they outnumber the living and devour them — the same threat Ishtar had earlier issued at the gate of *Kurnugê* in *The Descent of Ishtar*. The threat is the underworld's standard sanction: the queen's power to disrupt the boundary between the living and the dead.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Ereškigal's threat; the dead-outnumber-the-living motif); Dalley 2008, pp. 173–174 (the threat to the cosmic order).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Ereškigal's threat)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 173-174

The divine assembly receives Namtar's threat and decides — by various means in the partly-damaged passages of the SB tablet — to send Nergal back. The Sultantepe recension's portrayal of the council is fragmentary, but the outcome is preserved: Nergal returns to *Kurnugê*.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (the assembly's decision; Nergal's return); Dalley 2008, pp. 174–175 (the council scenes).]

=== Nergal's second descent and Anu's decree

Nergal's second descent in the Sultantepe recension is structurally and tonally different from his first. The first descent was cautious, equipped with Ea's protective stratagems and the fourteen demons; the second is impassioned and direct. Nergal seizes Ereškigal by the hair and draws her down from her throne — the same hair-seizing motif preserved in the Amarna recension's climactic confrontation, but here the gesture is one of reunion rather than existential threat. The Sultantepe seizure resolves in embrace; the Amarna seizure ends in Ereškigal's plea for life. The shared motif distributed differently across the two recensions is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that the brief Amarna and the long Sultantepe versions belong to a single tradition.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–509, 514–524 (the hair-seizing motif in both recensions); Dalley 2008, pp. 175–177 (the SB second descent).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 506-509, 514-524 (hair-seizing in both recensions)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 175-177

The Sultantepe recension closes with Anu's decree. Much of the decree is damaged on the tablet, but the surviving lines establish that Nergal is to remain forever with Ereškigal as co-ruler of the underworld. The poem's final preserved line shows Nergal and Ereškigal entering the bedchamber together. There is no separate wedding ceremony or coronation sequence in the surviving text: Anu's decree IS the ratification, and the bedchamber reunion IS the resolution. The Sultantepe text's romantic-courtly register thus closes not on a public ceremony but on a private reunion authorised by Anu's word.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Anu's decree; the closing bedchamber line); Dalley 2008, pp. 177–179 (the closing decree and bedchamber reunion); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Nergal (on Nergal's role as underworld consort).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 514-524 (Anu's decree, closing line)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 177-179
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Nergal

[INFERENCE: The Sultantepe recension's elaborate courtship-and-wedding register — extended Ea-instruction sequence, refusals catalogue, passionate union, separation, return, formal installation — is unusual within Akkadian myth, which more commonly tells its stories in violent or austere registers. The closest Akkadian-internal comparandum for the elaborated courtly-romantic register is found within the *Gilgameš* tradition (Tablet VI's encounter between Ištar and Gilgameš), but *Nergal and Ereškigal*'s Sultantepe recension develops the romantic-courtly mode at full length and as the composition's central matter rather than as an episode within a longer epic. The recension is one of the most extended Akkadian treatments of divine courtship as a literary subject in its own right. | basis: the elaborated courtship register is preserved at length in the Sultantepe recension (Foster 2005, pp. 514–524; Gurney 1960); the contrast with the Amarna recension's brief etiological treatment makes the SB recension's literary distinctiveness visible; Foster 2007 (pp. 30–34) places the SB recension within the Neo-Assyrian elite-literary milieu where this register is occasionally cultivated. | risk: the "elaborated courtly-romantic register" framing is the chapter's editorial reading; the Sultantepe scribes did not announce their text in those terms. === Ritual context and theological function

*Nergal and Ereškigal* serves multiple theological functions across its recensions. At the etiological level, the composition explains how the war-god and the underworld queen came to share the throne of *Kurnugê* — a cosmic-political configuration that the wider Akkadian theology presupposes (Nergal's identification with the underworld is well-attested across the Akkadian dossier independently of this composition). At the literary level, the Sultantepe recension develops the courtly-romantic register to a degree unusual in Akkadian myth.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506, 514–524 (etiological and literary functions); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (theological context); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Nergal, Ereškigal (cult and theology); Bottéro 2001, on the cult of Nergal.]

The Sultantepe recension's preservation in Aššurbanipal-period Anatolia is itself significant. Sultantepe (ancient Ḫuzirina) was a provincial Neo-Assyrian scribal centre, not a royal capital; its library preserved literary, scholarly, and ritual texts in a distinctively eclectic Neo-Assyrian register. The presence of an elaborated *Nergal and Ereškigal* recension in this library — alongside the Sultantepe witnesses of *The Poor Man of Nippur* (chapter 41), Lambert's *Babylonian Wisdom Literature* compositions, and other elite-literary texts — places the long recension within the wider Neo-Assyrian literary scribal culture.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 514–524 (Sultantepe library context); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (Sultantepe / Ḫuzirina scribal culture); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Sultantepe.]

The Amarna recension's preservation in Egypt is equally significant for a different reason. EA 357 was found in the Amarna archive of pharaonic correspondence and scribal training material, alongside other Akkadian literary tablets that show the international circulation of Akkadian narrative literature in the Late Bronze Age. *Nergal and Ereškigal*'s presence at Amarna — together with *Adapa* (EA 356), *Šar tamḫāri* (the *King of Battle*, EA 359), and miscellaneous lexical and educational material — is itself a piece of evidence for Akkadian's role as the lingua franca of Late Bronze Age scribal culture across Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Levant, and Anatolia.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 506 (Amarna recension; Late Bronze international circulation); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (Amarna scribal context); Black & Green 1992, s.v. [VARIANT: primary=Sultantepe SB recension (STT 28 + joins, edited by Gurney 1960 in *Anatolian Studies* 10), supplemented by the Late Babylonian Uruk fragment | alt=Amarna MB recension (EA 357), preserved at Amarna in Egypt; brief and earlier, with damage to the opening and closing | chosen: BOTH RECENSIONS PRESENTED in sequence, per the brief's co-equal classification — the chapter does not "choose" one over the other but treats them as roughly co-equal in scholarly weight | reason: per the chapter-claims skill's co-equal handling, both recensions are listed with their attributions; the body of the chapter narrates each recension separately, then the variants section discusses their relationship. The two principal recensions differ in length, register, and emphasis but share the same core narrative skeleton: divine banquet → Nergal's failure to rise → Ereškigal's offence → Nergal descends → encounter and union → joint rule. The Amarna brief recension treats the skeleton at minimal length, foregrounding the etiological resolution (joint rule established). The Sultantepe long recension elaborates each stage, with Ea's instruction sequence, the fourteen demons, the chair-trick, the prohibitions catalogue, the six-day union, the flight and return, the second impassioned descent (with the hair-seizing motif also attested in the Amarna climax), and the closing scene with Anu's decree and the bedchamber reunion. Where the recensions overlap, they confirm the same outcome.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–524 (the recensional comparison); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (recensional history; shared skeleton).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 30-34

The Late Babylonian Uruk fragment is brief and partly overlaps with the Sultantepe SB recension. It does not represent a third independent recension but rather a partial witness to the long version, valuable for filling some Sultantepe gaps and for confirming the long-recension's circulation into the Late Babylonian period.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 506 (Uruk fragment; relation to Sultantepe); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (Late Babylonian transmission).]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Nergal and Ereškigal* is one of the principal Akkadian underworld narratives. With *The Descent of Ishtar* (chapter 13), it forms the paired underworld dossier of the Akkadian narrative tradition: *Descent of Ishtar* gives the goddess's descent, captivity, retrieval, and substitution (the seasonal-vegetation cycle); *Nergal and Ereškigal* gives the war-god's descent, courtship, union, and joint rule (the cosmic-marriage cycle). Together the two compositions sketch the underworld theology of Mesopotamia: a queendom of the dead governed by Ereškigal, with Nergal as her co-ruler.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506, 498–505 (the paired underworld dossier; *Descent of Ishtar* and *Nergal and Ereškigal* together); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ereškigal, Nergal, underworld.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 506, 498-505
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ereškigal, Nergal, underworld

The composition's geographic spread — Amarna, Sultantepe, Uruk — is itself notable. Few Akkadian compositions are preserved across so wide a range: from Egypt to Anatolia to southern Mesopotamia, and across nearly a millennium of transmission. The geographic and chronological breadth places *Nergal and Ereškigal* among the most internationally and recensionally diverse compositions in the Akkadian narrative tradition.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506–524 (geographic and chronological spread); Foster 2007, pp. 30–34 (recensional diversity); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Amarna, Sultantepe.]
