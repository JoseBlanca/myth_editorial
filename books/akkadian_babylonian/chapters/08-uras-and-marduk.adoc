== Uraš and Marduk

A single poorly-preserved tablet, excavated at Ur and catalogued as UET 6, 398 in the corpus *Ur Excavations Texts* (vol. 6: Sumerian and Akkadian literary texts from Ur), preserves a short Akkadian composition that articulates a cosmogonic relationship between Marduk — patron god of Babylon — and Urash, the tutelary deity of the city of Dilbat. The composition has no preserved ancient title; W. G. Lambert's 2013 edition gives it the modern editorial title *Uraš and Marduk* after the two principal divine actors in the surviving lines.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 311–315 (text, translation, and commentary) and p. 496 (notes on the text); Streck 2014, "W. G. Lambert: *Babylonian Creation Myths*", review article in *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 77 (2014), 371–372, identifies the manuscript base as "Uraš and Marduk (UET 6, 398)".]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315 + p. 496

The standard — and only — modern critical edition is Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315, with notes on the text at p. 496. There is no widely-used English-language anthology translation: neither Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., 2005) nor Dalley's *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., 2008) includes *Uraš and Marduk*. Lambert's edition stands alone.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (the sole modern critical edition); Foster 2005 and Dalley 2008 (do not contain this composition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=full table of contents
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=full table of contents

The surviving tablet is poorly preserved, with substantial breaks throughout. The narrative is recoverable in outline but not in detail; major portions of the text — including the precise sequence of Marduk's creation acts, the role of any subsidiary deities, and the closing lines — are lost or heavily damaged.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (manuscript condition and apparatus).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315

The date of the composition is uncertain. The surviving tablet, UET 6, 398, was excavated at Ur, but the composition itself may have originated at Dilbat — the cult-centre of the tutelary god Urash — and may date to the Old Babylonian or Kassite period rather than to the late-Babylonian period in which much of the surrounding cosmogonic dossier was preserved. The tablet's date and the composition's date should be distinguished.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (manuscript date and composition date discussion); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, on the late-Babylonian preservation context for older compositions; the OB / Kassite / late-Babylonian dating range follows the standard Assyriological discussion of UET 6, 398.]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=on the late-Babylonian preservation context

=== Urash of Dilbat: a male agricultural tutelary

Urash, in this composition, is the tutelary god of Dilbat — modern Tell al-Deylam in Iraq's Babil Governorate, in the heartland of Marduk's Babylonian cultural sphere. He is an agricultural deity, and the late-Babylonian commentary tradition develops his profile through a chain of functional epithets: "Ninurta of the hoe", "of the calendar", "of the tenant farmer", and — most pointedly for this composition — "Marduk of planting". His main temple in Dilbat is *E-ibbi-Anum*, "Anu has called it into being" — a name that links him to Anu, the high-god of the older pantheon, without making him Anu's consort.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. Urash (the male tutelary of Dilbat; agricultural character; equation with Ninurta and Marduk in late commentary lists); RlA s.v. Uraš B (the male Dilbat deity; commentary equations and temple).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Urash (the male Dilbat tutelary)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Uraš B

This Urash must be distinguished from a homonymous female cosmogonic deity also called Uraš — the spouse of Anu, attested in earlier Mesopotamian theology as a personification of the earth and a primeval figure. The two are different deities, with different cults, iconographies, and theological roles. *Uraš and Marduk* is about the male Dilbat tutelary; the female cosmogonic Uraš is not the figure in this composition.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. Urash (explicitly distinguishing the male Dilbat deity from the female spouse of Anu); RlA s.v. Uraš A (the female cosmogonic deity, treated as a separate article from Uraš B); Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (commentary on the identity of Urash in the surviving lines).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Urash
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Uraš A and Uraš B
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315

[INFERENCE: The male Urash of Dilbat is best understood, in the late-Babylonian theological imagination, as a local-cult double or sub-aspect of Marduk himself. The "Marduk of planting" equation in late-Babylonian commentary lists treats Urash as one of Marduk's regional / functional aspects, alongside Marduk-Asalluhi, Marduk-Tutu, and the other syncretistic identifications collected in *Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII's fifty names of Marduk. *Uraš and Marduk* can be read as a small narrative articulation of this syncretism: Marduk creating plant life for what is effectively a local-cult version of himself. | basis: the late-Babylonian commentary equation "Urash = Marduk of planting" is articulated within the *Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII fifty-names commentary at Lambert 2013, pp. 264–265, and is attested in the syncretistic god-list tradition more broadly (Black & Green 1992, s.v. Urash); the *Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII fifty-names sequence as a whole (Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277) is the canonical late-Babylonian articulation of Marduk's syncretism with regional / functional deities; Bottéro 2001 on Marduk's syncretism in the Babylonian pantheon. | risk: this is a structural reading of the composition's place in the wider syncretistic dossier, not a textually attested self-identification within *Uraš and Marduk* itself; the surviving lines do not preserve any passage in which the composition explicitly thematises its syncretistic register.]

=== Marduk creates plant life for Urash

The surviving narrative content of *Uraš and Marduk*, in the standard scholarly summary that follows Lambert's reading, is best stated as: Marduk creates plant life for Urash. The text articulates a cosmogonic-agricultural relationship between the Babylonian patron god (Marduk) and the agricultural tutelary of Dilbat (Urash), in which Marduk's creative act produces the vegetative — the agricultural — domain over which Urash presides.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (the surviving narrative content; Marduk's creative acts and Urash's role); standard secondary summaries follow Lambert's reading on the basic narrative shape.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315

The cosmogonic register of the composition is therefore restricted to the agricultural and vegetative domain. *Uraš and Marduk* is not, in any sense, the broad cosmic-architectural narrative of *Enūma Eliš*, where Marduk creates the sky from Tiamat's body, the earth from her lower half, and assembles the structures of heaven and underworld in turn. It is a smaller-scale creation narrative — focused on plants and agriculture — and it fits within a broader pattern of Akkadian compositions that articulate Marduk's creation of specific aspects of the lived world.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (the agricultural-cosmogonic register); for the broader Marduk-creation pattern see Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš*) and Foster 2005, pp. 487 ff. (the "Myths of Origins" sub-section, including "Marduk, Creator of the World", "The Myth of the Plow", "Creation of Humankind", "Providing for the Gods").]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 487 ff. ("Myths of Origins")

The surviving lines do not preserve a complete sequence of the creation acts. The manuscript damage is extensive enough that the precise theological articulation — what plants are created, in what order, for what specific cultic or ritual purpose — is partly recoverable only through Lambert's editorial restorations. The narrative outline (Marduk creating plant life for Urash) is sustained by the preserved lines; the details are not.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (manuscript-condition limitations on the narrative recovery); Lambert 2013, p. 496 (notes on the text and the philological apparatus).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315 + p. 496

[INFERENCE: *Uraš and Marduk* is best read as a node in the late-Babylonian dossier of Marduk-creation narratives — not the canonical large-scale cosmogony of *Enūma Eliš* but the cluster of smaller Marduk-creation compositions that articulate his creation of specific aspects of the world (vegetation, agriculture, humans, the king, the plow, the calendar). Foster 2005's "Myths of Origins" sub-section in *Before the Muses* collects several of these smaller cosmogonies, and *Uraš and Marduk* belongs structurally with them, even though it is not in Foster 2005's anthology. | basis: Foster 2005 pp. 487 ff. ("Myths of Origins") collects "Marduk, Creator of the World" (p. 487), "The Myth of the Plow" (p. 489), "Creation of Humankind" (p. 491), "Providing for the Gods", "Creation of Sun and Moon", "Creation of the King" — all small-scale Marduk-creation narratives parallel in structure to *Uraš and Marduk*'s creation of plant life for Urash; Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 frames *Uraš and Marduk* within the late-Babylonian cosmogonic-agricultural dossier; Bottéro 2001 on Marduk's role as cosmogonic creator across multiple compositions. | risk: the structural reading depends on the surviving lines preserving enough of the narrative to confirm a creation-of-plant-life shape; the manuscript damage makes the structural attribution interpretive rather than textually proof-positive. Foster 2005's anthology omits *Uraš and Marduk*, and the structural-grouping with Foster 2005's Myths-of-Origins sub-section is a present-day editorial judgement, not an ancient grouping.]

=== Marduk's syncretism with local cult-figures

The composition's theological move — Marduk creating plant life for the agricultural tutelary of Dilbat — sits within the broader late-Babylonian theological pattern of Marduk's syncretism with local and functional cult-figures. *Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII gives Marduk fifty names, each identifying him with a function or with another deity; the *Marduk Ordeal* (Livingstone 1989, SAA 3 nos. 34–35) develops the syncretism further within the cultic-narrative register; and the late-Babylonian commentary tradition equates Marduk with deities across the pantheon under various functional epithets — "Marduk of planting", "Marduk of healing", and so on.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII fifty-names sequence and commentary; the "Marduk of planting" equation is articulated at pp. 264–265); Livingstone 1989 (*Court Poetry and Literary Miscellanea*, SAA 3 nos. 34–35, the Assur and Nineveh Versions of the *Marduk Ordeal*); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on Marduk's syncretism in the Babylonian pantheon.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277
// EVIDENCE: source_id=livingstone-1989 ; loc=Marduk Ordeal and related compositions
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Marduk's syncretism

The Urash–Marduk pairing in this composition is intelligible against this syncretistic background. Urash, in late-Babylonian commentary, is "Marduk of planting" — a local-cult instantiation of Marduk's agricultural-cosmogonic aspect. *Uraš and Marduk* gives narrative form to that equation: Marduk, the cosmic creator, creating plant life for Urash, his Dilbat agricultural-cult instantiation. The act articulates both Marduk's cosmogonic sovereignty over the agricultural domain and his theological identification with the Dilbat cult.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (commentary on the Urash-Marduk relationship), with the underlying "Marduk of planting" equation articulated in the *Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII fifty-names commentary at Lambert 2013 pp. 264–265; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Urash (the male Dilbat tutelary, agricultural character, syncretism with Marduk); Bottéro 2001 on Marduk's syncretism with regional deities.]

=== Place in the late-Babylonian cosmogonic dossier

*Uraš and Marduk* belongs to the cluster of compositions Lambert 2013 grouped under the heading "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" (Part III, pp. 281–401). The dossier includes *Enūma Eliš* (Chapter 1), *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6), *The Toil of Babylon* (Chapter 7), *Damkina's Bond* (Chapter 9, the next chapter in Lambert's TOC at pp. 321–325), *The Slaying of Labbu* (Chapter 5), *The Founding of Eridu* (Chapter 10), *The First Brick* (Chapter 12), *The Theogony of Dunnu* (Chapter 4), and additional shorter compositions and incantation prologues.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401 (full Part III "Further Babylonian Creation Tales", with the table of contents on the front matter pages); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on the late-Babylonian milieu

The composition is marginal in attestation. It survives only on the single tablet UET 6, 398, with no parallel manuscripts in any period or dialect, and no liturgical or first-millennium recensional footprint outside this single witness. There is no evidence that *Uraš and Marduk* was ever a widely-recited composition or part of any festival cycle.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (single-tablet attestation; absence of liturgical footprint); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

[INFERENCE: *Uraš and Marduk* is best understood not as a freestanding myth that demands narrative retelling on its own terms, but as a fragmentary witness to Marduk's cosmogonic-agricultural register and to the late-Babylonian theological project of articulating Marduk's relationships with local and functional cult-figures. Its preservation reflects scholarly interest — the temple-scholarly apparatus that preserved the wider Marduk dossier saved this composition as material for the larger theological project, not because it was a popular or liturgical text. | basis: the composition's marginal attestation, single-tablet preservation, and absence of liturgical footprint together indicate a scholarly rather than popular preservation profile (Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315; Foster 2007 on the late-Babylonian temple-scholarly milieu); the dossier as a whole exhibits this profile (Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401). | risk: the composition's date is uncertain — possibly Old Babylonian or Kassite rather than late-Babylonian, with origin at Dilbat rather than at the Babylonian temple-scholarly centres. If the composition is OB or Kassite, the late-Babylonian-temple-scholarly preservation framing applies only to the surviving tablet's transmission context, not to the composition's original setting; the chapter's framing is therefore consistent with both possibilities (composed early, preserved late) and does not require the composition itself to be late-Babylonian.]

=== Variants and witness

[VARIANT: primary=Lambert 2013's reading of the single tablet UET 6, 398 (from Ur) | alt=alternative restorations of breaks within the single witness, particularly in passages where the manuscript damage allows multiple philological readings | chosen: Lambert 2013's preferred reading | reason: the composition is preserved on a single tablet with no parallel witnesses; what survives is one philologically-restored translation of one damaged manuscript. *Uraš and Marduk* has no parallel manuscripts. It is preserved on the single tablet UET 6, 398 from Ur. There are no Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Kassite, or Neo-Assyrian witnesses to the composition (other than UET 6, 398 itself, whose own date is uncertain). The textual history is confined to this one tablet.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (single-tablet transmission); Foster 2007, on the limited recensional footprint of compositions in Lambert 2013's Part III dossier.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Uraš and Marduk* is marginal individually but significant within the late-Babylonian Marduk dossier as evidence that the late-Babylonian theological imagination extended Marduk's cosmogonic creativity into the agricultural and vegetative register, by way of a syncretistic relationship with the Dilbat tutelary Urash. It complements the canonical creation-narratives — *Enūma Eliš*'s cosmic architecture, *Atra-ḫasīs*'s human creation — by adding a small-scale agricultural-creation narrative to the dossier's theological range.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (the composition's significance within the dossier); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian theological imagination.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 311-315
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

The composition's narrative obscurity outside specialist scholarship reflects both its fragmentary preservation and its tonal distance from the larger creation narratives. It survives because the late-Babylonian temple-scholarly apparatus preserved it as material for the wider Marduk dossier, not because it was widely read or recited. Its existence is the product of the dossier's editorial logic: a city's priestly scholars kept the small agricultural-cosmogony alongside the great cosmogonic epic, and a single copyist — at Ur, on a single damaged tablet — transmitted it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 311–315 (transmission profile); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian scribal milieu.]
