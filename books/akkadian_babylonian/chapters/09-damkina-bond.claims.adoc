=== Source overview

*Damkina's Bond* is a short, partly fragmentary Akkadian composition that narrates a divine conflict between the pantheon of Marduk (Babylon) and the pantheon of Enlil (Nippur), resolved through Damkina's intervention. The composition is preserved on two manuscripts: a Neo-Assyrian tablet, A 7882 (Oriental Institute, University of Chicago), and a Neo-Babylonian tablet, BM 27776 (British Museum).footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 321–325 (text, translation, and commentary) and p. 497 (notes on the text); Oshima 2010, "Damkina shall not bring back her Burden in the future: A new Mythological Text of Marduk, Enlil, and Damkianna" (an independent critical edition; venue verification pending; identifies the two manuscripts as A 7882 NA and BM 27776 NB).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 + p. 497

The composition has a preserved title or designation — modern scholarship uses Lambert's title *Damkina's Bond*, after the *il-let-sa* (variously read "bond" or "burden") that the goddess releases in the surviving narrative.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 321 (modern title and composition overview); Oshima 2010 (alternative reading and translation as "Damkina shall not bring back her Burden in the future").]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 321

Lambert 2013 pp. 321–325 (with notes on the text at p. 497) is the standard modern critical edition. Takayoshi Oshima's 2010 publication on academia.edu provides an independent critical edition with substantive disagreement on the *il-let-sa* term — Lambert reads it as "bond" (something displayable, perhaps a clay tablet); Oshima reads it as "burden" (a potentially catastrophic plague or natural / celestial disaster). Both editions edit the same two manuscripts; the disagreement is philological, not exemplar-based.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the principal critical edition for this chapter); Oshima 2010 (the alternative reading, especially the il-let-sa interpretation).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

There is no widely-used English-language anthology translation: neither Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., 2005) nor Dalley's *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., 2008) includes *Damkina's Bond*. Lambert 2013 and Oshima 2010 are the only modern critical editions.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the sole modern critical edition in book form); Foster 2005 and Dalley 2008 (do not contain this composition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=full table of contents
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=full table of contents

The two manuscripts overlap partially. Per Oshima 2010, MS B (BM 27776, Neo-Babylonian) is generally to be preferred over MS A (A 7882, Neo-Assyrian) on philological grounds — the Assyrian copy contains scribal errors. Lambert 2013 collates both manuscripts into a composite text. Substantial portions of the narrative are damaged in both witnesses; the contour of the narrative is recoverable from the composite, but specific actions, divine speech, and the resolution's theological implications are partly lost.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (manuscript condition and composite-text apparatus); Oshima 2010 (the MS B preference and scribal-error discussion).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

[LACUNA: Two manuscripts — A 7882 (Neo-Assyrian, Oriental Institute Chicago) and BM 27776 (Neo-Babylonian, British Museum) — both partly damaged. The composite text Lambert 2013 reconstructs from the two witnesses preserves the narrative contour (Marduk's arrival at Nippur, the Eshumesha gods' capture of Anunnaki, Neretagmil's message to Damkina, Damkina's il-let-sa release, Marduk's capture of 360 gods of Eshumesha including Enlil) but loses specific actions, divine speech, and the closing theological implications in the breaks. | source: Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (manuscript score and apparatus); Oshima 2010 (independent edition with MS B preference) | scholarly_reconstruction: limited — the two manuscripts overlap partially and Lambert / Oshima collate them; no parallel composition in a different period or dialect supplies external textual fill, and the Akkadian Marduk-vs-Enlil conflict-narrative tradition is not otherwise attested in this specific shape.]

[RECONSTRUCTION: Lambert 2013's continuous translation joins overlapping passages from A 7882 and BM 27776; where neither manuscript preserves a passage, the translation is dotted or marked as broken. === Setting: Marduk-Enlil rivalry and the secret of Marduk

The composition belongs to the late-Babylonian / first-millennium theological dossier articulating Marduk's sovereignty over the older Mesopotamian pantheon. Marduk's takeover of cosmic offices is the central theological move of *Enūma Eliš*; *Damkina's Bond* extends that takeover into the specifically theological-political register of subordinating Enlil's establishment at Nippur — the elder pantheon's traditional cult-centre.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the Marduk-Enlil rivalry framing); Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš*'s Marduk-elevation theology, with Marduk receiving cosmic offices in Tablets IV–V); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on Marduk-Enlil rivalry and Babylon-vs-Nippur theological politics.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Marduk-Enlil rivalry

The colophon of the Neo-Babylonian manuscript (BM 27776) describes the composition's contents as a secret of Marduk — a *pirištu* — and warns that revealing them to anyone outside the intended audience of ancient scholars was a taboo of Marduk. Future generations are warned not to repeat the transgressions the narrative recounts.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the BM 27776 colophon and its secret-of-Marduk language); Oshima 2010 (the colophon's reading).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (BM 27776 colophon)

The dating of the composition is contested between the editors. Lambert 2013 dates it to the Kassite or Isin II dynasty — mid-second millennium BCE — on the basis that Marduk is already presented as king of the gods (a post-*Enūma Eliš* theological state) but Nabu is still his scribe rather than his son (a pre-late-Babylonian theological state, before Nabu's elevation). Oshima 2010 dates it to "the first quarter of the first millennium" (i.e., c. 1000–750 BCE), making it later than Lambert's proposal but still squarely within the Marduk-king theological era.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the Kassite / Isin II dating argument, on the Marduk-king + Nabu-as-scribe configuration); Oshima 2010 (the early-first-millennium dating).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (composition date)

Lambert 2013 proposes Malgium — an ancient Babylonian city — as the composition's place of origin. Oshima 2010 does not press a specific place of composition. The Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian transmission contexts (the two surviving copies) are scholarly archives — the Oriental Institute Chicago (A 7882) and the British Museum (BM 27776) — that received the tablets through the late-Mesopotamian temple and palace scribal networks.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Malgium origin proposal and transmission); Foster 2007, on the late-period Akkadian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (origin and transmission)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

=== Narrative: Marduk arrives at Nippur

The composition opens with Marduk's arrival at Nippur. The surviving lines preserve a flooding or inundation motif — Marduk comes with his army and the city is overrun. The action is a divine-military invasion of the elder pantheon's cult-centre by the new cosmic king's pantheon.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Marduk's arrival at Nippur and the flooding / inundation motif); Oshima 2010 (the same opening sequence in the alternative edition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

The gods of Eshumesha — Ninurta's temple at Nippur, the constellation of deities associated with Ninurta's cult and the Enlil-pantheon — capture Anunnaki deities in the early stages of the conflict. The Enlil-side initially gains ground; Marduk's forces are not yet decisive.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Eshumesha capture of Anunnaki); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Eshumesha (Ninurta's temple at Nippur); RlA s.v. Eshumesha.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (Eshumesha capture)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Eshumesha
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Eshumesha

[INFERENCE: The "Anunnaki" captured by the Eshumesha gods at this point in the narrative are the Marduk-side deities of the Babylon pantheon, not the broader generic "Anunnaki" of standard Mesopotamian theology. The composition's logic — Marduk attacks Nippur, Enlil's gods initially capture some of Marduk's, Damkina intervenes, Marduk reverses the situation and captures the Enlil-side gods — requires that the captured gods at this stage be on Marduk's side. | basis: the narrative shape preserved across the two manuscripts (Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325) requires this reading; the late-Babylonian theological dossier sometimes uses "Anunnaki" in a more restricted sense for a specific divine constellation rather than for all the gods (Black & Green 1992, s.v. Anunnaki). | risk: the Akkadian usage of "Anunnaki" is variable and the surviving lines do not unambiguously identify the captured gods; this is the most plausible reading but rests on the narrative-shape requirement, not on a textually unambiguous identification.]

=== Neretagmil's message to Damkina

A messenger figure intervenes. Neretagmil — the *sukkal* (vizier or messenger-god) of the god Nāru (the deified river) — delivers a message to Damkianna (the older Sumerian-style form of the name; Akkadian Damkina) about the battle. The message prompts Damkina's intervention.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Neretagmil and the message to Damkina); RlA s.v. Damgalnuna and Damkina (the Damkianna / Damkina equivalence).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (Neretagmil's message)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Damgalnuna / Damkina

Damkina is Marduk's mother — the consort of Ea, the wise god of fresh waters, and the mother of Marduk in the Babylonian pantheon. Her position in the narrative is structurally significant: she is on Marduk's side by maternal-genealogical tie, but she also occupies a senior generation in the divine family, and her intervention has the authority of a senior god, not the merely affective authority of a partisan mother.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. Damkina (consort of Ea, mother of Marduk); RlA s.v. Damkina; Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Damkina's structural position in the narrative).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Damkina
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Damkina
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

=== Damkina releases the il-let-sa

Damkina releases an object the surviving Akkadian text refers to as her *il-let-sa*. The act precipitates the conflict's resolution: after the release, Marduk captures the gods of Eshumesha. But the nature of the *il-let-sa* is contested between the two modern editors.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the il-let-sa release in the surviving lines); Oshima 2010 (the il-let-sa term and its alternative reading).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (il-let-sa)

[VARIANT: primary=Lambert 2013's reading "bond" (something displayable, perhaps a clay tablet); the il-let-sa is on Lambert's reading a positive theological gesture — a binding / pact / token that ends the conflict on Marduk's terms | alt=Oshima 2010's reading "burden" (a potentially catastrophic plague or natural / celestial disaster); on Oshima's reading the il-let-sa release is more ominous — releasing a catastrophe that compels the resolution | chosen: Lambert's "bond" reading, per the brief's locked source (Lambert 2013) and the chapter title *Damkina's Bond* | reason: the chapter follows Lambert 2013 as the locked source-anchor; Oshima's alternative reading is acknowledged in the body and in the comparative-hooks but is not the primary framing. The disagreement is philological — both readings are scholarly proposals on the same Akkadian word — and load-bearing for the composition's interpretation, so the alternative is preserved here rather than collapsed silently into the chosen reading.]

[INFERENCE: Whether the *il-let-sa* is read as "bond" (Lambert) or as "burden" (Oshima), the moment functions as the narrative's hinge: Damkina's release of the object determines the conflict's outcome, and Marduk's capture of the Eshumesha gods immediately follows. The two readings give different theological registers — Lambert's is the binding-of-the-cosmos / pact register; Oshima's is the catastrophe-precipitation register — but both place Damkina's intervention as causally decisive. | basis: the narrative shape across the two manuscripts (Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325; Oshima 2010) places the il-let-sa release immediately before the capture of the Eshumesha gods, with the release as the proximate cause of the resolution; both editors agree on the structural placement, even as they disagree on the term's meaning. | risk: the philological disagreement over il-let-sa is unresolved in the secondary literature, and the chapter's framing of the moment as "hinge of the narrative" rests on the structural placement, not on a settled reading of the term itself. Future philological work may settle the il-let-sa question and would then determine which theological register the chapter foregrounds.]

=== Marduk captures the 360 gods of Eshumesha

After Damkina's intervention, Marduk captures the 360 gods of Eshumesha all together. Among them is Enlil — the Mesopotamian high-god, the head of the elder pantheon. The narrative thus articulates Marduk's theological-political sovereignty over the older Mesopotamian divine order: he subordinates not just the elder pantheon's gods individually, but the entire Eshumesha establishment as a unit, including Enlil himself.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (Marduk's capture of the 360 Eshumesha gods, with Enlil among them); Oshima 2010 (the same scene); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Enlil (the high-god of the elder Mesopotamian pantheon).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (capture of 360 gods including Enlil)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Enlil

The number 360 is theologically and astronomically loaded. It corresponds to the canonical count of days in the Mesopotamian schematic year and to the count of stars in the standard Mesopotamian sky-list (the *Three Stars Each* tradition, *MUL.APIN*-related compendia). The 360 gods of Eshumesha are not merely a round number; they are a fully indexed divine constellation, and Marduk's capture of all of them at once is a totalising theological gesture.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. number symbolism (the 360-day year and the 360-fold divine constellation); Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (the 360-god capture as totalising theological gesture); Bottéro 2001, on Mesopotamian numerical-cosmological symbolism.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. number symbolism
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325 (the 360-god capture)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Mesopotamian numerical-cosmological symbolism

[INFERENCE: *Damkina's Bond* articulates Marduk's theological-political sovereignty over Enlil's establishment at Nippur in a way that is structurally parallel to *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6, Lambert 2013 pp. 281–298) — also a Marduk-subordinates-elder-divine-order narrative ending in capture, judgement, and the redistribution of cosmic offices. The two compositions form a sub-cluster within the late-Babylonian Marduk dossier articulating Marduk's specifically political-theological sovereignty. *Damkina's Bond* differs from *Enmešarra's Defeat* in foregrounding a maternal-feminine intervention (Damkina's il-let-sa release) as causally decisive, where *Enmešarra's Defeat* foregrounds Marduk's direct judgement and the warden-god Nergal's enforcement. | basis: the two compositions sit adjacent in Lambert 2013's Part III dossier (pp. 281–298 *Enmešarra's Defeat*; pp. 321–325 *Damkina's Bond*); both narrate Marduk's subordination of an elder divine order; both end with capture and political-theological redistribution. The structural pairing is articulated in the late-Babylonian theological imagination via the Marduk-elevation logic of *Enūma Eliš* (Lambert 2013 pp. 1–277, esp. Tablets IV–V on the conferring of cosmic offices). | risk: the structural reading is interpretive — the surviving texts do not present themselves programmatically as a paired sub-cluster — but the structural parallel is robust enough that the two compositions are routinely treated together in the secondary literature on the Marduk dossier (Bottéro 2001 on Marduk-Enlil rivalry; Foster 2007 on the late-Babylonian compositional milieu).]

=== Place in the late-Babylonian / first-millennium Marduk dossier

*Damkina's Bond* belongs to the cluster of compositions Lambert 2013 grouped under the heading "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" (Part III, pp. 281–401). The dossier includes *Enūma Eliš* (Chapter 1), *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6), *The Toil of Babylon* (Chapter 7), *Uraš and Marduk* (Chapter 8), *The Slaying of Labbu* (Chapter 5), *The Founding of Eridu* (Chapter 10), *The First Brick* (Chapter 12), *The Theogony of Dunnu* (Chapter 4), and additional shorter compositions and incantation prologues.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401 (full Part III "Further Babylonian Creation Tales"); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian / first-millennium compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on the late-Babylonian milieu

The composition's closest internal parallel is *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6) — a Marduk-subordinates-elder-divine-order narrative with binding / capture / judgement elements. The two compositions form a Marduk-vs-elder-pantheon sub-cluster within the dossier. The earlier framing of *Damkina's Bond* as paired with *Uraš and Marduk* under a "feminine consortship sub-cluster" rubric depended on a mis-identification of Urash as the female cosmogonic Uraš (corrected at the chapter-8 stage) and is dropped here.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 281–298 (*Enmešarra's Defeat*) and pp. 321–325 (*Damkina's Bond*); the structural pairing follows from the two compositions' shared Marduk-subordinates-elder-pantheon narrative pattern.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-298 and pp. 321-325

The composition is marginal in attestation — two manuscripts, no liturgical or first-millennium recensional footprint outside the cosmogonic dossier itself, and the Neo-Babylonian colophon's secret-of-Marduk classification suggests a restricted scholarly transmission rather than a popular liturgical use. There is no evidence that *Damkina's Bond* was ever a widely-recited composition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (limited attestation; secret-of-Marduk colophon); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian temple-scholarly milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

=== Variants and witness

*Damkina's Bond* has two parallel witnesses — A 7882 (Neo-Assyrian) and BM 27776 (Neo-Babylonian) — that overlap partially. Lambert 2013 collates them into a composite text; Oshima 2010 publishes an independent edition. The two manuscripts are not co-equal recensions in the sense of significantly divergent traditions; they are two witnesses to one text with sub-manuscript variation and scribal errors (especially in MS A). The Lambert / Oshima editorial disagreement on the *il-let-sa* term is a philological judgement on a single word's semantic range, not a recension difference.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (two-manuscript transmission; composite text); Oshima 2010 (alternative edition; MS B preference; il-let-sa reading).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 321-325

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Damkina's Bond* is marginal individually but significant within the late-Babylonian / first-millennium Marduk dossier as evidence that the Marduk-Enlil rivalry was articulated narratively as well as theogonically. *Enūma Eliš* establishes Marduk's cosmic kingship through combat with Tiamat and the conferral of cosmic offices on Marduk in Tablet V; *Damkina's Bond* extends that kingship into the specifically political-theological register of subordinating the Enlil establishment at Nippur — capturing 360 gods of Eshumesha, Enlil included, with Damkina's intervention as the catalyst.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš* Marduk-elevation theology) and pp. 321–325 (*Damkina's Bond* Marduk-vs-Enlil narrative); Bottéro 2001, on Marduk-Enlil rivalry in the Babylonian theological imagination.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277 and pp. 321-325
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Marduk-Enlil rivalry

The composition's narrative obscurity outside specialist scholarship reflects both its fragmentary preservation and its restricted-secret transmission profile. It survives because two scribal apparatuses — a Neo-Assyrian one (A 7882) and a Neo-Babylonian one (BM 27776) — preserved it as material for the Marduk-theological dossier, with the Neo-Babylonian colophon explicitly classing the text as a secret of Marduk.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 321–325 (transmission profile; secret-of-Marduk colophon); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian scribal milieu.]
