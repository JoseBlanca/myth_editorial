== Introduction: The Akkadian-Speaking World

In December 1872, a young curator named George Smith stood up at a meeting of the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London and announced that he had found, on a clay tablet from a 7th-century-BCE Assyrian library, a flood story that closely resembled the one in Genesis. The tablet was Tablet XI of what is now called the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš, recovered from the ruins of Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh and shipped to the British Museum in the 1850s. Smith's announcement is conventionally taken as the beginning of modern Akkadian studies. It also foreshadowed the first persistent question that the Akkadian-speaking literary tradition would pose to Europeans: how should one read a literature that is at once unmistakably foreign and disconcertingly familiar?footnote:[George, A.R., _The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: Introduction, Critical Edition and Cuneiform Texts_, 2 vols. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003), vol. 1, pp. ix-xxiii.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=vol. 1, pp. ix-xxiii

The literature in question spans roughly two thousand years. The earliest Akkadian-language texts that bear on this book are the royal inscriptions of Sargon and Narām-Sîn from the Old Akkadian period, around 2300 BCE; the latest are tablets copied in Hellenistic Babylon and Uruk down to the first century BCE, after Alexander, after the Seleucids, when the cuneiform script and the literature it transmitted were already archaic curiosities for a small priestly class. In between comes the Old Babylonian period (roughly 1900-1600 BCE), in which most of the major compositions of the corpus first take shape: the *Atra-ḫasīs* epic of creation and flood, the Old Babylonian forerunners of *Gilgameš*, the earliest *Anzû* and *Adapa* fragments, the disputation poem of the Tamarisk and the Date Palm, and the founding texts of the legend cycle around Sargon and Narām-Sîn.footnote:[Foster, B.R., _Before the Muses: An Anthology of Akkadian Literature_, 3rd ed. (Bethesda, MD: CDL Press, 2005), pp. 1-46 (general introduction).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 1-46

Then comes the long middle: the Middle Babylonian and Middle Assyrian periods (roughly 1600-1000 BCE), in which Akkadian becomes the international lingua franca of the entire ancient Near East. Hittite kings in Anatolia, Egyptian pharaohs in Akhetaten, Hurrian princes in Mitanni, and Canaanite mayors in Jerusalem and Byblos all corresponded with each other in Akkadian. The literary traffic that travelled with this diplomatic Akkadian is one of the distinctive features of the tradition: an Old Babylonian Sargon legend, the *King of Battle*, was preserved in an Egyptian royal archive at Amarna; the wisdom composition *Šimâ milka* was copied at Hattusa in central Anatolia, at Ugarit on the Levantine coast, and at Emar on the upper Euphrates. There is no comparable spread for Sumerian literature, and the cosmopolitanism of the Akkadian corpus is not incidental — it is part of the literature's character.footnote:[Cohen, Y., _Wisdom from the Late Bronze Age_, Writings from the Ancient World 29 (Atlanta: Society of Biblical Literature, 2013), pp. 81-176.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=cohen-2013 ; loc=pp. 81-176

The third period, the first millennium BCE, is the period of Standard Babylonian — a deliberately archaising literary register that was the prestige medium of high Babylonian and Assyrian culture from about 1100 BCE to the end of cuneiform writing.footnote:[Foster, B.R., _Akkadian Literature of the Late Period_, Guides to the Mesopotamian Textual Record 2 (Münster: Ugarit-Verlag, 2007), pp. 1-15.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 1-15
This is the period of the great library of Aššurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE), of the canonical recensions of *Enūma Eliš* and *Gilgameš*, of *Erra and Išum* and the *Babylonian Theodicy*, of the Sultantepe tablets that preserve *The Poor Man of Nippur* and the long Standard Babylonian recension of *Nergal and Ereškigal*. The bulk of what we have is preserved in copies from this period, even when the underlying composition is centuries older. When a modern reader encounters the *Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš*, they are reading a 7th-century scribe's copy of an 11th-century compilation by a scholar named Sîn-lēqi-unninni, who was himself working from Old Babylonian forerunners that had been circulating for half a millennium.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, pp. 28-39 (recensional history).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=vol. 1, pp. 28-39

The corpus is organised in this book into seven genre groups: cosmogony, divine conflict and underworld, heroic epic, royal legend, wisdom (with a subdivision between reflective dialogue and practical instruction), disputations and animal fables, and visionary and popular narrative. Forty-one compositions are treated. Some, like *Enūma Eliš* and *Gilgameš*, are central by every measure — extensively copied, embedded in ritual, cited in royal inscriptions. Others, like *The Worm and the Toothache* (a single etiological cosmogony preserved in a tooth-pain incantation) or *The Fable of the Willow*, are short and marginal. Both kinds belong here: the Akkadian tradition transmitted cultural knowledge in many forms beyond cosmogony and heroic epic, and a book that treats only the famous texts loses sight of how the tradition actually worked.

The scholarly editions on which this book rests are largely the work of the second half of the twentieth century. Wilfred Lambert produced both *Babylonian Wisdom Literature* (1960), the standard critical edition of the wisdom and disputation corpus, and *Babylonian Creation Myths* (2013), the standard edition of the entire creation dossier from *Enūma Eliš* down to the smallest etiological prologue.footnote:[Lambert, W.G., _Babylonian Wisdom Literature_ (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1960; repr. Eisenbrauns 1996); Lambert, W.G., _Babylonian Creation Myths_, Mesopotamian Civilizations 16 (Winona Lake, IN: Eisenbrauns, 2013).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-1960 ; loc=full volume
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=full volume
Wilfred Lambert and Alan Millard's *Atra-Ḫasīs* (1969) remains the reference text for the creation-and-flood epic.footnote:[Lambert, W.G., and Millard, A.R., _Atra-Ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood_ (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1969; repr. Eisenbrauns 1999).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=full volume
Andrew George's two-volume *Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic* (2003) is the definitive critical edition of the entire Akkadian Gilgameš tradition, from the Old Babylonian forerunners through the Standard Babylonian recension. Joan Goodnick Westenholz's *Legends of the Kings of Akkade* (1997) collects the Sargon and Narām-Sîn legend cycles. Luigi Cagni's *L'epopea di Erra* (1969), Amar Annus's *Standard Babylonian Epic of Anzu* (2001), and Shlomo Izre'el's *Adapa and the South Wind* (2001) are the standard editions of three of the corpus's most important divine-conflict and primaeval narratives. Takayoshi Oshima's *Babylonian Poems of Pious Sufferers* (2014) supersedes Lambert 1960 for the textual readings of *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* and the *Babylonian Theodicy*. The English-language entry point for the entire tradition is Benjamin Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., 2005), supplemented by Stephanie Dalley's accessible *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., 2008).footnote:[Foster 2005; Dalley, S., _Myths from Mesopotamia: Creation, the Flood, Gilgamesh, and Others_, rev. ed., Oxford World's Classics (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2008).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=full anthology
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=full anthology

A word on what this book is not. It is not a book of Sumerian mythology. The Sumerian-language literary tradition — the *me* of Inanna, *Lugal-e* of Ninurta, the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*, the Enmerkar and Lugalbanda cycles — is the subject of a companion volume. That tradition is the deep substrate of much of what follows here: every major Akkadian literary genre has Sumerian antecedents, the Akkadian scribes were trained in Sumerian as a learned language down to the end of cuneiform writing, and the Akkadian literary imagination was shaped by Sumerian models for two thousand years. A dedicated chapter on cultural influences appears later in this book, addressing both the Sumerian substrate and a second important contact — with the Hurrian and Hittite traditions of Anatolia, where Indo-European-speaking elites encountered the Akkadian literary world directly through diplomacy and the scribal apparatus. But the present book retells Akkadian compositions, in their Akkadian recensions, from the Akkadian-language manuscripts. The Sumerian originals are reference points, not donor texts.

What follows is a map of the territory: what each cycle contains, why it mattered to the Akkadian-speakers who copied and transmitted it, and where its themes show up in mythological traditions that the reader may already know.


=== Cosmogony, Creation, and Primordial Order

Twelve compositions in this book treat the creation of the cosmos and the origin of humanity. Three of them — *Enūma Eliš*, *Atra-ḫasīs*, and *Adapa and the South Wind* — are major works of Akkadian literature. Six are shorter creation narratives that survive in late Babylonian copies and form a "creation dossier" around the longer epics. Three are micro-cosmogonies embedded in incantation prologues, the shortest of which (*The Worm and the Toothache*) compresses the creation of heaven and earth, the rivers, the canals, the marshes, and the human tooth-worm into about a dozen lines.

*Enūma Eliš* (Chapter 1) is the central cosmogonic text of the entire Akkadian tradition. It tells how Marduk — head of the Babylonian pantheon, son of Ea and Damkina, slayer of Tiamat in *Enūma Eliš* — rises from the youngest generation of gods to defeat the primordial sea-mother Tiamat (the primordial salt-water mother, defeated by Marduk in *Enūma Eliš* and split to form heaven and earth) in single combat, splits her body to form the cosmos, and is exalted to kingship. The poem closes with the recitation of his fifty names, a hymnic theology of Babylonian sovereignty. The composition is central by every measure: critical editions and modern scholarship are extensive (Lambert 2013 alone runs to 600 pages); manuscripts survive from Babylon, Aššur, and Nineveh; the poem was recited at the *akītu* festival — the spring New Year ritual at Babylon during which *Enūma Eliš* was recited, re-enacting Marduk's victory over Tiamat and re-installing cosmic order for the coming year — and that recitation explicitly re-enacted Marduk's cosmic victory.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3-144 (critical edition); pp. 449-465 (akītu context).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-144 and 449-465
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=akītu festival discussion
The poem is composed in seven tablets and was almost certainly produced in the late second millennium BCE under Kassite or early post-Kassite patronage; its function is at once cosmological (it explains the origin of the world), theogonic (it enthrones Marduk above the older gods), and royal-legitimation (the king of Babylon's authority is grounded in Marduk's cosmic kingship).
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Hurrian Kumarbi succession (Hoffner 1998) — closest structural parallel; Hesiod Theogony's succession-by-combat; Genesis 1's separation of waters; Vedic Indra vs. Vritra; Ugaritic Baal vs. Yamm; Revelation 12's red dragon

*Atra-ḫasīs* (Chapter 2) is the Akkadian creation-and-flood epic, indispensable for any account of how Mesopotamians thought about the origin of humanity. The lower gods rebel against the toil of digging the earth's canals; the mother-goddess Mami (the mother goddess, also *Bēlet-ilī*, "Lady of the gods", who creates humanity in *Atra-ḫasīs*) creates humans to take over the labour. Humanity multiplies, its noise oppresses Enlil (storm and command-god of Nippur, second-ranking god of the cosmos, who decrees fates), and Enlil sends plague, then drought, then famine, and finally a flood. Atra-ḫasīs ('the exceedingly wise' — antediluvian sage who survives the flood by Ea's warning, hero of the *Atra-ḫasīs* epic) is warned by Ea (god of wisdom, the freshwater *Apsû*, and incantation, patron of craft and cunning, father of Marduk) and survives. The Old Babylonian recension preserved on tablets from Sippar (the so-called Ipiq-Aya manuscript, copied during the reign of Ammi-ṣaduqa, around 1635 BCE) is the most complete witness; first-millennium recensions are heavily fragmentary.footnote:[Lambert and Millard 1969, pp. 1-30 (introduction to the OB recension and Ipiq-Aya colophon).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 1-30
The flood narrative was independently incorporated into Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*, the tablet that George Smith first read in 1872. The composition is central — it is the load-bearing theological account of why human beings exist, why they suffer, and why the gods must occasionally chastise them — and its function is cosmogonic, anthropological (the noise-as-overpopulation logic), and ritual-aetiological (the closing list of birth restrictions).
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Genesis 6-9 flood (closest narrative parallel in any tradition); Greek Deucalion; Sumerian Eridu Genesis as comparative substrate

*Adapa and the South Wind* (Chapter 3) is the third of the major cosmogonic compositions. Adapa (antediluvian sage and priest of Ea at Eridu, the *apkallu* who refused immortality), priest of Ea at Eridu (the southernmost city of Mesopotamia, traditionally the first city of the world; seat of Ea's temple the E-abzu and home of Adapa), breaks the wing of the south wind in a fishing accident and is summoned to the heaven of Anu (sky god, formal head of the divine hierarchy, father of the Anunnaki). Warned by Ea against accepting the food and drink offered him there, he refuses what turn out to be the bread and water of immortality — losing eternal life for himself and humankind. Adapa is one of the *apkallū* — the seven antediluvian sages who brought civilization to humanity; Adapa is the first of them — and his story is the Akkadian account of how immortality was within reach and was forfeited.footnote:[Izre'el, S., _Adapa and the South Wind: Language Has the Power of Life and Death_, Mesopotamian Civilizations 10 (Winona Lake, IN: Eisenbrauns, 2001), pp. 5-25.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 5-25
The Akkadian witnesses are the Middle Babylonian Amarna fragment (a copy preserved in an Egyptian royal archive — itself evidence for the international circulation of Akkadian literary texts in the Late Bronze Age) and the Neo-Assyrian Nineveh fragments. There is no Old Babylonian Akkadian *Adapa*; an Old Babylonian Sumerian-language *Adapa* tablet was excavated at Tell Haddad (ancient Me-Turan), but that text belongs to the Sumerian tradition and is reserved for comparative discussion in the influences chapter.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Genesis 2-3 forfeited immortality and the forbidden fruit; Pandora's jar; Greek myths of mortals offered immortality and refused

The remaining cosmogonies are short, fragmentary, or both. Together they constitute a *creation dossier* — a corpus that shows just how varied Babylonian thinking about origins could be. *The Theogony of Dunnu* (Chapter 4) is the strangest of them: a genealogical creation in which successive generations of cosmic deities — Plough/Earth, Sea, Cattle-god — kill, marry, and replace their parents in the city of Dunnu. The text reads like a violent dynastic chronicle of cosmogony, and its parallels to the Hurro-Hittite *Kumarbi Cycle* — Anu's castration, Kumarbi's swallowing of the seed, Teshub's birth from the swallowed seed — are striking enough that scholarly opinion has long debated whether the Akkadian text is a Hurrian-influenced reworking or whether the influence runs the other way.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 387-395.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 387-395
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Hurro-Hittite Kumarbi succession (closest parallel); Hesiod Theogony Kronos-Zeus succession; the Indo-European succession motif

*The Slaying of Labbu* (Chapter 5), *Enmešarra's Defeat* (Chapter 6), *The Toil of Babylon* (Chapter 7), *Uraš and Marduk* (Chapter 8), and *Damkina's Bond* (Chapter 9) are short, late, and fragmentary. None survives in more than a single recension. Each addresses some aspect of the Babylonian creation tradition — *Labbu*, a dragon-combat narrative that may be the earliest surviving Akkadian Chaoskampf; *Enmešarra*, a chthonic-conflict narrative tied to the Akītu liturgical complex; *Toil of Babylon*, the origin of the city itself by divine and human labour; *Uraš and Marduk* and *Damkina's Bond*, theogonic narratives about Marduk's parentage. Lambert 2013 collects all of them in a single volume, and they are best read as a corpus rather than as individual texts: each shows that *Enūma Eliš* was not the last word on Babylonian creation theology, and that older or alternative cosmologies continued to be copied as late as the Hellenistic period.

*The Founding of Eridu* (Chapter 10) and *The First Brick* (Chapter 12) are short bilingual creation prologues that survive embedded in incantation rituals. The *Founding of Eridu*, also known by its Akkadian incipit *enūma Anum ibnû šamê* ("when Anu created the heavens"), narrates the creation of heaven and earth and the establishment of Eridu as the world's first city; *The First Brick* explains the cosmic origin of the act of brickmaking. Both originally functioned as ritual prologues — they were recited at the start of foundation ceremonies — and both circulated as freestanding cosmogonies.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376-386.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-386

*The Worm and the Toothache* (Chapter 11) is the smallest cosmogony in the corpus and one of the most charming. It is preserved on a single tablet from Aššurbanipal's library (CT 17, 50), and it tells how Anu created the heavens, how the heavens created the earth, the earth the rivers, the rivers the canals, the canals the marshes, and the marshes the worm — who, when offered a diet of figs and apples, chose instead to dwell in human teeth. The cosmogony grounds the magical cure for caries that follows. The text's significance is methodological as much as literary: it shows that for an Akkadian-speaker, even a toothache demanded a cosmogonic frame.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995-996; Lambert 2013, pp. 396-398.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 995-996
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: ritual-medical cosmogony genre (compare Egyptian Book of the Dead spell 17, Vedic Atharva-Veda creation hymns)


=== Divine Conflict, Descent, and the Underworld

Four compositions treat the divine conflicts and underworld journeys that structure the Akkadian theological imagination. The cluster is small but central: every text in it is preserved in multiple manuscripts, every one is anchored in named scholarly editions, and three of the four (the *Descent of Ishtar*, *Anzû*, and *Erra and Išum*) are among the most heavily studied compositions in the entire Mesopotamian corpus.

*The Descent of Ishtar* (Chapter 13) — Ištar (goddess of love, war, and the planet Venus; queen of heaven, sister of Ereškigal) descends to the underworld to confront her sister Ereškigal (queen of the netherworld, elder sister of Ištar), removing one item of jewellery or garment at each of seven gates until she stands naked before her sister, who hangs her on a hook. Ea creates a being to rescue her; she returns, with Dumuzi (shepherd-god, consort of Ištar; the dying god whose descent and substitution structure the *Descent of Ishtar*) substituted as her replacement in the underworld. The Akkadian Standard Babylonian recension is preserved on K.162 from Nineveh and its duplicates, and is substantially different from the earlier Sumerian *Inanna's Descent* — shorter, more tightly plotted, less ritually framed. It is not a translation of the Sumerian text and must not be treated as one. Its parallels to Greek Persephone-Demeter and Egyptian Osiris are direct and have been discussed since Frazer.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 498-505; Dalley 2008, pp. 154-162.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 498-505
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Persephone's descent and seasonal return; Osiris death-and-rebirth; the Sumerian Inanna's Descent as comparative substrate (ETCSL 1.4.1)

*Nergal and Ereškigal* (Chapter 14) tells how Nergal (god of war, plague, and the netherworld; consort of Ereškigal; identified with Erra in some contexts), war-god of the upper world, descends to Ereškigal's netherworld and ends as her co-ruler. The poem survives in two co-equal recensions: a brief, etiological Middle Babylonian text from the Amarna archive in Egypt (EA 357) and a long, romantic-courtly Standard Babylonian text from Sultantepe in southern Turkey, with a fragmentary Late Babylonian witness from Uruk.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 506-524.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 506-524
The two recensions are so different in scope and tone that this book treats them side by side rather than collapsing them into a single account; the Amarna witness is itself further evidence for the international circulation of the Akkadian literary corpus in the Late Bronze Age.

*Anzû* (Chapter 15) — Anzû (the divine lion-headed eagle, thief of the Tablet of Destinies), the storm-bird, steals the Tablet of Destinies (the Tablet of Destinies (*ṭuppi šīmāti*) — the cosmic decree-tablet that confers supreme authority on whoever holds it; central artifact in *Enūma Eliš* and *Anzû*) from Enlil. After two heroes refuse the task, the slayer is sent to recover it; following a fierce aerial combat, he slays Anzû. The composition is one of the central Mesopotamian Chaoskampf narratives. Its single most distinctive feature is recensional: in the Old Babylonian *Anzû*, the slayer is Ningirsu, the warrior-god of Lagaš; in the Standard Babylonian recension, the slayer is Ninurta (warrior-son of Enlil, slayer of Anzû, champion of the gods), the warrior-son of Enlil, and the OB→SB substitution is one of the cleanest cases in Mesopotamian literature of a divine name changing across recensions of the same poem.footnote:[Annus, A., _The Standard Babylonian Epic of Anzu_, SAACT 3 (Helsinki: Neo-Assyrian Text Corpus Project, 2001), pp. 1-13; Vogelzang, M.E., _Bin šar dadme: Edition and Analysis of the Akkadian Anzu Poem_ (Groningen: Styx, 1988), pp. 1-30.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=annus-2001 ; loc=pp. 1-13
// EVIDENCE: source_id=vogelzang-1988 ; loc=pp. 1-30
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: the Sumerian Lugal-e (ETCSL 1.6.2) as comparative substrate; the Indo-European HERO SLAY SERPENT formula (Watkins); Vedic Indra vs. Vritra; Zeus vs. Typhon

*Erra and Išum* (Chapter 16) is the most distinctively Babylonian composition in this section. Erra (god of plague and violence, sometimes identified with Nergal; protagonist of the *Erra and Išum* poem), god of plague and violence, is roused by his demons (the Sebitti — Erra's seven demonic warriors, the war-band whose voices rouse him to attack Babylon in *Erra and Išum*) to attack Babylon. While Marduk is induced to leave his throne for ritual cleaning, Erra unleashes catastrophic war upon Mesopotamian cities. His vizier Išum (vizier of Erra and herald of the gods; the calming counterweight to Erra's rage) eventually persuades him to relent; the poem closes with Erra's restoration to good order and a prophecy of future Babylonian victory. The five-tablet poem was composed in the eighth century BCE — the colophon names a scholar called Kabti-ilāni-Marduk as the composer — and it is the rare Akkadian literary work whose date can be fixed with some confidence to a specific historical horizon. The poem itself functioned as a protective amulet: copies were buried under foundations and worn as pendants, on the explicit promise of the closing tablet that its possessors would be safe from plague.footnote:[Cagni, L., _L'epopea di Erra_, Studi Semitici 34 (Rome: Istituto di Studi del Vicino Oriente, 1969), pp. 1-30 (introduction).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=cagni-1969 ; loc=pp. 1-30

=== Heroic and Mythic Epics

Two heroic epics are treated here. The first is the central composition of the entire Akkadian corpus; the second is a shorter but no less Akkadian counterpart. The remaining heroic material in the Akkadian tradition belongs in the next section, on royal legend.

*The Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš* (Chapter 17) — Gilgameš (king of Uruk, two-thirds divine, hero of the Standard Babylonian epic), king of Uruk (the walled city of Gilgameš in the southern Mesopotamian alluvium, seat of Ištar's temple the E-ana), and his wild companion Enkidu (wild man fashioned by the gods, companion of Gilgameš, whose death precipitates the search for immortality) undertake heroic adventures: defeating the giant Ḫumbaba (guardian of the Cedar Forest appointed by Enlil, defeated by Gilgameš and Enkidu in *Gilgameš* IV-V) in the Cedar Forest, slaying the Bull of Heaven, and refusing Ištar's marriage offer. Enkidu's death plunges Gilgameš into a search for immortality, leading him to Uta-napišti ('he found life' — the flood-survivor whom Gilgameš seeks out for the secret of immortality, the Akkadian counterpart of *Atra-ḫasīs*) the flood survivor; the epic closes with his return to Uruk having understood that immortality lies only in the works of one's hands. The Standard Babylonian recension is the work of Sîn-lēqi-unninni, a Middle Babylonian scholar of the late second millennium BCE; it builds on Old Babylonian and Middle Babylonian forerunners (the Pennsylvania tablet, the Yale tablet, the Sippar tablet, the Boğazköy fragments) that George 2003 publishes alongside the SB text.footnote:[George 2003, vol. 1, pp. 28-39 (recensional history) and pp. 159-218 (OB and MB forerunners).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=vol. 1, pp. 28-39 and 159-218
The composition is the central work of Akkadian literature by every measure: extensive critical scholarship, dozens of manuscripts, citation in royal inscriptions, integration with the *Atra-ḫasīs* flood tradition, and a continuous reception history from the second millennium BCE down to George Smith and the modern world.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: the Sumerian Bilgames poems (ETCSL 1.8.1.x) as comparative substrate; Achilles in the Iliad (mortality and the choice of glory); Beowulf; the quest for immortality as a universal mythic motif

*Etana* (Chapter 18) is the second-tier heroic epic. Etana, an early king of Kish granted kingship by the gods but lacking an heir, aids a wounded eagle imprisoned in a pit by the serpent he has betrayed. In gratitude, the eagle carries Etana on its back up to heaven to seek the plant of birth from Anu and Ištar. The composition survives in three substantial recensions — Old Babylonian, Middle Assyrian, and Standard Babylonian — that differ in detail; the ending is lost in all of them.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 533-554.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 533-554
[LACUNA: The endings of all three recensions are damaged; whether Etana succeeded in obtaining the plant of birth, and whether the entire ascent was a dream or a literal journey, cannot be determined from the surviving manuscripts.]

=== Royal Legends and Pseudo-Historical Epics

Eight compositions in this book belong to the genre of royal legend — the *narû* (the *narû* genre — pseudo-autobiographical royal literature in which a king (most often Sargon or Narām-Sîn) addresses a future ruler from the inscribed monument that bears his words) genre that is one of Mesopotamia's distinctive literary inventions. A *narû* is a stele or stone monument bearing a royal inscription, and the *narû*-text genre is its literary cousin: a first-person royal narrative cast in the voice of the king on the monument, reaching across centuries to address the future. The kings most often inhabited by these texts are Sargon (Šarru-kīn ('the king is true') — founder of the Akkadian empire (ca. 2334 BCE), protagonist of the *Birth Legend* and *šar tamḫāri*) of Akkad (Agade — the lost capital city of Sargon's empire, and the name of the kingdom that gave Akkadian its name), Sargon's grandson Narām-Sîn ('beloved of Sîn' — grandson of Sargon, fourth king of Akkad (ca. 2254-2218 BCE), protagonist of the *Cuthaean Legend*, *Great Revolt*, and *Erra and Narām-Sîn*), and the Middle Assyrian king Tukulti-Ninurta I.

*The Birth Legend of Sargon* (Chapter 19) opens with one of the most striking first-person addresses in ancient literature: "I, Sargon, mighty king, king of Akkad..." The story Sargon then tells — of being born to a priestess in concealment, set in a basket of reeds, sealed with bitumen, cast on the river, and rescued by Aqqi the water-drawer who raised him as a gardener — is preserved on Neo-Assyrian library tablets (K.3401 and duplicates) and is firmly Akkadian. Its similarity to the biblical Moses birth narrative (Exodus 2:1-10) is so close that scholarly debate over the direction of borrowing has continued for over a century, and the question is taken up at length in the influences chapter.footnote:[Westenholz, J.G., _Legends of the Kings of Akkade: The Texts_, Mesopotamian Civilizations 7 (Winona Lake, IN: Eisenbrauns, 1997), pp. 36-49.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 36-49
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: the Moses birth narrative (Exodus 2); the universal "exposed-infant-of-noble-birth" motif (Cyrus the Great in Herodotus; Romulus and Remus); Karna in the Mahabharata

*The King of Battle* (Chapter 20) — the šar tamḫāri — narrates Sargon's distant campaign against Nūr-Daggal, ruler of Anatolian Purušḫanda, undertaken at the request of Akkadian merchants who have been mistreated. The text survives in two recensions, one from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh and a second on a tablet from the 14th-century-BCE archive at Amarna in Egypt (EA 359, now Cairo Museum 48396). The Amarna witness is again diagnostic of the international reach of the Akkadian literary corpus: Akkadian was the language of pharaonic correspondence, and the legends of the Akkadian kings travelled with the language.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 102-139.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 102-139

The remaining royal legends are clustered around Narām-Sîn. *The Great Revolt against Narām-Sîn* (Chapter 21) is preserved in four substantially different recensions — an Old Akkadian exercise tablet, two Old Babylonian excerpt tablets, an incomplete OB recension, and a fourth OB excerpt ending with a curse formula of the type found in votive inscriptions. The narrative tells of a coalition of city-rulers led by Iphur-Kiš of Kish and Amar-Girid of Uruk who rise against Narām-Sîn and are defeated. *Narām-Sîn and the Lord of Apišal* (Chapter 22), preserved on a single Neo-Assyrian tablet, is a shorter siege narrative. *Erra and Narām-Sîn* (Chapter 23) is an Old Babylonian composition (BM 120003) distinct from both the first-millennium *Erra and Išum* (different protagonist, different period, different plot) and the *Cuthaean Legend* of the same king.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 173-188 (Apišal); pp. 189-202 (Erra and Narām-Sîn); pp. 221-262 (Great Revolt).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 173-262

*The Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn* (Chapter 24) is the foundational *narû* text. Narām-Sîn, in his own voice, recounts how he rashly waged war against monstrous invaders against the divine warning, suffered catastrophic defeat, and finally learned wisdom; the text closes with an explicit injunction to the future king to consult oracles before war. The composition survives in both Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian recensions, both anchored in the city of Cutha (Kutha — the northern Babylonian city of Nergal, the setting of the *Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn*), Nergal's city in northern Babylonia.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 263-368.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 263-368
*Elegy on the Death of Narām-Sîn* (Chapter 25) closes the OB Narām-Sîn cycle with a lament for the king's death.footnote:[Westenholz 1997, pp. 203-220.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=westenholz-1997 ; loc=pp. 203-220

The eighth royal-legend chapter steps forward by a millennium. *The Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* (Chapter 26) is a Middle Assyrian poem composed shortly after the events it narrates: Tukulti-Ninurta I's campaign against Kashtiliash IV of Babylon, around 1230 BCE. The epic frames the Assyrian invasion as Marduk's deliberate abandonment of Babylon for Aššur (the city on the Tigris that gave its name to the Assyrian kingdom, and the head god of the Assyrian pantheon who shares the name)'s sake; Tukulti-Ninurta is divinely guided to victory and to the carrying-off of Marduk's statue.footnote:[Machinist, P.B., _The Epic of Tukultī-Ninurta I: A Study in Middle Assyrian Literature_, PhD diss., Yale University (1978), pp. 1-50.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=machinist-1978 ; loc=pp. 1-50
The epic is unique among Akkadian literature in being a near-contemporary literary record of a specific war.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: pseudo-autobiographical royal-legend genre as a Mesopotamian innovation; Egyptian Königsnovelle (the Sinuhe and Kamose narratives); biblical Davidic court narratives; Hittite "Apology of Hattušili"


=== Wisdom: Reflective Dialogue and Complaint

Four compositions take up the Akkadian theology of suffering. They are read together because they form the deepest sustained engagement in any ancient Near Eastern literature with the question of why the pious suffer and what, if anything, can be said in answer. The reading order in this book is chronological: the earliest text is also the earliest known Akkadian theodicy, antedating the more famous *Babylonian Theodicy* by some seven hundred years.

*Dialogue between a Man and his God* (Chapter 27) is the foundational Akkadian theodicy. It is preserved on a single Old Babylonian tablet (AO 4462, Louvre), attributed by colophon to the scribe Kalbanum, and dated to the late Old Babylonian period — most likely the reign of Ammi-Ditana (around 1683-1640 BCE). A first-person sufferer complains of unjust affliction; his personal god answers; reconciliation is reached. The structural and rhetorical patterns that *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* and the *Babylonian Theodicy* will develop are already present in compressed form here, seven centuries earlier.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 148.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., p. 148

*Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* (Chapter 28) — "I will praise the lord of wisdom" — is the most famous of the four. A first-person poetic monologue of a sufferer who has lost everything (health, property, social standing) despite his piety, the poem recounts his vivid dreams of three intercessors who lift his afflictions; it concludes with restoration and a hymn of praise to Marduk, the inscrutable "lord of wisdom" of the title. Composition is conventionally dated to the Kassite period (around 1300 BCE); the poem is traditionally attributed to a courtier called Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan, who appears in the text as the sufferer himself.footnote:[Oshima, T., _Babylonian Poems of Pious Sufferers: Ludlul Bēl Nēmeqi and the Babylonian Theodicy_, Orientalische Religionen in der Antike 14 (Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 2014), pp. 1-200.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=oshima-2014 ; loc=pp. 1-200
The four-tablet structure was transmitted continuously through the Standard Babylonian period and into the Hellenistic; the parallels to the biblical book of Job — pious sufferer, three friends, divine speech, restoration — are too close to be accidental and too distant to be direct, and the comparison has been a fixed point of Mesopotamian-biblical scholarship since the late nineteenth century.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: biblical Job (the closest parallel in any tradition); the Sumerian "Man and His God" (ETCSL 5.2.4) as comparative substrate; Egyptian Dispute between a Man and his Ba

*The Babylonian Theodicy* (Chapter 29) is a 27-stanza acrostic dialogue between a sufferer and his wise friend on the problem of why the wicked prosper while the pious suffer. The friend defends the gods and the social order; the sufferer presses ever more pointed objections; the dialogue ends in formal closure rather than resolution. The acrostic spells out the author's name and a prayer formula: "I, Saggil-kīnam-ubbib, the incantation-priest, am a worshipper of god and king." Saggil-kīnam-ubbib is independently attested in colophons of incantation tablets, which is one of the few cases in Akkadian literature in which an author's name can be securely placed in a professional and chronological context.footnote:[Oshima 2014, pp. 201-380.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=oshima-2014 ; loc=pp. 201-380
[INFERENCE: The acrostic is one of the earliest sustained alphabetic acrostics in any literary tradition, and Saggil-kīnam-ubbib's specific identification of himself as an incantation-priest situates the *Theodicy* squarely within the professional scribal apparatus rather than in any popular or folk register.]

*Dialogue of Pessimism* (Chapter 30) — also called *Master and Servant* — is the strangest of the four. The master proposes a series of actions (going to the palace, hosting a banquet, taking a wife, doing a good deed); the servant agrees and supplies a reason; the master then reverses, and the servant supplies an opposite reason. The poem ends with the master's despairing question — what then is good? — and the servant's macabre answer. The composition is conventionally read as a satirical complement to the more solemn theodicy poems, though some scholars have argued for a serious philosophical reading.footnote:[Lambert 1960, pp. 139-149.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-1960 ; loc=pp. 139-149
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Ecclesiastes (Qohelet); Egyptian Dispute between a Man and his Ba; Cynic and Sceptical Greek philosophy; the Akkadian theodicy tradition as the deepest pre-classical engagement with theodicy outside Job


=== Wisdom: Practical Instruction

Three compositions belong to the second wisdom subgenre — practical instruction, the father-to-son transmission of ethical and social knowledge. They are shorter than the dialogic compositions and structured around a long succession of maxims rather than a developed argument. The genre's significance is partly comparative: practical instruction is one of the great pan-Near-Eastern literary genres, and the Akkadian texts are the link between Sumerian disputation and Egyptian *Instruction* literature on one side and biblical Proverbs and Aramaic *Aḥiqar* on the other.

*Counsels of Wisdom* (Chapter 31) and *Counsels of a Pessimist* (Chapter 32) are first-millennium Akkadian compositions preserved on Neo-Assyrian library tablets. *Counsels of Wisdom* is the longer of the two, comprising about 160 lines of practical and ethical advice on speech and silence, the choice of friends and wife, the treatment of the poor, business honesty, and conduct before officials. *Counsels of a Pessimist* is a shorter fragmentary composition emphasising the transience of life and the futility of striving — its title reflects its rhetorical kinship to the *Dialogue of Pessimism*, not a textual relationship.footnote:[Lambert 1960, pp. 96-107 (Counsels of Wisdom); pp. 107-109 (Counsels of a Pessimist).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-1960 ; loc=pp. 96-109

*Šimâ milka* (Chapter 33) — "Hear the counsel" — is preserved at three peripheral Late Bronze Age sites: Emar (on the upper Euphrates), Ugarit (on the Levantine coast), and Hattusa (in central Anatolia). The sage Šūpê-amēli instructs his son in practical wisdom on travel, friends, women, debts, and the limits of human knowledge; the son responds with a long pessimistic reflection on the futility of all counsel in the face of mortality. The peripheral attestation is itself the point: an Akkadian wisdom composition copied at three foreign capitals, in three different scribal milieus, is direct evidence for the spread of Akkadian as the international literary lingua franca of the Late Bronze Age.footnote:[Cohen 2013, pp. 81-176.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=cohen-2013 ; loc=pp. 81-176
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Egyptian Instructions of Amenemope and Ptahhotep; biblical Proverbs (esp. Prov. 22-24); Aramaic Aḥiqar; the pan-Near-Eastern wisdom genre as evidence of literary contact zones


=== Disputations and Animal Fables

Six compositions belong to the disputation and fable genre. Two personified entities — usually a pair of plants, animals, or trades — argue their relative merits before a divine judge, who delivers a verdict. The genre is distinctively Mesopotamian, and the Akkadian texts collected here continue a Sumerian tradition that produced disputations between the *Hoe and Plough*, *Ewe and Grain*, and *Bird and Fish* (the Sumerian companions to these texts are treated in the companion volume).

*The Tamarisk and the Date Palm* (Chapter 34) is the oldest of the Akkadian disputations, attested in Old Babylonian and Middle Assyrian recensions. The two trees argue before the king as to which is more useful: the tamarisk for its uses in cult, scribal craft, and royal regalia; the date palm for its sustenance, shade, and rope-making fibre. The dispute is staged as a court trial, and the composition is significant in the history of Mesopotamian wisdom literature for being the apparent ancestor of the "trial of trees" motif preserved in the Aramaic *Words of Aḥiqar* and reflected in biblical material such as Jotham's parable in Judges 9.footnote:[Lambert 1960, pp. 151-164.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-1960 ; loc=pp. 151-164
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Aramaic Aḥiqar trial-of-trees; biblical Jotham's parable (Judges 9); the Sumerian disputation poems (ETCSL 5.3.x) as comparative substrate

*The Ox and the Horse* (Chapter 35), *The Series of Nisaba and Wheat* (Chapter 36), and *The Fable of the Willow* (Chapter 37) are shorter and more fragmentary. The *Ox and the Horse* contests the dignity of the labour each animal performs (the ox boasts of the plough and the canal; the horse of the chariot and the chase); *Nisaba and Wheat* sets the grain-goddess against the personified Wheat over agricultural primacy; *The Fable of the Willow* preserves a few fragments of a contest between trees.footnote:[Lambert 1960, pp. 165-185.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-1960 ; loc=pp. 165-185

*The Series of the Fox* (Chapter 38) — *iškar šēlebi* — is the largest fable composition in the Akkadian corpus. The composition is an *iškaru* (the *iškaru* — a canonical multi-tablet literary series, transmitted as a single composition in scribal training; e.g., *iškar šēlebi*, the *Series of the Fox*) — a canonical multi-tablet series transmitted through scribal training as a single composition — and it preserves about 200 lines across multiple tablets. The fox, accused before the gods of crimes by his fellow animals, defends himself with cunning rhetoric, attempting to turn each charge against his accusers.footnote:[Kienast, B., _Iškar šēlebi: Die Serie vom Fuchs_, Freiburger Altorientalische Studien 22 (Stuttgart: Franz Steiner, 2003), pp. 1-260; Lambert 1960, pp. 186-209.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kienast-2003 ; loc=pp. 1-260
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Aesop's animal fables; the medieval Reynard the Fox cycle; the pan-Near-Eastern fable tradition mediated through Aramaic Aḥiqar

*The Fable of the Riding-Donkey* (Chapter 39) is a short brief fable in which a riding donkey complains to its poor master about its lot. It survives in fragments only, and its title in older scholarship sometimes confused it with *The Poor Man of Nippur* — the two compositions are unrelated.footnote:[Lambert 1960, pp. 209-212.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-1960 ; loc=pp. 209-212


=== Visionary and Popular Narratives

The two compositions in this final section step outside the genres surveyed so far. One is a visionary narrative — a first-person otherworld journey set down in court literature. The other is a comic folktale set in the back streets of a Mesopotamian city. Each shows that the Akkadian literary imagination ran broader than divine drama and royal legend.

*The Underworld Vision of an Assyrian Prince* (Chapter 40) is preserved on a single tablet from Aššur (VAT 10057, Vorderasiatisches Museum Berlin). An Assyrian prince called Kummâ — possibly Aššurbanipal in literary disguise, possibly a literary persona — recounts his terrifying dream-journey to the netherworld. He sees the underworld assembly with monstrous demons in attendance; the centrepiece of the vision is a long demonography catalogue, naming and describing fifteen netherworld deities and demons in succession. He is finally returned to life with a divine warning. The vision is framed as a moral and political lesson on royal piety.footnote:[Livingstone, A., _Court Poetry and Literary Miscellanea_, State Archives of Assyria 3 (Helsinki: Helsinki University Press, 1989), no. 32; Foster 2005, pp. 832-839.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=livingstone-1989 ; loc=SAA 3 no. 32
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 832-839
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Greek katabasis (Odysseus in Hades, the Aeneid Book 6); biblical apocalyptic visionary literature; Egyptian Book of the Dead and the underworld journey of the Pharaoh; the late-Hellenistic and early-Christian tour-of-hell genre

*The Poor Man of Nippur* (Chapter 41) is a comic folktale of social inversion preserved on a 7th-century-BCE tablet from Sultantepe (STT 38, with two minor witnesses STT 39 and STT 116). Gimil-Ninurta, a poor citizen of Nippur, exchanges his last goat for an audience with the city governor — only to be cheated by him. Gimil-Ninurta revenges himself three times in absurd, escalating ways: as a ride-mooching nobleman, as a club-wielding stranger, and as a hidden assailant. The triple-revenge structure is the basic engine of comic folktale across many traditions, and *The Poor Man of Nippur* has occasionally been cited as a possible distant ancestor of the *1001 Nights* cycle. The connection is conjectural and the chapter takes the parallel up only as a comparative possibility, not a documented chain.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 357-364; Dalley 2008, pp. 213-222.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=BTM 3rd ed., pp. 357-364
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: triple-revenge folktale structure; the 1001 Nights tradition (suggested but not documented); Aesop and the popular-narrative reception of high literary genres


=== Patterns and the Question of Influence

Step back from the individual texts and the Akkadian literary corpus reveals four sustained concerns. They are not the only ones — wisdom poetry's reflection on the inscrutability of divine justice is its own preoccupation, and the cosmogonic dossier is its own kind of theological laboratory — but they are the four that recur across cycles, that command the most manuscripts, and that the scribal apparatus seems to have prioritised in transmission.

First, *the establishment and maintenance of cosmic order*. From *Enūma Eliš* through the creation dossier, *Anzû*, *Erra and Išum*, and the cosmogonic incantation prologues, the Akkadian tradition is preoccupied with how the cosmos was set in order, how it can be threatened (by Tiamat, by Anzû, by Erra), and how the order is restored or guaranteed. The *akītu* festival in Babylon, at which *Enūma Eliš* was recited and Marduk's victory re-enacted, was understood not as a commemoration of a past event but as a renewal of cosmic order for the year ahead. This connection between literary text and ritual practice is the deepest in the Akkadian tradition.footnote:[Bottéro, J., _Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia_, trans. T. L. Fagan (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2001), pp. 86-127.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 86-127

Second, *the theology of kingship*. Almost every central Akkadian composition has something to say about who rules, how, and by what right. *Enūma Eliš* legitimates Marduk's cosmic kingship and, with it, the king of Babylon's earthly sovereignty. The royal legends of Sargon and Narām-Sîn establish the *narû* genre as a tool for kings to address future kings about the proper exercise of power. The *Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* is a Middle Assyrian rationalisation of an act of imperial conquest. The *Cuthaean Legend* uses Narām-Sîn as a cautionary example for the present king. Even the wisdom literature has a royal dimension: *The Babylonian Theodicy*'s author Saggil-kīnam-ubbib worked in the royal scribal apparatus, and *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* was attributed to the courtier Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan.

Third, *the pious sufferer and the inscrutability of divine justice*. The Akkadian theodicy tradition — *Dialogue between a Man and his God*, *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*, the *Babylonian Theodicy*, and the *Dialogue of Pessimism* — is the most sustained engagement with the problem of suffering in any pre-classical literature outside Job. The four poems take the question across seven centuries, in different registers — first-person complaint, four-tablet poetic monologue, acrostic dialogue, satirical reversal — without ever reaching a single answer. The persistence of the question across so much time, in so many forms, is one of the distinctive markers of Akkadian intellectual culture.

Fourth, *the transmission of knowledge through the scribal apparatus itself*. The disputation poems, the practical instruction texts, the *iškaru* canonical-series structure that organised so much of the corpus, the Late Bronze Age circulation of compositions through Amarna, Hattusa, Emar, and Ugarit — all of these reflect a literary culture acutely aware of the scribal craft as a vehicle for cultural memory. The three Akkadian wisdom genres in particular (reflective dialogue, practical instruction, disputation) are the means by which the *ṭupšarrūtu* — the cuneiform-trained literate professional who copied and transmitted the texts of this book; the scribal craft as a whole was called *ṭupšarrūtu* — taught itself to think.footnote:[Foster 2007, pp. 1-15.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 1-15

The Akkadian literary tradition did not arise in isolation. It is a junction in a far larger network of contacts, translations, and inheritances that ran across the ancient Near East from the third millennium BCE down to the end of cuneiform writing. Three contact streams in particular shaped the corpus and deserve mention here as a preview of the dedicated influences chapter that follows the comparative chapter at the end of this book.

The first and deepest is the *Sumerian inheritance*. Akkadian literature is not Sumerian literature in translation, but every Akkadian genre has Sumerian antecedents, and every Akkadian scribe was trained in Sumerian as the prestige learned language down to the end of cuneiform writing. The Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* is not a translation of the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*, but the Sumerian text is its substrate. The Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš* incorporates and transforms five Sumerian poems about the same hero. *Atra-ḫasīs* responds to and supersedes the Sumerian Eridu Genesis. The Akkadian disputation poems continue a Sumerian disputation tradition. The *narû* royal-legend genre takes up a Sumerian preoccupation with royal speech and adapts it. None of these are simple acts of borrowing; all of them are creative transformations of an inherited literary culture.

The second is the *Hurro-Hittite contact*. The kingdom of Mitanni in northern Mesopotamia and the Hittite Empire in Anatolia produced a literature in Hurrian and Hittite that drew on Mesopotamian cosmology and produced its own transformations. The *Kumarbi Cycle* — Anu's castration, Kumarbi's swallowing of the seed, Teshub's birth from the swallowed seed — has structural parallels to *Enūma Eliš* and to the *Theogony of Dunnu* close enough that scholars have argued for direct literary contact in either direction.footnote:[Hoffner, H.A. Jr., _Hittite Myths_, 2nd ed., Writings from the Ancient World 2 (Atlanta: Scholars Press, 1998), pp. 40-65 (Kumarbi Cycle).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=hoffner-1998 ; loc=pp. 40-65
The Mitanni dynasty included rulers with Indo-Aryan names; the treaties between the Hittites and the Mitanni invoke Indo-Iranian deities (Mitra, Varuna, Indra, the Nāsatyas) alongside Mesopotamian ones. The Akkadian literary corpus and the Indo-European-speaking ruling class of Mitanni were in direct contact through the Akkadian scribal apparatus. The influences chapter takes up this question — what was borrowed, what was independently developed, what is structural parallel without contact — at length.

The third is the *Ugaritic and West Semitic resonance*. The Ugaritic *Baal Cycle* (storm god vs. sea god vs. death god) is structurally close to *Enūma Eliš* and to the divine-conflict narratives more broadly, and the Akkadian Cedar Forest of *Gilgameš* is most likely the same forest as the one referenced in Ugaritic and Phoenician sources. Akkadian was the language of diplomatic correspondence between the Levantine coast and the Mesopotamian heartland; literary contact in both directions is documented through the Amarna and Ugarit archives.footnote:[Smith, M.S., _The Ugaritic Baal Cycle, Volume I_, Vetus Testamentum Supplements 55 (Leiden: Brill, 1994), pp. 1-100.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=smith-1994 ; loc=pp. 1-100

[INFERENCE: The convergence of these three contact streams in a single literary corpus — Sumerian below, Hurro-Hittite to the north, West Semitic to the west — and the centrality of Akkadian as the international lingua franca that mediated all of them, is the deepest reason this book treats the Akkadian-Babylonian-Assyrian tradition as a single corpus. The Akkadian literary world was the central node of the Late Bronze and Early Iron Age Near East, and its literature is shaped by and reciprocally shapes the traditions around it.]

The cross-cultural resonances that run through these forty-one compositions — the flood, the forfeited immortality, the descent to the underworld, the dragon-slaying that establishes cosmic order, the pious sufferer, the trial of trees, the rescue of the abandoned infant who becomes a king — are striking not because they prove borrowing or common ancestry (though in some cases they do), but because they reveal the depth and reach of the questions the Akkadian-speakers were asking. How was the world organised, and who organised it? Why do humans exist? What happens after death? Is power legitimate? Why do the pious suffer? How does cultural knowledge survive its own transmission?

These are the questions that every major mythological tradition addresses. The Akkadian answers, worked out in clay tablets across two thousand years by named and anonymous scribes, are among the earliest surviving attempts to address them at length and in writing. They are also, through the international circulation of Akkadian as a Late Bronze and Early Iron Age literary language, among the most consequential. Many of the answers given in these forty-one compositions reach forward, through the contact zones described above, into the literatures of Greece, Israel, and Iran — and from there into the broader stream of Western literary tradition that George Smith's audience, in 1872, was just beginning to recognise as not entirely unfamiliar.

The territory begins, in the next chapter, with Marduk and the saltwater chaos-figure he had to defeat to make a world.
