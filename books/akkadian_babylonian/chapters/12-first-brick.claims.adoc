=== Source overview

*The First Brick* is a short late-Babylonian Akkadian building incantation that grounds the temple-renovation ritual in a cosmogonic narrative: the wise god Ea creates the cosmic freshwater deep and then fashions from clay a divine craft-pantheon — the labour-force whose human counterparts later perform temple-renovation work.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 376–383 (text, translation, and commentary; chapter 33 in the volume's "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" section, Part III).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383

The composition has no preserved ancient title. The modern editorial title — *The First Brick* — is taken from the surviving content's foregrounding of the *libittu maḫrītu* ("first/former brick") in the temple-renovation ritual. Lambert 2013 establishes the editorial title and the chapter's place in the late-Babylonian creation dossier.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 376 (modern editorial title; chapter 33 placement among the "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" of Part III); Streck 2014, "Review of W. G. Lambert, *Babylonian Creation Myths*", *Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies* 77 (2014), 371–372 (independent confirmation of the title and Part-III placement; named-but-flagged — not currently in sources.yaml).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 376

The standard critical edition — and the only modern critical edition — is W. G. Lambert's treatment in *Babylonian Creation Myths* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 16, Eisenbrauns 2013), pp. 376–383. There is no widely-used English-language anthology translation of *The First Brick* in either Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., CDL Press 2005) or Dalley's *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., Oxford World's Classics 2008); Lambert 2013 stands alone as the modern reference for the composition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the sole modern edition); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, on the late-Babylonian creation dossier (general reference; Foster 2007 does not give a full translation of this composition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on the late-Babylonian dossier

The composition is preserved on multiple Late Babylonian witnesses, principally on the cuneiform tablet published as WVDOG 4 plate 12 (the *Wissenschaftliche Veröffentlichungen der Deutschen Orient-Gesellschaft* 4 publication of the early-twentieth-century Berlin excavations), with additional Late Babylonian tablets that Streck's 2014 review of Lambert 2013 records summarily as "etc." A late Assyrian fragment is also attested, providing limited cross-witness control alongside the Late Babylonian copies. The witnesses function as duplicates of a single recension rather than as recensional variants.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (manuscript condition and apparatus); Streck 2014, *BSOAS* 77, 371–372 ("The First Brick (WVDOG 4, pl. 12, etc.)"; named-but-flagged for the manuscript-history reference; not currently in sources.yaml).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383

=== Setting: temple renovation and the *libittu maḫrītu*

The composition's ritual setting is Babylonian temple **renovation**, not new-temple foundation. Babylonian and Assyrian temple-renovation rites required the recovery of the *libittu maḫrītu* — literally "the first/former brick" — from the structure of the existing temple before its rebuilding. The recovered brick was preserved through the rebuilding process and reset into the new structure as a continuity-marker between the old and new temples, ensuring the cult's ritual identity across the renovation.footnote:[Novotny, "Temple Building in Assyria", on the *libittu maḫrītu* rite in the royal inscriptions of Esarhaddon and Assurbanipal (named-but-flagged: J. Novotny, "Temple Building in Assyria: Evidence from Royal Inscriptions", in M. J. Boda and J. Novotny, eds., *From the Foundations to the Crenellations: Essays on Temple Building in the Ancient Near East and Hebrew Bible*, AOAT 366, Münster: Ugarit-Verlag 2010, pp. 109–139); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on the temple-foundation and temple-renovation cult.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on temple-foundation and temple-renovation cult

*The First Brick* gives this renovation rite a cosmogonic genealogy: the practitioners who recover and reset the *libittu maḫrītu* recapitulate, on the ritual scale, the divine creation of the craft-pantheon that Ea performed at the beginning. The composition therefore frames the renovation labour as the human echo of an originating divine labour-force.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the cosmogonic-genealogy reading of the renovation rite); Bottéro 2001, on the typological logic of Mesopotamian foundation rites.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on foundation-rite typology

The composition's manuscript form is late Babylonian — first millennium BCE — but the temple-renovation ritual context it presupposes is older, with the *libittu maḫrītu* concept attested in Assyrian royal-inscription practice from the seventh century BCE (Esarhaddon and Assurbanipal) and likely deeper into Mesopotamian temple-building cult. The composition belongs to the long tradition of Mesopotamian incantation and ritual literature in which architectural acts are theologically grounded.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. brick, foundation rituals (on Mesopotamian foundation-and-renovation ritual practice across periods); Bottéro 2001, on royal piety and the temple-foundation-and-renovation cult.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. brick, foundation rituals
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on royal piety

=== Ea creates the freshwater deep

Ea — god of wisdom, the freshwater *Apsû*, and incantation, patron of craft and cunning, father of Marduk — is the principal creator deity of the composition. The narrative opens with Ea's creation of the freshwater deep (*Apsû*; the Sumerian form *Abzû* appears in the late-Babylonian Akkadian-Sumerian register that Lambert's translation preserves). The *Apsû* is Ea's own dwelling and the cosmic water-source from which the materials of subsequent creation will be drawn.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea as principal creator deity; the *Apsû* as the cosmogonic starting-point); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ea (Ea as patron of the *Apsû* and of craft); Bottéro 2001, on Ea's role in the Babylonian theology of cosmogony and craft.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ea
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on Ea and craft

The Ea-centred framing of *The First Brick* is distinctive within the late-Babylonian creation dossier. Where *Enūma Eliš* (chapter 1), *Enmešarra's Defeat* (chapter 6), the *Toil of Babylon* (chapter 7), *Uraš and Marduk* (chapter 8), *Damkina's Bond* (chapter 9), and *The Founding of Eridu* (chapter 10) are Marduk-centred, *The First Brick* foregrounds Ea as the principal creator deity. Marduk is not named in the surviving narrative of *The First Brick*; the composition belongs to the dossier's Ea-centred minority register.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea as principal creator deity; absence of Marduk from the surviving narrative); Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401 (the wider late-Babylonian creation dossier, in which Marduk is the dominant divine actor of the majority of compositions); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian creation dossier.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on the late-Babylonian dossier

[INFERENCE: The Ea-centred framing of *The First Brick* is theologically coherent with Ea's wider role in Babylonian theology as the patron of craft, of skilled artisans, and of the techniques of construction. A composition that takes the divine craft-pantheon as its cast naturally has Ea at its head, since Ea is the source of crafts-knowledge in the late-Babylonian theological system. The Marduk-centred dominance of the wider creation dossier reflects the cult-political prominence of Marduk in late-Babylonian theology; *The First Brick* preserves an Ea-centred alternative that fits the composition's craft-and-construction subject. | basis: Ea is consistently identified in the late-Babylonian theological tradition as the patron of *kullatu* / the craftsmen's pantheon (Black & Green 1992, s.v. The reading is supported by Babylonian theology's standard assignment of craft-patronage to Ea but is the chapter's editorial framing, not a textually programmatic claim of *The First Brick* itself.]

=== Ea fashions the craft-pantheon from clay

After the creation of the *Apsû*, Ea fashions from clay a series of named deities, each tasked with a specific role in the temple-renovation labour. The composition presents this clay-creation as the foundational divine act behind the human craft-pantheon: every Mesopotamian artisan whose human work maintains the temples has a divine archetype in the cohort Ea creates here.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea's clay-creation of the named deities; the clay-creation as the foundational divine labour-force); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian incantation-cosmogony genre.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on incantation-cosmogony

The first of the deities created is Kulla, the brick-god — the divine artisan responsible for renovating the *libittu maḫrītu*. Kulla's creation by Ea is the cosmogonic centre of the composition's narrative: the brick-god, fashioned from the same clay he will subsequently work, is the divine archetype of the brick-laying artisan whose human counterparts perform the temple-renovation rite.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Kulla as brick-god created by Ea; Kulla's role in renovating the *libittu maḫrītu*); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Kulla (named-but-flagged: Black & Green 1992 includes a Kulla entry; consult).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Kulla

After Kulla, Ea creates Ninildu, Ninsimug, and Ninagal — three further craft-deities tasked with assisting in the construction work. The trio represents the wider craftsmen's roster: the divine carpenter (Ninildu), the divine smith (Ninsimug), and the divine metalworker (Ninagal), each named in the surviving lines as a participant in the temple-renovation labour.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ninildu, Ninsimug, Ninagal; the construction-craft trio); Black & Green 1992, s.v. craftsmen-deities.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. craftsmen-deities

Ea then creates a further cohort of deities — Arazu, Guškinbanda, Ninzadim, Ninkurra, Ašnan, Laḫar, Siris, Ningišzida, Ninšar, Adag, Umunmutamgu, and Umunmutamnag — tasked variously with supplying resources to mankind for the regular offerings that the maintained temples will require. The composition thus extends its cosmogonic framework outward from the renovation itself to the full economy of cult provisioning: each of the offered substances (grain, beer, wool, vegetables, etc.) has a divine patron-deity whose creation Ea performs alongside the construction-craft trio.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the resource-supply cohort of created deities); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian theology of cult provisioning.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on cult provisioning

The final deity Ea creates is Kusu, addressed as the "chief priest of the great gods." Kusu is tasked with performing the rites of the temple — the priestly counterpart to the artisanal Kulla. Where Kulla represents the divine archetype of the brick-laying craftsman, Kusu represents the divine archetype of the temple-priest, and the two together cover the full sacerdotal-and-artisanal economy of the temple-renovation ritual.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Kusu as "chief priest of the great gods"; Kusu's role in performing the rites of the temple); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Kusu (named-but-flagged: Black & Green 1992 includes a Kusu entry; consult).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Kusu

In addition to the seventeen named craft-pantheon deities, Ea creates a king and mankind — the full institutional and human counterparts to the divine labour-force. The king will commission the temple-renovation work; mankind will provide the regular offerings that the maintained temples require; and the divine craftsmen and priest will provide the divine archetypes that authorise both. The composition's full cosmogonic scope is thus not just the divine craft-pantheon but the entire economy of temple-cult that the Babylonian world depended on.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (Ea's creation of the king and mankind alongside the craft-pantheon); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian theology of cult-state-mankind integration.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on cult integration

[INFERENCE: The seventeen named craft-pantheon deities of *The First Brick* — Kulla, Ninildu, Ninsimug, Ninagal, Arazu, Guškinbanda, Ninzadim, Ninkurra, Ašnan, Laḫar, Siris, Ningišzida, Ninšar, Adag, Umunmutamgu, Umunmutamnag, Kusu — are largely Sumerian-named, preserved within an Akkadian-language theological framework. The composition shows the late-Babylonian preservation of Sumerian-named divine craftsmen as a continuous tradition: the names are inherited from the Sumerian craft-pantheon, and the Akkadian-language *First Brick* receives them as the standard divine personnel of the temple-renovation rite. | basis: the names listed above are well-attested in Sumerian theological literature and god-lists (Black & Green 1992 entries on the relevant deities); their preservation in an Akkadian-language late-Babylonian composition is consistent with the wider late-Babylonian retention of Sumerian-named craft-pantheon deities (Foster 2007 on late-Babylonian Sumero-Akkadian bilingualism). | risk: the "Sumerian inheritance" reading is a linguistic-historical observation rather than a claim of the composition itself; *The First Brick* presents the deities as already existing within its own theological framework, not as inheritances from another tradition. The reading is supported by the names' Sumerian etymologies but is the chapter's contextual framing, not a textually programmatic claim.]

=== The labour of renovation: the divine archetype

The composition concludes by tying the cosmogonic narrative back to the ritual present. The deities Ea has created are tasked, collectively, with the temple-renovation work; their human counterparts — the king who commissions, the artisans who labour, the priests who perform the rites, the populace whose offerings sustain the cult — perform the same work in the ritual present. Each renovation of each Babylonian temple is, in the composition's theology, a recapitulation of the divine labour-force's foundational work; each *libittu maḫrītu* recovered and reset is a continuity-marker not only between the old and new temple but between the human renovation and the divine creation that authorises it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (the closing section: the divine craft-pantheon as the archetype of the human renovation labour); Bottéro 2001, on the typological logic of Mesopotamian renovation rites; Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian incantation-cosmogony theology.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim on renovation-rite typology
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on incantation-cosmogony theology

The Akkadian common noun *libittu* — "brick" — is the underlying lexical term; the technical foundation/renovation phrase used in the composition and in the parallel royal-inscription rites is *libittu maḫrītu* ("first/former brick"). The qualifier *maḫrītu* (from *maḫrû* "first, former, prior") marks the brick as the original brick of the existing temple, recovered for reuse in the new structure rather than as a brand-new inaugural brick. The composition's theology depends on this temporal logic: the *libittu maḫrītu* is "first" not because it inaugurates a new building but because it is the original brick of the old building, preserved through its reuse.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (lexical commentary on *libittu maḫrītu*); CAD L, s.v. libittu (the standard Akkadian masonry term); CAD M/I, s.v. maḫrû (the qualifier "first, former, prior").]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=cad ; loc=L, s.v. libittu; M/I, s.v. maḫrû

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The First Brick* is marginal in attestation. Its modest manuscript footprint — a few Late Babylonian tablets and a late Assyrian fragment — places it well below the canonical-recension distribution of (e.g.) *Enūma Eliš* or *Atra-ḫasīs*. Its scholarly significance lies less in liturgical breadth than in its position within the late-Babylonian creation dossier as one of the dossier's Ea-centred minority compositions, alongside the cosmogonic incantation prologues and certain bilingual creation accounts.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (place within the dossier; modest manuscript footprint); Foster 2007, on the late-period Akkadian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

Within the dossier, *The First Brick* contributes the polytheistic / craft-pantheon-creation register: cosmogony staged as the divine creation of a labour-force. The composition is one of the clearest Akkadian witnesses to the theology in which divine creation is itself a labour of crafts — Ea fashioning artisans from clay rather than (as in *Enūma Eliš*) Marduk splitting a primordial body, or (as in *Atra-ḫasīs*) the lower gods rebelling against forced labour. The cosmogonic breadth of the dossier as a whole is enriched by the inclusion of compositions like *The First Brick* that hold their own ground against the Marduk-centred dominant register.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 376–383 (craft-pantheon-creation register); Foster 2007, on the diversity of late-Babylonian cosmogonic registers; Bottéro 2001, on the breadth of Babylonian theological thought.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 376-383

=== Closing reflection: the diversity of Babylonian creation thought

This chapter is the closing piece of the cosmogonic cycle in the book (chapters 1–12). The cycle has moved from *Enūma Eliš* — the dominant Babylonian cosmogony, the *akītu*-festival recitation that frames Marduk's kingship in cosmic-combat terms — through ten further compositions that together register the diversity of Babylonian creation thought beyond the dominant epic.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–401 (the wider Babylonian creation dossier collected in a single volume); Foster 2007, on the diversity of cosmogonic registers in the late-Babylonian compositional milieu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

The cosmogonic dossier, taken as a whole, has shown that Babylonian creation thought is not monolithic. Cosmogony is staged in this corpus as combat (*Enūma Eliš*, chapter 1; *The Slaying of Labbu*, chapter 5; *Enmešarra's Defeat*, chapter 6); as flood-survival and human-creation (*Atra-ḫasīs*, chapter 2); as the failure-narrative of human-divine boundary (*Adapa*, chapter 3); as theogonic genealogy and violent succession (*The Theogony of Dunnu*, chapter 4); as labour-rebellion and divine alienation (*The Toil of Babylon*, chapter 7); as boundary-disputes between gods (*Uraš and Marduk*, chapter 8); as marital bond and cosmic order (*Damkina's Bond*, chapter 9); as city-and-rivers foundation (*The Founding of Eridu*, chapter 10); as etiological incantation against affliction (*The Worm and the Toothache*, chapter 11); and, in this final chapter, as the divine creation of a craft-pantheon for temple renovation (*The First Brick*).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–401 and the per-chapter critical editions cited throughout; Foster 2007 on the diversity of registers; Bottéro 2001 on the breadth of Babylonian theological thought.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim

[INFERENCE: The breadth of registers preserved in the cosmogonic dossier — combat, flood, theogony, labour-rebellion, boundary-disputes, marital bond, city-foundation, incantation, craft-pantheon-creation — argues against any reading of Babylonian cosmogony as a single epic dressed in different costumes. The compositions are not variants of one another; they are distinct theological registers that the late-Babylonian scribal tradition preserved together because each carried its own load-bearing function (festival recitation, foundation ritual, medical-magical incantation, theogonic education, royal-piety performance, temple-renovation incantation). *Enūma Eliš* is the dominant composition by attestation, liturgical footprint, and length; it is not the dominant composition by theological scope. Compositions like *The First Brick*, with its Ea-centred craft-pantheon-creation register, hold their own ground within the dossier's theological economy — and would be obscured if the dossier were assimilated to a Marduk-centred whole. | basis: the dossier collected in Lambert 2013 (pp. 1–401) preserves the full range of registers; Foster 2007 surveys the late-period dossier and registers the diversity; the per-chapter critical editions and translations cited throughout chapters 1–11 of this book establish each composition's distinctive theological contribution; *The First Brick*'s Ea-centred framing (verified in this chapter's round-2 revision) is itself a witness to the dossier's theological diversity. | risk: the closing-reflection framing is the chapter's editorial reading, written from the vantage of the cosmogonic-cycle's end; the late-Babylonian scribal tradition itself preserves no programmatic statement that the dossier should be read as a register-diverse whole rather than as *Enūma Eliš* and its supplements. The structural reading is supported by the dossier's content but is the modern editorial framing rather than an ancient theological program.]

// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Mesopotamian internal (Sumerian background) — the seventeen named craft-pantheon deities created by Ea in *The First Brick* are largely Sumerian-named (Kulla, Ninildu, Ninsimug, Ninagal, Arazu, Guškinbanda, Ninzadim, Ninkurra, Ašnan, Laḫar, Siris, Ningišzida, Ninšar, Adag, Umunmutamgu, Umunmutamnag, Kusu). Their preservation within an Akkadian-language late-Babylonian composition shows the continuous late-Babylonian retention of Sumerian-named craft-pantheon deities. The Sumerian background is the wider tradition from which the named deities are inherited; the Sumerian craft-pantheon would belong to the companion *Myths of Sumer* volume as primary material, while *The First Brick* itself is in scope as an Akkadian composition. Reserved for the influences chapter.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Hebrew Bible — Job 38:6, "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? ... Who laid its cornerstone, when the morning stars sang together?", shares the cosmogony-as-architectural-construction imagery at the broadest typological level. The Hebrew text postdates the Akkadian by centuries and the relationship is typological rather than transmissional; the shared imagery represents the wider ancient Near Eastern pattern of cosmogony-as-construction. Reserved for the influences chapter.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Egyptian — Egyptian temple-renovation rituals likewise traced architectural acts to divine archetypes. The libittu maḫrītu concept (a continuity-brick recovered from the old temple and reset in the new) has Egyptian functional analogues in foundation-deposit rituals that bridge old and new temple structures. The parallels are typological — the "founding/renovation as recapitulation of cosmogonic action" pattern is widespread across ancient Near Eastern temple-cult traditions. Reserved for the influences chapter.
// COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Methodological / book-structural — this is the closing chapter of the cosmogonic cycle in the book (chapters 1–12). The closing reflection on the diversity of Babylonian creation thought beyond *Enūma Eliš* feeds directly into the comparative chapter and the influences chapter, where the dossier-as-a-whole becomes the load-bearing observation for cross-cultural comparison. *The First Brick*'s contribution is the Ea-centred craft-pantheon-creation register — a register that would be obscured if the dossier were assimilated to a Marduk-centred whole.
