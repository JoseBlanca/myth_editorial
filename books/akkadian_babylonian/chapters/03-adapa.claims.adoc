== Adapa and the South Wind — Claims

=== Source overview

*Adapa and the South Wind* survives in Akkadian on three fragmentary witnesses across two recensions: a Middle Babylonian copy from Amarna in Egypt (Fragment A, EA 356, 14th century BCE) and Neo-Assyrian copies from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh (Fragments B, C, and D, with K.8743 the principal manuscript, 7th century BCE).footnote:[Izre'el 2001, *Adapa and the South Wind: Language Has the Power of Life and Death*, pp. 9–11 (manuscript catalogue); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 525, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 525

The standard critical edition is Shlomo Izre'el's *Adapa and the South Wind: Language Has the Power of Life and Death* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 10, Eisenbrauns 2001), which edits the Akkadian fragments together and provides English translation, philological commentary, and recensional analysis.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, full volume; English translation pp. 9–73.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-73

The accessible English translation cited throughout for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "Adapa," in *Before the Muses*, third edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 525–530.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 525–530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 525-530

A third translation, by Stephanie Dalley in *Myths from Mesopotamia* (revised edition, Oxford World's Classics 2008), pp. 182–188, serves as a triangulation reference.footnote:[Dalley 2008, pp. 182–188.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 182-188

Fragment A — the Amarna copy — is the longest single witness and provides the bulk of the heaven-audience scene; its reverse is partly damaged, and the conclusion of the audience and Adapa's return are partly broken.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and pp. 17–35 (Fragment A edition and apparatus); Foster 2005, pp. 525–528.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11, 17-35

The Nineveh fragments (B, C, D) are shorter individually but preserve a substantively different framing of the same scene; their beginning is partly lost, and the south-wind episode is reconstructed from cross-fragment readings.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 35–61 (Nineveh fragments edition); Foster 2005, pp. 528–530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 35-61

Neither recension alone is complete; both are needed for the full narrative, and Izre'el's edition presents them in parallel rather than as a single composite.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 (recensional methodology); Foster 2005, p. 525, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11

There is no Old Babylonian Akkadian *Adapa*. The Sumerian-language tablet from Tell Haddad (ancient Me-Turan), published by Cavigneaux and Al-Rawi 1993, is sometimes cited in older literature as an Old Babylonian witness; it is in fact a Sumerian composition and belongs to the Sumerian literary tradition rather than to the Akkadian *Adapa* corpus.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. xv–xviii (history of scholarship); ETCSL 1.7.5 (the Sumerian Adapa from Tell Haddad), cited as comparative material only.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. xv-xviii
// EVIDENCE: source_id=etcsl ; loc=ETCSL 1.7.5 (comparative)

Adapa is integrated into the *apkallu* (antediluvian-sage) tradition by the Late Babylonian period, where he is reckoned the first of seven sages who brought civilization to humanity before the flood.footnote:[Kvanvig 2011, *Primeval History*, pp. 107–115 (Adapa and the apkallu lineage); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 71–73; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Apkallu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 107-115
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 71-73
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Apkallu

The *apkallu* lineage that names Adapa as the first of seven sages is a Late Babylonian overlay on the original story; it is not present in the Amarna copy, where Adapa is simply Ea's priest at Eridu.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and commentary; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 107–115, on the stratification.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 107-115

=== Setting: Adapa, priest of Ea at Eridu

Adapa is the priest of Ea at Eridu, the oldest city in Mesopotamian tradition and Ea's principal cult-centre.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, p. 17 (Fragment A, opening); Foster 2005, p. 526; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Adapa.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=p. 17
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Adapa

In his role as Ea's priest, Adapa daily fishes the Persian Gulf to provide the temple with food offerings.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 17–19 (Fragment A, lines 1–10); Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 17-19

The Amarna recension introduces him with a hymn to his exceptional wisdom: he is *atra-ḫasīs* (the same epithet as the flood hero), full of understanding, the model of the priestly sage.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 17–19 (Fragment A, lines 1–10); Foster 2005, p. 526; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 105–110.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 17-19

Adapa's name itself appears in variant readings across the recensions: Adapa (the standard), Adamu in some late lists, Uan in the *apkallu* genealogy.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 11–13 (name variants); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Adapa, Apkallu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 11-13

=== The Amarna recension (Fragment A): the south wind and Ea's warning

In the Amarna recension, Adapa goes out fishing one day in his boat.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 19–23 (Fragment A, lines 10–25); Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 19-23

The south wind (Akkadian *šūtu*) rises against him and capsizes his boat into the freshwater Apsû.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 19–23 (Fragment A, lines 10–25); Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 19-23

Adapa is enraged. He pronounces an incantation against the south wind and breaks her wing — in some readings, snaps her wing seven times, the seven-fold breaking that disables her completely.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 21–23 (Fragment A, lines 17–25); Foster 2005, p. 526.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 21-23

The south wind ceases blowing for seven days. The cosmic balance is disrupted; the marshland breeze that cools the alluvium is gone.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 23–25 (Fragment A, lines 25–35); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 23-25

Anu in the heavens notices the absence and demands an explanation. He summons Adapa to his court to answer for the breaking of the south wind's wing.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 25–29 (Fragment A, lines 35–55); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 25-29

Ea, anticipating the summons, instructs Adapa in detail before he ascends to heaven.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 27–31 (Fragment A, lines 55–75); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 27-31

Ea's instructions specify ritual and rhetorical preparations: dress in mourning clothes; let your hair go unkempt; at the gate of heaven Tammuz and Gizzida will challenge you, mourning their own absence from the world below — say that you mourn for them, and they will intercede for you.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 27–31 (Fragment A, lines 55–75); Foster 2005, p. 527; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tammuz, Ningishzida.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 27-31
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tammuz

Tammuz (also called Dumuzi in the Akkadian tradition) and Gizzida (Akkadian for the Sumerian Ningišzida — an underworld-vegetation god) stand as Anu's gatekeepers in this scene; they have descended to the underworld in their seasonal pattern and are mourning their own exile.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 27–31; Foster 2005, p. 527; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tammuz, Ningishzida.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 27-31

Ea's central instruction concerns what Anu will offer Adapa once the audience proper begins. Anu will offer "the bread of death" (*akala ša mūti*) and "the water of death" (*mê ša mūti*); Ea warns Adapa not to eat or drink them — they will kill him.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 29–33 (Fragment A, lines 60–80); Foster 2005, p. 527; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–125 (the bread/water of life/death cruxes).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 29-33
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-125

Ea further instructs Adapa to accept the oil and the clothing Anu will also offer; these will be safe.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 31–33 (Fragment A, lines 75–85); Foster 2005, p. 527.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 31-33

Adapa ascends to heaven by the road that leads up.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 33–37 (Fragment A, lines 85–105); Foster 2005, p. 528.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 33-37

At Anu's gate Adapa meets Tammuz and Gizzida. He gives the answer Ea coached him to give — that he mourns for them, who have gone from the land — and the two gods are favourably impressed. They intercede with Anu on his behalf.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 35–39 (Fragment A, lines 100–125); Foster 2005, p. 528.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 35-39

[LACUNA: Fragment A (Amarna), reverse — the conclusion of the heaven audience and Adapa's return | source: Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and pp. 17–35 | scholarly_reconstruction: partial; Fragment B (NA) supplies a different framing of the audience scene but is not strictly substitutable]

=== The Nineveh recension (Fragments B, C, D): the bread and water of life

[LACUNA: Fragments B, C, D (Nineveh), beginning — Adapa's identity and the south-wind incident are partly lost | source: Izre'el 2001, pp. 35–61 | scholarly_reconstruction: yes — internal Nineveh joins; Izre'el reconstructs the SB composite from cross-fragment readings]

[RECONSTRUCTION: The opening of the Nineveh recension — including the seven-fold breaking of the south wind's wing — is reconstructed from cross-fragment readings within the Neo-Assyrian witnesses. | gap_source: Fragments B, C, D opening | fill_source: internal cross-fragment joins among the Nineveh manuscripts (K.8743 + duplicates), collated by Izre'el 2001 | confidence: medium]

In the Nineveh recension, the heaven-audience scene is extended. After Tammuz and Gizzida intercede, Anu's anger turns to a question: who has revealed the secrets of heaven and earth to this man?footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 39–47 (Fragments B/C, audience scene); Foster 2005, pp. 528–529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 39-47

Anu's question identifies Ea as the source of Adapa's wisdom. The audience shifts from a court-disciplinary scene to a recognition-and-honour scene.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 41–47 (Fragments B/C); Foster 2005, p. 529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 41-47

In a turn that the Amarna recension does not preserve in full, Anu now offers Adapa not the bread of death but "the bread of life" (*akala ša balāṭi*) and "the water of life" (*mê ša balāṭi*) — the food and drink that confer immortality.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 45–51 (Fragments B/C, the bread-of-life scene); Foster 2005, p. 529; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–125.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 45-51
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-125

Adapa, faithful to Ea's instructions — instructions Ea had framed as a warning against the bread of *death* — refuses the bread and the water that Anu now offers him.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 47–51 (Fragments B/C, the refusal); Foster 2005, p. 529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 47-51

Adapa accepts the oil and the clothing — as Ea instructed — and is anointed and dressed.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 49–51 (Fragments B/C); Foster 2005, p. 529.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 49-51

Anu's reaction is the interpretive crux of the poem. Different fragments preserve readings between laughter and sorrow. He observes that Adapa has refused immortality, and that the loss is now permanent — for Adapa and for humanity through him.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 51–55 (Fragments B/C, Anu's reaction); Foster 2005, pp. 529–530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 51-55

Adapa is sent back down to the earth without the immortality he was offered. The opportunity is foreclosed; humanity will remain mortal.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 53–57 (Fragments B/C/D, the descent); Foster 2005, p. 530.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 53-57

=== The interpretive crux: was Ea's warning a deception?

The poem turns on a misreading: Ea warns Adapa against the bread of *death*, but Anu offers the bread of *life*. Whether Ea was protecting Adapa or deliberately misleading him is the central interpretive question of the text.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, p. 121 (commentary on the warning); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–135 (survey of readings); Foster 2005, p. 525, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=p. 121
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-135

One reading, attested in scholarship since the early 20th century, treats Ea as having given a sincere warning that turned out to be wrong — Ea did not know what Anu would offer.footnote:[Kvanvig 2011, pp. 115–135 (survey of the sincere-warning reading); Izre'el 2001 commentary.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 115-135

A second reading, more recent, treats Ea as deliberately deceptive — Ea is the god of wisdom who knows the secrets of heaven, and he chose to keep immortality from Adapa.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, p. 121 (deception reading); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 130–135.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=p. 121
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 130-135

A third reading treats the warning as a misunderstanding by Adapa rather than a deception by Ea: Adapa heard "death" because he was cued for danger, and missed the chance to recognise that Anu's posture had changed.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 121–125 (the misunderstanding reading); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 130–135.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 121-125

[INFERENCE: The poem's didactic function depends on the interpretive ambiguity rather than on its resolution. The text rewards rereading and produces a Mesopotamian commonplace — that human wisdom, even at its highest in a sage like Adapa, is bounded by structural limits. | basis: the text preserves the ambiguity rather than resolving it; Izre'el's commentary p. 121 and Kvanvig 2011 pp. 115–135 both treat the ambiguity as load-bearing; Foster 2005's headnote characterises the poem as a meditation on the limits of knowledge. | risk: the ambiguity-as-feature reading is itself a scholarly judgement; some readers prefer to settle on one of the three resolutions above.]

=== Variants and recensional history

[VARIANT: primary=co-equal Amarna (MB, Fragment A) and Nineveh (NA, Fragments B/C/D) recensions presented side by side per the brief | alt=The Amarna recension emphasises Ea's instructions and the south-wind episode in greater detail; the Nineveh recension extends the Anu-audience scene and the bread-and-water-of-life sequence | chosen: both, presented in parallel | reason: neither recension alone is complete; the Amarna copy preserves the south-wind episode, the Nineveh fragments preserve the heaven-audience climax]

[VARIANT: primary=Adapa as the sage's name in all major recensions | alt=Adamu and Uan are alternative readings of the same figure in some Late Babylonian apkallu lists; the apkallu-genealogy framing is itself a Late Babylonian overlay | chosen: Adapa | reason: standard form across the recensions; Adamu and Uan are noted in a footnote where the apkallu genealogy is discussed]

The recensional history of *Adapa* is therefore not a story of one prior text descending into many copies but of two substantially different framings preserved in different scribal cultures. The Amarna copy was produced for the Egyptian royal archive; the Nineveh fragments were produced for Aššurbanipal's library; both descend from earlier traditions whose Old Babylonian Akkadian witnesses, if they ever existed, do not survive.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and pp. xv–xviii; Foster 2007, pp. 71–73.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. xv-xviii
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 71-73

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Adapa* is one of the Akkadian texts that consistently links the wisdom tradition to the cult of Ea at Eridu. The hero is Ea's priest, and the wisdom that fails him is Ea's wisdom.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 121–125 (commentary on the wisdom theme); Kvanvig 2011, pp. 105–115.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 121-125
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 105-115

In the Late Babylonian tradition, Adapa is integrated into the *apkallu* list — the seven antediluvian sages who brought the arts of civilization to humanity before the flood. He is reckoned the first of the seven, and his story is invoked in apotropaic rituals that name the seven sages.footnote:[Kvanvig 2011, pp. 107–115 (apkallu lineage and ritual context); Foster 2007, pp. 71–73; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Apkallu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=pp. 107-115

This *apkallu*-frame is a later overlay. In the Amarna copy, Adapa is simply Ea's priest at Eridu; the seven-sage genealogy is not present.footnote:[Izre'el 2001, pp. 9–11 and commentary; Kvanvig 2011, pp. 107–115.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=izreel-2001 ; loc=pp. 9-11
