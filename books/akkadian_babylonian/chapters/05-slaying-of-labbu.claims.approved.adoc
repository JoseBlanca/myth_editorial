=== Source overview

*The Slaying of Labbu* is a short, heavily fragmentary Akkadian combat-myth preserved primarily on a Neo-Assyrian tablet from Aššurbanipal's library, published as CT 13, plates 33–34 (British Museum, *Cuneiform Texts from Babylonian Tablets in the British Museum*, vol. 13; Rm 282 = CDLI P385885). A more fragmentary Old Babylonian copy is also attested but is too broken to supply parallel readings.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 361–365 (text, translation, commentary, and manuscript description); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 581, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-365
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 581

The composition has no preserved ancient title. The modern title is editorial, taken from the name of the antagonist, Labbu — Akkadian for "raging" or "lion-like" — who is the dragon-monster the surviving fragment describes.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 361 (modern title and lexical note); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Labbu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 361
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Labbu

The standard critical edition is W. G. Lambert's treatment in *Babylonian Creation Myths* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 16, Eisenbrauns 2013), pp. 361–365.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–365 (full edition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-365

The accessible English translation cited throughout for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "The Slaying of Labbu," in *Before the Muses*, third edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 581–582.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 581–582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 581-582

The Neo-Assyrian manuscript (CT 13, plates 33–34) is the only continuous witness to the composition. The Old Babylonian fragment is too broken to supply parallel readings, and no Middle Babylonian or other manuscripts are known. In the Old Babylonian recension the hero is reportedly named as Nergal rather than the Tišpak of the Neo-Assyrian version, but the OB lines are too damaged to confirm this in detail.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (single-manuscript transmission for the NA recension; OB fragment status); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-362

[LACUNA: CT 13, 33–34 — extensive breaks throughout the surviving column. The kill scene itself, the post-combat outcome, and a strategy-advice scene where a separate god's name is broken are all lost in the lacuna. | source: Lambert 2013, pp. 361–365 | scholarly_reconstruction: not available — the OB recension is too broken to fill the NA lacunae, and no other Akkadian witnesses exist for cross-fragment fill]

The surviving Neo-Assyrian fragment preserves a creation/design scene, a divine planning council, and the start of the combat. The kill itself is broken away, and the post-combat aftermath is not recoverable.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (surviving narrative scope); Foster 2005, p. 581, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-362

=== Labbu is created: Sea bears him, Enlil designs his shape

In the surviving opening, the Sea (Akkadian *tâmtu*) bears Labbu, and Enlil in heaven designs his shape — the dragon is created by the gods themselves.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (the creation/design scene; preserved opening lines: "Sea [created] the serpent, / Enlil in heaven designed [his shape]"); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-362

This creation-by-the-gods detail is load-bearing. It makes the Labbu narrative not just a combat poem but a creation poem in which the cosmic order has produced its own threat. The pattern is structurally close to *Atra-ḫasīs*, where the gods create humanity and then move to destroy it; here the gods create the dragon and then move to slay it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 362 (commentary on the creation-then-combat structure); Foster 2005, p. 581; Bottéro 2001, on the combat-myth typology.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 362
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=combat-myth typology

=== Labbu's monstrous form

The text describes Labbu's monstrous form in dimensions and composite features. The dragon is enormous: fifty leagues (*bēru*) in length, one league in width, with a mouth half a *nindanu* wide and an ear-span of one *nindanu*. Its features are leonine and serpentine: the head of a lion or serpent, the horns of a horned viper, the front legs of a lion and the hind legs of a bird of prey.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 362–363 (Labbu's physical description); Foster 2005, p. 581; Black & Green 1992, s.v. monsters.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 362-363
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. monsters

Labbu emerges from the saltwater Sea rather than from the freshwater Apsû.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 362–363; Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 362-363

The gods are terrified by Labbu's appearance. They convene in council to decide how to respond.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 362–363 (the divine fear); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 362-363

=== The divine council and the dispatch of Tišpak

The moon-god Sîn appears in the surviving council scene, addressing the assembled gods about the threat Labbu poses.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 363 (Sîn's role in the council); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 363

The gods dispatch Tišpak — warrior-god of Ešnunna in the Diyala region — as the divine champion against Labbu. The dispatch line is preserved in the Neo-Assyrian recension: "Go, Tišpak, k[ill] Labbu! Save the broad land!"footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 363–364 (the dispatch line; Tišpak named in the surviving text); Foster 2005, p. 581; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tišpak.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 363-364
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tišpak

Tišpak is the warrior-god of Ešnunna, identified in the iconographic record (especially Diyala-region cylinder seals) with the dragon *mušḫuššu* — a serpentine composite creature. His selection as the slayer of Labbu fits his known combat profile.footnote:[Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tišpak (on the warrior-god's iconography and Ešnunna affiliation); Lambert 2013, p. 364 (commentary on Tišpak's role).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tišpak
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 364

[INFERENCE: The Labbu myth was probably composed in part to justify Tišpak's installation as the tutelary god of Ešnunna — a cult-aetiological reading that explains the myth's local-political function. | basis: Tišpak's iconographic association with the dragon *mušḫuššu* on Diyala-region cylinder seals; the structural fit between the myth's dragon-slaying and Tišpak's combat profile (Wiggermann's argument in "Tišpak, his seal, and the dragon mušḫuššu"). | risk: Lambert 2013 expresses skepticism about this cult-aetiological reading, calling such theories speculative given the poor preservation of the text; the Old Babylonian recension may have named a different hero (Nergal), which complicates a Tišpak-specific cult-aetiology and suggests the dragon-slaying motif circulated independently of any single deity's local cult.]

Tišpak is equipped with weapons and divine authority for the combat. The surviving text preserves part of this equipping scene, including reference to a storm that erupts in association with the combat and an arrow that the champion uses against Labbu.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 363–364 (the equipping scene; storm and arrow as combat elements); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 363-364

The combat itself begins on the preserved tablet. The surviving lines describe the champion's approach to Labbu and the opening exchanges of the combat — Tišpak fires an arrow against the dragon, with the storm rising around him — before the manuscript breaks off.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 364 (the combat opening; the storm-and-arrow combat); Foster 2005, p. 582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 364

=== What is lost

The decisive kill scene is not preserved. Whether the arrow flight is the killing blow, whether Tišpak addresses Labbu in speech first, and whether other weapons (Tišpak's seal, in some readings of Wiggermann) are deployed are unrecoverable in detail.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 364–365 (on what is lost); Foster 2005, p. 582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 364-365

The post-combat aftermath is also lost. Whether Labbu's body becomes a feature of the cosmos (as Tiamat's does in *Enūma Eliš*), whether Tišpak is exalted to higher status (as Marduk and Ninurta are after their combats), and whether the text closes with a hymnic-theological recitation are all unknown.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 364–365; Foster 2005, p. 582.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 364-365

A separate god's name is also broken in a strategy-advice scene preserved earlier in the surviving text — the god who advises Tišpak on how to approach the combat. The advisor is unnamed in the surviving lines.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 363–364 (the strategy-advice scene with broken speaker); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 363-364

[INFERENCE: The lost ending most likely included a decisive kill and some form of cosmic-political consequence — the standard pattern in the Akkadian combat-myth corpus. | basis: every preserved Akkadian combat-myth (Marduk vs. Tiamat, Ninurta vs. Anzû, Erra's plague-combat) culminates in a decisive kill or victory followed by political consequence; Lambert 2013 p. 365 reads the lost ending as fitting this pattern; Bottéro 2001's combat-myth typology supports the structural reading. | risk: the surviving text does not preserve the ending, and the pattern-completion is therefore an extrapolation from the corpus rather than a textually attested reconstruction.]

=== Variants

The Slaying of Labbu has two attested recensions: the Neo-Assyrian CT 13, 33–34 (the principal witness, with Tišpak as hero) and a more fragmentary Old Babylonian copy (in which Nergal is reportedly the hero). The OB recension is too damaged to supply parallel readings.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 361–362 (the two recensions); Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 361-362

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The Slaying of Labbu* is marginal in attestation but significant as one node in the broader Akkadian combat-myth dossier. Akkadian literature preserves several combat-myths — Marduk versus Tiamat (in *Enūma Eliš*), Ninurta versus Anzû, Erra and Išum's plague-combat, and the Cuthaean Legend's monstrous adversaries — and Labbu sits among them as an alternative combat-cosmogony that survives only in fragmentary form.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 364–365 (Labbu's place in the combat-myth dossier); Foster 2005, p. 581, headnote; Bottéro 2001, on the combat-myth typology.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 364-365
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 581
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=combat-myth typology

The composition's significance lies less in its own narrative — too fragmentary to support deep treatment — than in its presence as evidence that the Akkadian theological imagination ran multiple combat-cosmogonies in parallel. Marduk's defeat of Tiamat in *Enūma Eliš* is the canonical version, but other compositions described other dragon-slayings, with other heroes, in other places.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 365; Foster 2005, p. 581.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 365

[INFERENCE: The Slaying of Labbu is best read as part of an alternative combat-cosmogony dossier that surrounded the dominant Marduk-Tiamat narrative — evidence that the cosmic-combat motif circulated in multiple recensions in the Akkadian theological imagination, even when only one (the Marduk version) achieved canonical status through the *akītu* festival. | basis: Lambert 2013 pp. 364–365 places Labbu within this dossier; Foster 2005's headnote characterises the composition as one of several "alternative" combat-cosmogonies; Bottéro 2001 on the combat-myth typology; the dual-recension transmission (NA Tišpak / OB Nergal) is itself evidence that the dragon-slaying motif circulated with different heroes in different cultic milieus. | risk: the structural reading is interpretive; the fragmentary state of the text leaves uncertain whether Labbu was a major rival to Marduk-Tiamat or a peripheral local-cult composition.]
