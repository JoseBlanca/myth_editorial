=== Source overview

*The Founding of Eridu* is a short bilingual Sumerian-Akkadian incantation prologue that narrates Marduk's creation of the primordial cities, the rivers, and humankind. The composition is preserved on a single manuscript, BM 93014 (British Museum), in the bilingual scribal tradition of late-Babylonian temple-scholarly compositions.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 366–375 (text, translation, and commentary); the manuscript identifier BM 93014 is also given in older bilingual-text literature, traceable to L. W. King's *The Seven Tablets of Creation* II (1902).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375

The composition has multiple alternative editorial titles in scholarly use. Lambert 2013 uses "The Founding of Eridu" (after the cosmogonic centrality of Eridu in the surviving lines); older bilingual-text literature, going back to L. W. King's *The Seven Tablets of Creation* II (1902), used "The Bilingual Creation of the World by Marduk" or "The Bilingual Version of the Creation Legend"; Benjamin Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., 2005) translates the same composition under the editorial title "Marduk, Creator of the World" within the "Myths of Origins" sub-section.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 366 (modern editorial title and the multiple-title situation); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 487 ("Marduk, Creator of the World" within the "Myths of Origins" sub-section).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 366
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487 ("Marduk, Creator of the World")

The standard modern critical edition is Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375. Foster's *Before the Muses* p. 487 supplies the accessible English translation under the editorial title "Marduk, Creator of the World"; Dalley's *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., 2008) does not contain this composition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the principal critical edition); Foster 2005, p. 487 (accessible translation); Dalley 2008 (does not contain this composition).]

The single manuscript, BM 93014, is bilingual: parallel Sumerian and Akkadian columns on the same tablet. Approximately 40 lines are preserved in complete or nearly-complete form, with the remainder of the tablet fragmentary. The bilingual parallelism — each column glossing the other — is the principal internal-reconstruction mechanism Lambert 2013 deploys for the damaged passages.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (manuscript condition and bilingual-parallelism reconstruction method); Foster 2007, on the bilingual scribal tradition.]

=== Setting: a bilingual incantation prologue

The composition is not a freestanding myth. It is the cosmogonic prologue of a longer ritual incantation. The cosmogony narrative establishes Marduk's authority as cosmic creator, and the incantation that follows on the same tablet invokes that authority for ritual purposes. The text itself indicates that the incantation was recited in the E-Zida temple — probably Nabu's great temple at Borsippa, the principal cult-centre of Marduk's son.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (incantation-prologue setting; E-Zida ritual context); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian incantation-prologue genre and its temple-recitation contexts.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=on incantation-prologue temple contexts

The composition is dated to the Neo- or Late-Babylonian period in its surviving manuscript form (sixth century BCE), but the underlying composition is likely earlier — probably Kassite or post-Kassite — based on its theological content (Marduk already supreme creator, but the bilingual-tradition framework rather than the Standard Babylonian epic register of *Enūma Eliš*) and its ritual context (temple-incantation use, likely older than the Neo-Babylonian copy).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (dating discussion; Neo-Babylonian manuscript with possibly earlier composition date); Foster 2007, on Kassite-and-later incantation literature.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375 (dating)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

The composition's bilingual format — parallel Sumerian and Akkadian columns — is characteristic of late-Babylonian temple-scholarly compositions in which Sumerian, by then a learned and liturgical language rather than a vernacular one, is preserved alongside Akkadian. Per scope.md, the Sumerian column is in scope only insofar as it is integral to the Akkadian literary product as transmitted; the chapter treats the composition as an Akkadian text with a load-bearing Sumerian gloss-column, not as a Sumerian text in its own right.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (bilingual-tradition framing); Foster 2007, on late-Babylonian Sumero-Akkadian bilingualism; scope.md (in-scope status of the Sumerian column).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

=== Negative-cosmogonic opening: "the holy house had not yet been made"

The composition opens on a negative-cosmogonic statement: "the holy house, the house of the gods in the holy place had not yet been made". This is not a positive-action opening (cf. *Enūma Eliš*'s *enūma eliš lā nabû šamāmū* — "When on high the heavens had not yet been named"); it is a negative inventory of what does not yet exist. No temple, no city, no land emerged from the primordial waters has yet come into being.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the negative-cosmogonic opening; bilingual columns of the opening lines); Foster 2005, p. 487; Black & Green 1992, s.v. cosmogony (on the negative-cosmogonic opening pattern in Mesopotamian creation literature).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375 (opening lines)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. cosmogony

[INFERENCE: The negative-cosmogonic opening pattern — "X had not yet been made" — situates *The Founding of Eridu* within a broader Mesopotamian cosmogonic typology shared with *Enūma Eliš*'s *enūma eliš lā nabû šamāmū* opening, *Atra-ḫasīs*'s opening on the gods doing the work before mankind was created, and (further afield) Genesis 1's "the earth was without form and void" opening. The pattern stages cosmogony as the filling-in of an initially empty cosmos rather than as a transformation of an initially full or chaotic one. | basis: the structural-typological framing follows the standard Assyriological reading (Black & Green 1992, s.v. cosmogony; Bottéro 2001 on cosmogonic openings); the comparison with *Enūma Eliš* is direct (both are Marduk-centred Akkadian cosmogonies with negative openings); the comparison with Genesis 1 is typological. | risk: the negative-cosmogonic opening is a widely shared pattern in ancient Near Eastern creation literature, and its specific deployment in *The Founding of Eridu* may differ from the deployments in *Enūma Eliš* and Genesis 1 in ways the surviving lines do not let us specify; the typological framing is structural rather than textually programmatic.]

=== Marduk founds Eridu in the Apsû

After the negative inventory, the composition's first positive-cosmogonic act is Marduk's founding of Eridu. Eridu is established in the Apsû — the freshwater cosmic abyss beneath the earth — and its temple is built. Eridu is the cosmologically first city in Mesopotamian theological geography, the traditional cult-centre of Ea (Enki).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the founding of Eridu in the Apsû); Foster 2005, p. 487; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Eridu (the first city; Ea's cult-centre); RlA s.v. Eridu.]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Eridu
// EVIDENCE: source_id=rla ; loc=s.v. Eridu

[INFERENCE: That Marduk — Babylon's patron god — is given the founding of Eridu as his first cosmogonic act represents a theological move characteristic of the late-Babylonian Marduk dossier: Marduk takes credit for the foundational cosmogonic acts traditionally attributed to other deities. Ea, the traditional founder and patron of Eridu, here functions as the cosmic-water domain (the Apsû) within which Marduk's foundation occurs, rather than as the founder himself. The composition is theologically aligned with *Enūma Eliš* in placing Marduk at the cosmogonic centre, but uses a bilingual incantation-prologue register rather than the Standard Babylonian epic register. | basis: Eridu is universally recognised in Mesopotamian theology as Ea / Enki's traditional cult-centre (Black & Green 1992, s.v. Eridu; RlA s.v. Eridu); the late-Babylonian Marduk dossier (Lambert 2013 Part III pp. 281–401) repeatedly features this kind of theological-political move where Marduk absorbs the cosmogonic prerogatives of older deities (cf. *Enmešarra's Defeat*, Chapter 6; *Damkina's Bond*, Chapter 9). | risk: the surviving lines may not preserve enough of the divine-cast language to confirm that Ea is being deliberately backgrounded rather than incidentally absent; the framing rests on the broader dossier's pattern, not on a programmatic statement in *The Founding of Eridu* itself.]

=== Marduk creates Babylon, Nippur, and Erech

After Eridu, the composition narrates Marduk's foundation of additional primordial cities: Babylon (with its temple Esagil); Nippur (with its temple E-kur); and Erech (Sumerian Uruk; with its temple E-Aena). The cities and their temples are presented as cosmic structures created together with the world rather than as historical foundations.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the founding of Babylon, Nippur, and Erech, with temples); Foster 2005, p. 487; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Babylon, Nippur, Uruk.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375 (founding of cities)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Babylon, Nippur, Uruk

The composition's geographic scope is the southern Mesopotamian alluvium — the cities of the Sumerian-Akkadian heartland: Eridu, Uruk, Nippur, Babylon. The northern Akkadian and Assyrian cities (Aššur, Nineveh, Kalḫu) are not in the cosmogonic founding-list. The composition's worldview is Babylon-centred but takes in the canonical southern-Mesopotamian cities as the primordial-cosmos cohort.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the southern-Mesopotamian geographic scope); Bottéro 2001, on Mesopotamian theological geography.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Mesopotamian theological geography

=== Marduk dams the cosmic waters and creates the rivers

After the cities, the composition narrates Marduk's establishment of the dry land by damming the cosmic waters and the creation of the two great rivers — the Idiglat (Tigris) and the Purattu (Euphrates) — by Marduk's name-giving. Marduk names the rivers and sets them in their courses; the act of cosmogonic naming is the act of cosmogonic establishment.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (damming of waters; creation and naming of Tigris and Euphrates); Foster 2005, p. 487; Black & Green 1992, s.v. rivers, Tigris, Euphrates.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 366-375 (rivers)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. rivers

The cosmogonic act of naming — Marduk pronounces the rivers' names and they exist in their named courses — parallels the name-giving that establishes Marduk's own cosmic kingship in *Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII. In both compositions, divine speech (specifically, the act of pronouncing a name) is the mechanism by which cosmic structures come into being.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the naming of the rivers); Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš* Tablet VII fifty-names sequence; the naming-as-cosmogony mechanism); Bottéro 2001, on cosmogonic naming.]

=== Marduk and Aruru create humankind

The composition's anthropogonic moment is shared between Marduk and Aruru. The two deities together create "the seed of mankind" — Aruru as the mother-goddess co-creator, Marduk as the cosmogonic-supreme co-creator. The stated motive is to "settle the gods in the dwelling of (their) heart's delight" — i.e., humans are created so that the gods may dwell in their cult-cities and receive offerings without having to perform the labour of agriculture and provision themselves.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the joint Marduk-Aruru creation of humankind; the gods'-dwelling motive); Foster 2005, p. 487; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Aruru.]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Aruru

[INFERENCE: The collaborative Marduk-Aruru anthropogony of *The Founding of Eridu* is theologically distinct from *Enūma Eliš*'s solo-Marduk anthropogony (where Marduk creates humankind from Qingu's blood after the combat with Tiamat). The two compositions articulate complementary anthropogonies within the late-Babylonian Marduk dossier: the violent-epic register (*Enūma Eliš*) and the bilingual-collaborative register (*The Founding of Eridu*). === The ritual incantation that follows

After the cosmogonic prologue, the tablet continues with the ritual incantation proper. The incantation portion is more heavily damaged than the cosmogony, but it is clear that the cosmogony grounds the magical efficacy of the ritual: Marduk, having been established by the prologue as the cosmic creator, is invoked in the ritual under that authority. The cosmogonic prologue's purpose is, in part, ritual-aetiological — to authorise the magical act that follows.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the ritual-incantation portion of the tablet; cosmogony-as-aetiology framing); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian incantation-prologue genre.]

=== Place in the late-Babylonian cosmogonic dossier

*The Founding of Eridu* belongs to the cluster of compositions Lambert 2013 grouped under the heading "Further Babylonian Creation Tales" (Part III, pp. 281–401). Within the dossier, the composition's closest internal parallel is *The Worm and the Toothache* (Chapter 11) — the other principal incantation-prologue cosmogony in the Akkadian corpus. Both compositions deploy a brief creation narrative as the magical-aetiological ground for a ritual incantation that follows on the same tablet; *The Founding of Eridu* is the larger and more developed instance.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 281–401 (Part III "Further Babylonian Creation Tales", with *The Worm and the Toothache* at a different location in the dossier — to be cross-referenced from chapter 11's brief); Foster 2007, on the incantation-prologue genre.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 281-401
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim on incantation prologues

The composition is significant in attestation among the Lambert 2013 Part III dossier — significant rather than marginal because it is among the principal late-Babylonian creation texts treated in the secondary literature alongside *Enūma Eliš* itself, and because its cosmogonic content (city-foundation, river-naming, joint-anthropogony) is widely cited in discussions of Babylonian creation theology.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (the composition's significance within the dossier); Foster 2005, p. 487 ("Marduk, Creator of the World" within "Myths of Origins"); Bottéro 2001, on Babylonian creation theology.]

There are no Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Kassite, or Neo-Assyrian witnesses to the composition apart from BM 93014 itself, whose precise dating within the late-Babylonian period is not specified beyond the Neo-/Late-Babylonian frame.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (single-tablet transmission; Neo-/Late-Babylonian manuscript dating); Foster 2007, on the limited recensional footprint of compositions in Lambert 2013's Part III dossier.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The Founding of Eridu* is significant within the late-Babylonian Marduk-cosmogony dossier as the principal bilingual instantiation of Marduk's cosmogonic supremacy outside *Enūma Eliš*. Where *Enūma Eliš* deploys the Standard Babylonian epic register and the violent-combat narrative pattern, *The Founding of Eridu* deploys the bilingual incantation-prologue register and the constructive-foundation narrative pattern. The two registers together articulate Marduk's cosmogonic supremacy across the late-Babylonian theological imagination.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 1–277 (*Enūma Eliš* Marduk-elevation theology) and pp. 366–375 (*The Founding of Eridu* bilingual cosmogony); Bottéro 2001, on the complementary registers of Marduk theology in the late-Babylonian dossier.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 1-277 and pp. 366-375
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Marduk theology registers

The composition's preservation in a single tablet for ritual-incantation use, combined with its accessibility through Foster 2005's "Marduk, Creator of the World" translation in *Before the Muses*, has made it one of the most cited late-Babylonian creation texts outside *Enūma Eliš* itself. Its bilingual format also makes it an important witness to the late-Babylonian Sumero-Akkadian scholarly tradition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 366–375 (manuscript-and-ritual context); Foster 2005, p. 487 (translation accessibility); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian Sumero-Akkadian bilingual scribal tradition.]
