=== Source overview

*The Worm and the Toothache* is a short Akkadian incantation against toothache, preserved with a cosmogonic prologue that grounds the magical efficacy of the cure. The principal cuneiform publication is CT 17, plate 50 — R. Campbell Thompson, *Cuneiform Texts from Babylonian Tablets in the British Museum*, Part XVII (London: British Museum, 1903), plate 50 — with multiple duplicates from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh and from Aššur.footnote:[Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, pp. 995–996 (Foster's English translation of the incantation, with the manuscript identification CT 17 50 and reference to the duplicates); Thompson 1903, *Cuneiform Texts from Babylonian Tablets in the British Museum*, Part XVII, plate 50 (the principal cuneiform publication; named-but-flagged — not currently in sources.yaml).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996

The standard accessible modern English translation is Benjamin Foster's *Before the Muses* (3rd ed., CDL Press 2005), pp. 995–996. Foster's translation supersedes Speiser's older translation in *Ancient Near Eastern Texts* (ANET, 1969), which sources.yaml explicitly excludes as a current reference. Lambert's *Babylonian Creation Myths* (2013) may include the composition in his "Mythological Introductions on Creation" section (pp. 399–402, chapter 37, which collects multiple short cosmogonic incantation prologues), but this has not been directly verified at the brief or claims stage. Dalley's *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., 2008) does not contain this composition.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the principal current-reference English translation); Lambert 2013, possibly within pp. 399–402 ("Mythological Introductions on Creation") but unverified; Dalley 2008 (does not contain this composition).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=full table of contents

The composition is preserved on multiple manuscripts that overlap. The principal witness is the Nineveh tablet (CT 17 50, from Aššurbanipal's library); duplicates are also known from Aššur. The composite text is stable across the duplicates; minor lexical variants between Nineveh and Aššur copies appear in the worm's request to Šamaš and Ea, but the cosmogonic chain itself is invariant.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (composite-text status; Nineveh principal witness with Aššur duplicates).]

=== Setting: an incantation against toothache

The composition is not a freestanding cosmogony; it is the cosmogonic prologue of an incantation, deployed in Akkadian medical-magical practice against toothache. The cosmogonic chain establishes the worm's origin and its place in the cosmic order; the etiology grounds the practitioner's authority to address the affliction. Knowing the worm's origin is the practitioner's tool against it.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (incantation-prologue setting; ritual-medical context); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on Mesopotamian medical-magical practice and etiology-as-cure.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Mesopotamian medical-magical practice

The composition's surviving copies are first-millennium Babylonian / Assyrian (the Nineveh witness from Aššurbanipal's seventh-century BCE library; the Aššur duplicate(s) from the same period). The composition's compositional date is older than the surviving copies — a colophon on at least one duplicate notes that the text was copied from an earlier version, indicating the incantation circulated in older recensions before the Neo-Assyrian period.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (manuscript-and-composition dating); Foster 2007, on the late-period Akkadian incantation-tradition transmission.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

=== The seven-step cosmogonic chain

The composition opens with a seven-step cosmogonic sequence: Anu created heaven; heaven created earth; earth created rivers; rivers created canals (irrigation ditches); canals created marsh; marsh created mud; mud created the worm. (Some translations group the sequence as six steps by combining marsh and mud into one node.) The chain is hierarchical and sequential — each step generates the next, descending from the cosmological summit (Anu, the sky-god) to the lowest creature (the worm) by a chain of cosmogonic generation.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the cosmogonic chain; the seven-step sequence); Black & Green 1992, s.v. cosmogony (on hierarchical cosmogonic chains in Mesopotamian literature).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996 (cosmogonic chain)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. cosmogony

The worm — Akkadian *tūltu*, "worm" or "maggot" — is the seventh and final term of the cosmogonic chain. The text personifies the worm as a speaking agent capable of conversation with the gods, but the worm is named after a generic noun (a category of creature) rather than as an individual deity. The personified worm is the protagonist of the etiology; the cosmogonic chain establishes its place in the divinely-ordered world.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the *tūltu* / worm as the etiology's protagonist); Black & Green 1992, s.v. demons (on personified pests in Akkadian incantation literature).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. demons

[INFERENCE: The seven-step cosmogonic chain of *The Worm and the Toothache* sits within a broader Mesopotamian cosmogonic typology — the genealogical / generative chain of cosmic begetting that also underlies *The Theogony of Dunnu* (Chapter 4), the cosmogonic prologues collected in Foster 2005's "Myths of Origins" sub-section, and (further afield) Genesis 1's hierarchical creation order. The pattern stages cosmogony as a chain of begettings or generations rather than as a single fiat-creation by a supreme deity. *The Worm and the Toothache* is the most-anthologised brief instance of this typology because the cosmogonic chain is fully indexed in a self-contained text whose magical-medical purpose makes the chain's structure load-bearing. | basis: the cosmogonic-chain typology is widely attested in Mesopotamian and ancient Near Eastern creation literature (Black & Green 1992, s.v. cosmogony); the *Theogony of Dunnu* (Chapter 4, Lambert 2013 pp. 387–395) is the principal Akkadian-internal comparandum for the genealogical-chain pattern; Foster 2005's "Myths of Origins" sub-section (pp. 487 ff.) collects multiple short cosmogonies that deploy chain structures. | risk: whether the seven-step chain in *The Worm and the Toothache* is a deliberate echo of the longer theogonic chains, or simply an ad-hoc generative sequence built for the magical-medical purpose, is interpretive. The text does not preserve a programmatic statement about its cosmogonic logic; the structural reading is a typological framing.]

=== The worm's lament before Šamaš and Ea

After the cosmogonic chain, the composition narrates the worm's lament. The worm weeps before Šamaš — the sun-god and judge of gods and men — with its tears flowing before Ea, the wise god of fresh waters. The worm asks: "What will you give me to eat? What will you give me to suck on?"footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the worm's lament; Šamaš and Ea as the gods addressed); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Šamaš (the sun-god as judge), s.v. Ea (the wise god of fresh waters).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996 (the worm's lament)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Šamaš, Ea

The gods' answer is the offer of ordinary food: the ripe fig and the apricot. The worm is offered the natural produce of the cosmos as its appropriate diet — fruit, the gift of an ordered cosmogonic system in which each creature has its proper food.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the gods' offer of fig and apricot).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996

=== The worm's defiance and demand

The worm rejects the offer. In the central speech of the composition, the worm declares its refusal: "What good is the ripe fig and the apricot? Lift me up, and let me dwell between the teeth and the gums! I will suck the blood of the tooth, and I will gnaw on the bone of the jaw!"footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the worm's defiance and the demand to dwell in human teeth).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996 (the worm's demand)

The worm's choice — to dwell in human teeth and gnaw their roots rather than to subsist on fruit — is the etiological hinge of the composition. The aetiology of dental caries is established here: the worm has chosen the tooth as its dwelling, against the gods' offer of ordinary food. Dental decay is, in this theological account, the consequence of the worm's defiant election.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the aetiological hinge); Bottéro 2001, on Mesopotamian etiological narratives.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Mesopotamian etiological narratives

=== Ea's curse

Ea responds to the worm's defiance with a curse: "Because you have said this, O worm, may Ea strike you with the might of his hand!" The curse is articulated in the third person — Ea pronounces the curse upon the worm, with the divine power of his own hand as the punitive instrument. The curse converts the etiological narrative into a magical performance: the practitioner who recites the incantation invokes Ea's curse upon the worm in the patient's tooth.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (Ea's curse and its third-person formulation); Bottéro 2001, on the Akkadian magical-curse register.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996 (Ea's curse)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on Akkadian magical-curse register

[INFERENCE: The cosmogonic prologue's function is performative — it establishes the worm's place in the cosmic order so that the practitioner can address the worm under the authority of that order. The cosmogonic chain is not decorative or contemplative; it is part of the spell's mechanism. To know the worm's origin is, in the composition's magical logic, to have power over the worm; reciting the chain invokes that power. | basis: the composition's overall structure (cosmogony → lament → demand → curse → ritual prescription) places the cosmogony as the foundational move of the magical performance; the etiology-as-cure pattern is widespread in Mesopotamian magical-medical practice (Bottéro 2001 on Mesopotamian medicine). The brief structure of the composition — fewer than two dozen lines — leaves no room for non-functional cosmogony. | risk: whether the cosmogonic chain in this composition was experienced by Akkadian practitioners as performatively powerful in the way modern scholarly reading frames it, or simply as a conventional opening for an incantation, is interpretive. The structural-functional reading is a present-day editorial framing supported by parallel Akkadian incantation literature, not a textually programmatic statement in *The Worm and the Toothache* itself.]

=== Ritual prescription

The closing portion of the tablet contains the ritual prescription — instructions to the practitioner for applying the incantation to the patient's tooth. The prescription specifies the ingredients of a paste or compound to be applied (the surviving lines mention beer or other fluid as a vehicle), the recitation of the incantation as the paste is applied, and the number of repetitions. The practitioner thus performs the incantation as part of a medical-magical procedure that combines verbal and material elements.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the ritual-prescription portion of the tablet); Foster 2007, on the late-Babylonian medical-magical incantation-prescription genre; Bottéro 2001, on the practitioner's combined verbal-and-material technique.]

The closing ritual-prescription is partly damaged in some duplicates of CT 17 50 — the precise list of ingredients, the exact words of the recitation directives, and the specific repetition count are not fully recoverable from any single witness. The composite text Foster 2005 reflects represents a partial reconstruction; some details remain indeterminate.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (manuscript damage in the ritual-prescription).]

The first-millennium recension of *The Worm and the Toothache* is preserved on CT 17 50 (Nineveh principal witness, with Aššur duplicates). There are no Old Babylonian or Middle Babylonian witnesses to this specific composition (though the related but distinct OB worm-cosmogony YOS 11, 5 is preserved separately). The textual history of *The Worm and the Toothache* is confined to the first-millennium recension and its first-millennium witnesses.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (first-millennium recension; manuscript history); Hussey-van Dijk YOS 11, 5 (OB variant; named-but-flagged — not currently in sources.yaml).]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The Worm and the Toothache* is significant within the Akkadian corpus as the most-anthologised brief cosmogony — frequently cited in introductions to Akkadian literature, Mesopotamian religion, and the history of medicine. Its compactness (fewer than two dozen lines) combined with its complete cosmogonic chain (Anu to worm) make it the illustrative type for the incantation-prologue cosmogony genre.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the composition's anthological status); Foster 2007, on the incantation-prologue genre.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

The composition's wider cultural reception is unusually long. The "tooth-worm" theory of dental caries — that decay is caused by a worm dwelling in the tooth — persisted in European medical thinking through the 18th century, traceable in part through the long transmission history of Akkadian medical-magical material. The Akkadian text is not the only source for the tooth-worm theory in European medicine, but it is the oldest documented instantiation.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, on the Mesopotamian-to-later-traditions transmission of medical concepts; Foster 2005, pp. 995–996 (the tooth-worm theory's wider transmission history).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=on transmission of medical concepts
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 995-996

Within the Akkadian incantation-prologue cosmogony dossier, the closest internal parallel to *The Worm and the Toothache* is *The Founding of Eridu* (Chapter 10) — the bilingual Sumerian-Akkadian incantation prologue on BM 93014. *The Founding of Eridu* is the larger and more developed instance of the genre (a temple-incantation prologue narrating Marduk's foundation of the cities, rivers, and humankind); *The Worm and the Toothache* is the most-anthologised brief instance (a medical-magical-incantation prologue with a seven-step cosmogonic chain ending in the worm). Together, the two compositions exemplify how Akkadian incantations frame magical acts in cosmogonic narrative.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 487 (*The Founding of Eridu* / "Marduk, Creator of the World") and pp. 995–996 (*The Worm and the Toothache*); Foster 2007, on the incantation-prologue genre as a whole.]
