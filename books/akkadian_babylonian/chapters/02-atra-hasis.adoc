[[ch-02-atra-hasis]]
== Atra-ḫasīs: When the Gods Were Like Men

=== The text and how it survives

_Atra-ḫasīs_ is a three-tablet Akkadian poem composed in the Old Babylonian period, ca. 1700 BCE, transmitted in Middle Babylonian and Standard Babylonian recensions through the first millennium.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, *Atra-Ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood*, pp. 1–30 (introduction); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 227, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 1-30
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 227
The poem is named in Akkadian convention from the hero's epithet — _Atra-ḫasīs_, "the exceedingly wise" — though its actual incipit is _inūma ilū awīlum_, "when the gods were like men."footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, p. 42 (OB Tablet I, line 1); Foster 2005, p. 227.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=p. 42
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 227

The standard critical edition is W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard's _Atra-Ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood_ (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1969; reprinted Eisenbrauns 1999), which provides the cuneiform transliteration, English translation, manuscript score, and commentary.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, full volume; English translation pp. 42–137.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 42-137
The accessible English translation cited throughout for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "Atra-hasis," in _Before the Muses_, third edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 227–280, which incorporates joins and reconstructions published since Lambert & Millard.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 227–280.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 227-280
A third translation, by Stephanie Dalley in _Myths from Mesopotamia_ (revised edition, Oxford World's Classics 2008), pp. 1–38, serves as a triangulation reference.footnote:[Dalley 2008, pp. 1–38.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 1-38

The most complete single witness to the poem is the Old Babylonian Sippar recension preserved on three large tablets known as Manuscript A, copied by the scribe Ipiq-Aya during the reign of Ammi-ṣaduqa (ca. 1646–1626 BCE).footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 31–40 (manuscript catalogue and Ipiq-Aya colophon).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 31-40
Manuscript A's colophon names Ipiq-Aya as the scribe and dates the copy to a specific year of Ammi-ṣaduqa's reign — one of the rare cases in Akkadian literature where a manuscript can be dated precisely from internal evidence.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 31–40, on the colophon and dating; Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 21–24.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 31-40
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 21-24

Middle Babylonian and Standard Babylonian recensions of _Atra-ḫasīs_ are heavily fragmentary; they survive on tablets from Sippar, Nippur, Ur, Aššur, and Nineveh.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 31–40, on the manuscript distribution.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 31-40
The composite text consulted by modern readers is reconstructed primarily from Manuscript A, supplemented by the MB and SB joins.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 31–40 (composite reconstruction methodology); Foster 2007, pp. 21–24, on post-1969 joins.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 31-40
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 21-24
The Borger fragments published since 1969 have clarified the Igigi-rebellion scene and the creation-of-humans passage.footnote:[Foster 2007, pp. 21–24, on the Borger fragments and other post-1969 joins.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 21-24

The flood narrative of Tablet III was independently incorporated into Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian _Gilgameš_, where it is recounted in the first person by the flood survivor Uta-napišti — the Akkadian counterpart of _Atra-ḫasīs_ in that derivative context.footnote:[George 2003, *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic*, pp. 506–512 (on the textual borrowing of *Atra-ḫasīs* III into *Gilgameš* XI).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=pp. 506-512
The textual relationship runs from _Atra-ḫasīs_ into _Gilgameš_ XI, not the other way around: the _Atra-ḫasīs_ flood scene is the prior, independent composition, and _Gilgameš_ XI is a downstream borrowing.footnote:[George 2003, pp. 506–512, on the direction of borrowing.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=pp. 506-512

The story, as Manuscript A and its later supplements preserve it, goes as follows.

=== Setting: two divine orders and the labour of digging the canals

The poem opens with two orders of gods at work in a cosmos that has already been organised. The Anunnaki — the ruling order of gods — and the Igigi — the lower order of working gods — divide the cosmic labour between them.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 42–44 (OB Tablet I, lines 1–18); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Anunnaki, Igigi.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 42-44
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Anunnaki
In the cosmic division attested at the poem's opening, Anu rules the heavens, Enlil rules the earth, and Ea rules the freshwater Apsû.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 42–44 (OB Tablet I, lines 1–18); Foster 2005, pp. 228–229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 42-44
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 228-229

The Anunnaki delegate the labour of digging the cosmic canals — the Tigris, the Euphrates, the irrigation network — to the Igigi, who do the work for the Anunnaki's benefit.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 42–46 (OB Tablet I, lines 18–40); Foster 2005, p. 229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 42-46
This is a cosmos in which the upper gods do not labour. The lower gods do the work and the upper gods enjoy the produce.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, pp. 219–229 (creation of humans and the flood).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229

=== Tablet I: the rebellion of the Igigi and the creation of humanity

[LACUNA: OB Tablet I, lines 1–37 (the opening cosmic-labour scene) | source: Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 42–46 | scholarly_reconstruction: filled from Sippar duplicates and a Tablet I fragment from Nippur; Foster 2005 incorporates additional post-1969 joins from the Borger fragments]

Years pass — by some readings of the broken opening, forty years of the Igigi's labour; by others, the labour extends to three thousand six hundred years before the rebellion breaks out.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 44–46 (OB Tablet I, lines 36–43, partly reconstructed; the textual problems concerning the duration of the Igigi's labour are summarised here); Foster 2005, p. 229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 44-46
After the long labour, the Igigi grow exhausted and break.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 46–48 (OB Tablet I, lines 39–60); Foster 2005, p. 229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 46-48
In a coordinated act of rebellion, the Igigi burn their tools — their hoes, baskets, and digging implements — at midnight.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 46–48 (OB Tablet I, lines 60–80); Foster 2005, pp. 229–230.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 46-48
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 229-230
They march in arms to the gate of Enlil's house and surround it.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 48–50 (OB Tablet I, lines 80–104); Foster 2005, p. 230.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 48-50

Enlil's vizier Nusku alerts him to the rebellion at the gate.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 48–50 (OB Tablet I, lines 80–104); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Nusku.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 48-50
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Nusku
Enlil summons Anu to descend from the heavens; Ea is also called.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 50–52 (OB Tablet I, lines 104–140); Foster 2005, p. 230.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 50-52
The three high gods — Anu, Enlil, Ea — convene in council and question Nusku, who reports the Igigi's grievance: the labour is intolerable.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 52–54 (OB Tablet I, lines 140–180); Foster 2005, pp. 230–231.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 52-54

Ea proposes a solution: the gods will create a substitute workforce. Humans will be made to do the work the Igigi have refused.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 54–58 (OB Tablet I, lines 180–222); Foster 2005, p. 231.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 54-58
Mami — also called Nintu and Bēlet-ilī ("Lady of the gods") — the mother goddess, is summoned to perform the creation.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 56–58 (OB Tablet I, lines 189–230); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Mami; Foster 2005, p. 231.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 56-58
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Mami

The creation method is specified: humans are to be made from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 56–60 (OB Tablet I, lines 200–230); Foster 2005, pp. 231–232.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 56-60
The god slain for the purpose is named Wê — sometimes Wê-ila — a god whose role in the divine order before this moment is not specified in the text.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 58–60 (OB Tablet I, lines 220–235); Foster 2005, p. 232 (printed as "Aw-ilu"); Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229. The same deity is variously transliterated as Wê, Wê-ila, Aw-ilu, Geshtu-E, or Ilawela in modern editions, all reading the same cuneiform name.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 58-60
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229
Wê is killed by the assembled gods; his flesh and blood are mixed with clay by Mami.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 58–60 (OB Tablet I, lines 220–235); Foster 2005, p. 232.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 58-60

From this mixture humans receive a divine component: the _ṭēmu_, the rationality or animating spirit, derives from the slain god's flesh.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 58–60 (OB Tablet I, line 223); Foster 2005, p. 232; Kvanvig 2011, *Primeval History*, on the *ṭēmu* concept.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 58-60
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=on the *ṭēmu* concept
A second concept derives from the slain god's blood: the _eṭemmu_, the ghost or shade that persists after a human's death — humans are mortal, but a part of them is divine and outlasts their body.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 58–60 (OB Tablet I, line 217); Foster 2005, p. 232; Kvanvig 2011, on the *eṭemmu* concept. The flesh→*ṭēmu* / blood→*eṭemmu* attribution given here follows Bottéro 2001 and Kvanvig 2011; alternative readings differ on which physical component yields which divine quality.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 58-60
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=on the *eṭemmu* concept

Mami spits on the clay-and-blood mixture and pinches off seven male and seven female pieces — the first humans, made in pairs.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 60–62 (OB Tablet I, lines 249–276); Foster 2005, p. 233.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 60-62
The first humans are placed in the temple court. They take up the labour the Igigi have refused: digging the canals, cultivating the fields, providing offerings for the gods.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 62–66 (OB Tablet I, lines 276–340); Foster 2005, pp. 233–234.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 62-66
The Igigi are formally relieved of their labour; the humans take their place.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 64–66 (OB Tablet I, lines 320–340); Foster 2005, p. 234.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 64-66

=== Tablet II: noise, plague, drought, and famine

Time passes. The humans multiply.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 66–68 (OB Tablet II, lines 1–8); Foster 2005, p. 234.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 66-68
The Akkadian word for the trouble that follows is _rigmu_ — clamour, noise — sometimes paired with _ḫubūru_, the din of a crowded city. The growing human population produces _rigmu_ that disturbs Enlil's rest.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 66–68 (OB Tablet II, lines 1–11); Foster 2005, p. 234; Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229, on the *rigmu / ḫubūru* motif.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 66-68
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229
The noise complaint structurally echoes Apsû's complaint about the noise of the younger gods in _Enūma Eliš_ Tablet I. In _Atra-ḫasīs_, the noisemakers are humans rather than gods, and the offended party is Enlil rather than Apsû.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229 (on the noise motif as a Mesopotamian theological commonplace); Foster 2005, p. 234.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229

Enlil resolves to reduce the human population. The first instrument is plague.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 68–70 (OB Tablet II, lines 11–55); Foster 2005, pp. 234–235.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 68-70
Enlil's plague is sent against humanity. The hero Atra-ḫasīs ("the exceedingly wise") is introduced at this point as a man of unusual piety, devoted to Ea.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 68–70 (OB Tablet II, lines 11–32); Foster 2005, pp. 234–235.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 68-70
Atra-ḫasīs appeals to Ea, his patron, who instructs him in the ritual countermeasure: have the people make offerings to Namtar, the plague-god, and the plague will be lifted.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 70–72 (OB Tablet II, lines 32–55); Foster 2005, p. 235; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Namtar.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 70-72
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Namtar
The plague is lifted. The humans return to multiplying.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 70–72 (OB Tablet II, lines 32–55); Foster 2005, p. 235.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 70-72

After another long interval, the _rigmu_ returns. Enlil tries again, this time with drought.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 72–76 (OB Tablet II, lines 55–110); Foster 2005, pp. 235–236.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 72-76
Adad — storm god, lord of thunder and the rains that bring or break the harvest — is instructed to withhold his rain. The earth dries up and the harvests fail.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 72–76 (OB Tablet II, lines 55–110); Foster 2005, p. 235; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Adad.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 72-76
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Adad
Atra-ḫasīs again appeals to Ea, who instructs the people to make offerings to Adad — the same ritual logic as before. The drought is lifted.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 74–78 (OB Tablet II, lines 80–140); Foster 2005, p. 236.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 74-78

[LACUNA: OB Tablet II, columns vi–viii (the drought-and-famine sequence) | source: Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 78–86 | scholarly_reconstruction: partly filled from Sippar duplicates, Nippur fragments, and Aššur copies; the precise sequence of the third chastisement (famine, salt-soil) is reconstructed]

[RECONSTRUCTION: The third population-control measure — a famine compounded by salt-poisoning of the soil — is partially reconstructed from secondary witnesses. | gap_source: OB Tablet II columns vi–viii | fill_source: Sippar duplicates, Nippur fragments, Aššur copies, and the post-1969 Borger fragments collated by Lambert & Millard 1969 and Foster 2005 | confidence: medium]

A third chastisement follows: the heavens are sealed against rain again, and the earth is salted so that crops will not grow. The famine is described in graphic terms — the people consume their own children.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 78–86 (OB Tablet II, columns vi–viii, partly reconstructed); Foster 2005, pp. 236–237.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 78-86
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 236-237
Atra-ḫasīs again intervenes through Ea. The third chastisement is lifted, but Enlil's frustration with the cycle of rebellion-and-rescue mounts.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 84–86 (OB Tablet II, end); Foster 2005, p. 237.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 84-86

The cycle of plague–drought–famine in Tablet II is one of the poem's signature structures. Each chastisement is followed by Atra-ḫasīs's appeal, Ea's counsel, the humans' compliance, and the lifting of the chastisement.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229 (on the structural cycle); Foster 2005, pp. 234–237.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229

=== Tablet III: the flood

Enlil's frustration reaches its limit. He resolves on a final solution: a flood that will destroy humanity entirely.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 86–90 (OB Tablet III, lines 1–25); Foster 2005, pp. 237–238.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 86-90
To prevent Ea from warning Atra-ḫasīs again, Enlil compels all the gods to swear an oath of silence. None may reveal the flood plan to humanity.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 88–92 (OB Tablet III, lines 18–35); Foster 2005, p. 238; Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 88-92

Ea, technically bound by the oath, evades it through a famous rhetorical device. He addresses not Atra-ḫasīs himself but the wall of Atra-ḫasīs's reed-hut.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 88–92 (OB Tablet III, lines 18–35); Foster 2005, p. 238; Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 88-92
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 238
In the address to the reed-hut, Ea instructs the wall — and Atra-ḫasīs, listening on the other side — to dismantle the house, build a boat, and load it with the seed of all living things.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 90–92 (OB Tablet III, lines 18–35); Foster 2005, p. 238.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 90-92
The reed-hut device preserves the technicality of Ea's oath. He has not addressed a human; he has spoken to a wall. That a human happens to overhear is, by oath-logic, not Ea's responsibility.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229 (on the reed-hut speech as legal-rhetorical device); Foster 2005, p. 238.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229

Atra-ḫasīs builds the boat under Ea's instruction.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 92–94 (OB Tablet III, lines 35–55); Foster 2005, pp. 238–239.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 92-94

[LACUNA: OB Tablet III, columns iv–vi (the flood proper and the post-flood sacrifice scene) | source: Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 94–104 | scholarly_reconstruction: partly filled from later SB recensions and cross-checked against Gilgameš XI, which preserves an independent retelling of the same scene]

[RECONSTRUCTION: The boat's specifications and the flood's specific duration are partially reconstructed. | gap_source: OB Tablet III columns iv–vi | fill_source: later SB recensions of *Atra-ḫasīs* and the parallel scene in Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš* Tablet XI (a downstream borrowing); cross-checked but not used as a donor for content the OB *Atra-ḫasīs* itself does not attest | confidence: medium]

Atra-ḫasīs loads the boat with his family and with animals — the seed of every living thing.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 92–94 (OB Tablet III, lines 35–55); Foster 2005, p. 239.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 92-94
The flood comes. Adad's storm rages for seven days; the seven-days-and-seven-nights doublet familiar from the parallel scene in _Gilgameš_ XI is not preserved as such in the partly-reconstructed OB _Atra-ḫasīs_ III, though it is structurally similar.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 94–98 (OB Tablet III, lines 55–115, partly reconstructed); Foster 2005, pp. 239–240; cross-checked against George 2003 on the Gilgameš XI parallel.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=on the Gilgameš XI parallel
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 94-98

The gods themselves cower at the violence of the flood. Mami, the mother goddess who created humanity, laments the destruction of her children.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 96–100 (OB Tablet III, lines 90–135); Foster 2005, pp. 239–240.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 96-100
The lament of Mami is one of the poem's most-quoted passages. She mourns that her offspring have become "like flies on the river" and reproaches the gods who allowed the flood.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 96–100 (OB Tablet III, the Mami lament); Foster 2005, pp. 239–240; Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 96-100

The flood subsides. The boat comes to rest.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 98–100 (OB Tablet III, lines 115–140); Foster 2005, p. 240.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 98-100
Atra-ḫasīs disembarks and offers a sacrifice. The gods, who have been without offerings throughout the flood, gather around the smoke "like flies."footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 98–102 (OB Tablet III, lines 130–155); Foster 2005, p. 240.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 98-102
The "pleasing aroma" of the sacrifice — Akkadian _iriš tābi_ in the form preserved in the text, lemma form _erēšu ṭābu_ — is accepted by the gods, who recognise that they have starved themselves by destroying the only beings who fed them.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 100–102 (OB Tablet III, lines 145–160); Foster 2005, p. 240; Kvanvig 2011, on *erēšu ṭābu* / *iriš tābi* and its biblical reception (Hebrew *rêaḥ nîḥôaḥ*).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 100-102
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=on *erēšu ṭābu*

Enlil arrives at the sacrificial scene and is enraged that any human has survived. He demands to know who broke the oath.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 100–102 (OB Tablet III, lines 160–180); Foster 2005, pp. 240–241.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 100-102
Ea acknowledges his role and defends it. The flood was disproportionate — a punishment that destroyed the innocent along with the noisy. Enlil should not have decreed it.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 102–104 (OB Tablet III, lines 175–215); Foster 2005, p. 241.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 102-104

Ea proposes new arrangements that will control human population without destroying it. Mortality, infant mortality, infertility, and certain priestly classes who cannot bear children will be instituted.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 102–106 (OB Tablet III, lines 215–250); Foster 2005, p. 241; Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 102-106
This is the poem's anthropological-aetiological climax: the institution of mortality and birth-control as theological responses to the human-multiplication problem. Death is not a punishment for sin in this theology. It is a structural solution to a population-management problem the gods adopted after the flood.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229; Kvanvig 2011, on the post-flood arrangements and the theology of mortality.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 219-229
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=on the post-flood arrangements

[INFERENCE: The birth-control measures of the post-flood council are the poem's structural answer to the question its first two tablets raise: how can the gods enjoy human labour without humans multiplying past the point of bearable noise? The answer is mortality. | basis: the explicit linkage in OB Tablet III lines 215–250 between the human-multiplication problem and the new measures; Lambert & Millard 1969's commentary; Bottéro 2001 pp. 219–229 on the structural reading. | risk: the structural reading is interpretive; the poem itself does not state the principle quite this baldly, though every commentator since Lambert & Millard has read it this way.]

The poem ends with the new arrangements ratified.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 104–106 (OB Tablet III closing); Foster 2005, p. 241.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 104-106
The closing colophon of MS A names Ipiq-Aya as the scribe and gives the date of copying — late in the reign of Ammi-ṣaduqa.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 31–40 (colophon and date); Foster 2007, pp. 21–24.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 31-40

=== Variants and recensional history

[VARIANT: primary=OB Sippar Manuscript A (Ipiq-Aya, ca. 1635 BCE) — the most complete witness, the basis of Lambert & Millard's composite text | alt=MB and SB recensions (heavily fragmentary; Sippar, Nippur, Aššur, Nineveh) — preserve a slightly different framing of flood causation, with the noise-pollution motif sharper in OB and more theological in SB | chosen: OB MS A composite | reason: the OB recension is the most complete and earliest witness; MB and SB recensions are partial and later, and are used to fill gaps rather than to supply a substantively different reading]

The MB and SB recensions of _Atra-ḫasīs_ preserve a slightly different theological framing of the flood causation — the _rigmu_ / noise motif is sharper in the OB recension, where it is the immediate trigger; in SB the framing is more theological, with Enlil's anger framed in more abstract terms.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 31–40 (recension comparison); Foster 2007, pp. 21–24.]

Foster 2005's translation incorporates joins published after 1969, including the Borger fragments, which clarify the structure of the Igigi rebellion and the creation-of-humans passage. Where Foster's reading materially supplements Lambert & Millard's, the chapter follows Foster, with the Lambert & Millard reading noted in a footnote.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 227–280; Foster 2007, pp. 21–24, on the post-1969 joins.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

_Atra-ḫasīs_ is one of the load-bearing texts of Akkadian literature. Its three tablets bundle anthropogony, theodicy, and a flood narrative into a single composition, and the latter two themes echo through the rest of the corpus.footnote:[Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 1–30 (introduction); Bottéro 2001, pp. 219–229; Kvanvig 2011, passim on the primaeval-history reading.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-millard-1969 ; loc=pp. 1-30
// EVIDENCE: source_id=kvanvig-2011 ; loc=passim

Tablet III's flood scene was independently adopted into the Standard Babylonian _Gilgameš_ (Tablet XI), where Uta-napišti retells it in the first person. The textual relationship is asymmetric: _Atra-ḫasīs_ is prior, _Gilgameš_ XI is a downstream borrowing.footnote:[George 2003, pp. 506–512; Foster 2005, p. 227.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=george-2003 ; loc=pp. 506-512
The Sumerian _Eridu Genesis_ (ETCSL 1.7.4) preserves a different flood narrative with Ziusudra as the survivor; the Akkadian _Atra-ḫasīs_ is not a translation of the Sumerian text but an independent composition on a shared theme. The Sumerian material is comparative background, not a donor source for the Akkadian content.footnote:[ETCSL 1.7.4 (Sumerian Eridu Genesis), cited as comparative; Lambert & Millard 1969, pp. 1–30 on the relationship.]
