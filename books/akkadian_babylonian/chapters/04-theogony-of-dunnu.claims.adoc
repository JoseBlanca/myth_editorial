=== Source overview

*The Theogony of Dunnu* is a short Akkadian theogonic-cosmogonic poem preserved on a single Late Babylonian manuscript: BM 74329, published as CT 46, 43 (British Museum, *Cuneiform Texts from Babylonian Tablets in the British Museum*, vol. 46, plate 43).footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, pp. 387–395 (text, translation, commentary, and manuscript description); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 487, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 387-395
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487

The composition has no ancient title. The modern title is editorial, taken from the place-name *Dunnu* used as the setting of the cosmogony.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 387; Foster 2005, p. 487.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 387

The standard critical edition is W. G. Lambert's treatment in *Babylonian Creation Myths* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 16, Eisenbrauns 2013), pp. 387–395, which provides the cuneiform transliteration, English translation, and commentary.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 387–395 (full edition).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 387-395

The accessible English translation cited throughout for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "Theogony of Dunnu," in *Before the Muses*, third edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 487–489.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 487–489.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 487-489

A third translation, by Stephanie Dalley in *Myths from Mesopotamia* (revised edition, Oxford World's Classics 2008), pp. 278–281, serves as a triangulation reference.footnote:[Dalley 2008, pp. 278–281.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 278-281

The single manuscript is Late Babylonian in date; the underlying composition may be earlier, but no Old Babylonian or Middle Babylonian witnesses survive.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 387 (manuscript dating); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, on the late-Babylonian cosmogonic dossier.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 387
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=passim

The opening lines and the closing succession episodes are partly broken. The cycle's later generations are reconstructed from internal context rather than from parallel manuscripts.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 387–388 (manuscript condition); Foster 2005, p. 487.]

=== Setting: Dunnu, the obscure Mesopotamian city

The cosmogony is set in Dunnu, an obscure Mesopotamian place-name attested in Old Babylonian and later administrative records.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 387–388 (on the place-name and possible identifications); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Dunnu.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 387-388
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Dunnu

Dunnu is a real Babylonian town, not a fictional cosmic setting; Lambert 2013 discusses possible identifications, though the precise location remains uncertain.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 388 (on the identification of Dunnu).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 388

The choice of Dunnu as the cosmogonic setting is itself unusual. Most Akkadian creation poems are set in Babylon (Esagil) or Eridu; the Theogony of Dunnu locates its action in a peripheral town, which may reflect a local theological tradition rather than the central Babylonian theology of *Enūma Eliš*.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 388 and commentary; Foster 2005, p. 487, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 388

=== The genealogical narrative: violent succession across generations

The Theogony of Dunnu is structured as a genealogical cosmogony. Successive generations of cosmic deities are produced by intercourse, and each new generation displaces the previous one through killing or marriage.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 388–390 (structural overview); Bottéro 2001, pp. 92–97 (on the variant theogonic traditions); Foster 2005, p. 487, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 388-390
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 92-97

The first father in the cycle is Ḫarab — the deified plough — and the first mother is Earth (Akkadian *erṣetu*).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 388–390 (Tablet I opening, partly reconstructed); Foster 2005, p. 487.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 388-390

The cosmic deities at the head of the genealogy are agricultural-pastoral entities: the plough turns the earth, the earth produces sea, the sea produces cattle. Cosmogony is rooted in agrarian work.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 392 (commentary on the agrarian frame); Foster 2005, p. 487.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 392

Ḫarab and Earth produce a daughter, Sea (Akkadian *tâmtu*).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 388–390 (the first generation); Foster 2005, p. 488.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 388-390

In the next generation Ḫarab — the father — marries his daughter Sea. The incestuous union produces a son: Sumuqan, also written Šakkan, the cattle-god.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 388–390 (second generation; the incestuous succession); Foster 2005, p. 488.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 388-390

Sumuqan in turn marries his mother Earth — another incestuous union — and then kills his father Ḫarab and takes his place.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 388–392 (the killing of Ḫarab); Foster 2005, p. 488.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 388-392

The pattern repeats. Each new generation marries within the family (mother, sister, daughter) and kills its parent or displaces its predecessor. The succession is dynastic and violent throughout.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 388–394 (the succession pattern); Foster 2005, pp. 488–489; Bottéro 2001, pp. 92–97.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 388-394

A fourth generation introduces Lahar, the shepherd-god, who continues the pattern.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 392–394 (Lahar generation); Foster 2005, p. 489; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Lahar.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 392-394
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Lahar

The text continues through several more generations, each repeating the marry-and-replace cycle. Specific later names are partly broken in the manuscript and are reconstructed from internal context.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 392–394 (later generations, partly reconstructed); Foster 2005, p. 489.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 392-394

The closing of the manuscript is broken. The poem may have continued with further generations, or may have terminated at the figure of Enlil; the surviving text does not allow a confident reconstruction of the ending.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 394–395 (manuscript end, broken); Foster 2005, p. 489.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 394-395

=== The agrarian-cosmic identification

A distinctive feature of the Theogony of Dunnu is its identification of cosmic deities with agricultural and pastoral elements. The first father is the plough; the first mother is the earth; their daughter is the sea; their grandson is the cattle-god; a later figure is the shepherd-god.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 392 (the agrarian frame); Foster 2005, p. 487, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 392

This is a cosmogony rooted in the work of the Mesopotamian alluvial plain: the plough that breaks the earth, the rivers and sea that the rains and floods produce, the herds that follow on cultivated land, the shepherds that tend them. The cosmic order described is the order of the agricultural year, projected onto a divine genealogy.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 392 commentary; Foster 2005, p. 487.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 392

[INFERENCE: The agrarian-cosmic identification distinguishes the Theogony of Dunnu from *Enūma Eliš* and other major Akkadian cosmogonies, which typically begin from primordial waters or undifferentiated matter rather than from the implements and animals of cultivation. | basis: Lambert 2013 p. 392 commentary; Foster 2005 headnote; Bottéro 2001 pp. 92–97 on the comparative position of Dunnu within Mesopotamian cosmogonic traditions. | risk: the contrast with *Enūma Eliš* is the chapter's framing rather than the text's explicit claim; some readers might emphasise the structural similarities (cosmic-violent-succession) over the agrarian-versus-watery contrast.]

=== Variants

The Theogony of Dunnu has no parallel manuscripts. The single witness BM 74329 is the only surviving copy of the composition, and there are no recensional alternates to record.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 387–388 (single-manuscript transmission); Foster 2005, p. 487.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

The Theogony of Dunnu is marginal in attestation — a single late tablet — but significant in comparative scholarship. It is the principal Akkadian witness to a "violent succession" cosmogony pattern, and its similarity to the Hurro-Hittite *Kumarbi Cycle* (Anu castrated by Kumarbi; Kumarbi displaced by Teshub) and to Hesiod's *Theogony* (Ouranos castrated by Kronos; Kronos overthrown by Zeus) has been the basis of scholarship on the transmission of theogonic motifs across the eastern Mediterranean.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 393 (comparative discussion); Foster 2005, p. 487, headnote; Hoffner 1998, *Hittite Myths*, on the Kumarbi Cycle as a comparative parallel.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 393
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 487
// EVIDENCE: source_id=hoffner-1998 ; loc=Kumarbi Cycle

The standard direction of comparison runs from Mesopotamian (Theogony of Dunnu) through Hurro-Hittite (Kumarbi) to Greek (Hesiod's *Theogony*), with the Hurrian and Hittite material as the proposed mediating layer; this is the principal case taken up in the influences chapter at the end of this book.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 393; Hoffner 1998, on the Hurrian intermediary.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 393

The Theogony of Dunnu is therefore one of the most consequential of the small Akkadian creation-dossier texts. Its narrative footprint is short, but its comparative reach is substantial.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 387–395 (full edition); Foster 2005, p. 487; Bottéro 2001, pp. 92–97.]
