=== Source overview

Enūma Eliš is a seven-tablet Akkadian poem composed in the late second millennium BCE, conventionally taken as the canonical Babylonian creation epic.footnote:[Lambert 2013, *Babylonian Creation Myths*, introduction (pp. xiii–xx) and pp. 3–25 on the manuscript tradition; Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, p. 436, headnote.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. xiii-xx, 3-25

The poem's title in Akkadian convention is taken from its opening words, *enūma eliš* — "when on high".footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 50 (Tablet I, line 1); Foster 2005, p. 439.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 50

The standard critical edition is W. G. Lambert's *Babylonian Creation Myths* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 16, Eisenbrauns 2013), which provides the cuneiform transliteration, an English translation, the manuscript score, and full philological commentary across 600 pages.footnote:[Lambert 2013, full volume; English translation pp. 50–134; commentary pp. 449–488.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 50-134, 449-488

The accessible English translation cited throughout this chapter for narrative passages is Benjamin Foster's "Epic of Creation," in *Before the Muses*, 3rd edition (CDL Press 2005), pp. 436–486.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 436–486.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 436-486

A third translation by Stephanie Dalley in *Myths from Mesopotamia* (rev. ed., Oxford World's Classics 2008), pp. 228–277, serves as a triangulation reference where Lambert and Foster diverge.footnote:[Dalley 2008, pp. 228–277.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 228-277

Lambert's manuscript catalogue lists more than seventy witnesses to Enūma Eliš.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3–15, manuscript list.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-15

The witnesses are distributed across Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian library and temple copies of the first millennium BCE, with the latest copies dating to the Late Babylonian and Seleucid periods.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3–15, on the manuscript distribution.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-15

The Neo-Assyrian witnesses include copies from Aššurbanipal's library at Nineveh and from temple libraries at Aššur, Kalḫu, and Sultantepe.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3–15, on the Neo-Assyrian witnesses.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-15

The Neo-Babylonian witnesses come from Babylon, Borsippa, and Sippar.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3–15, on the Neo-Babylonian witnesses.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-15

A small number of Late Babylonian witnesses come from Uruk and continue into the Achaemenid and Seleucid periods.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3–15, on the Late Babylonian witnesses.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-15

The composition's date of authorship is debated, with the prevailing view placing it in the late second millennium BCE, most commonly under Nebuchadnezzar I of the Second Dynasty of Isin (reigned ca. 1125–1104 BCE).footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction on the dating debate; Foster 2005, p. 436.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=introduction

The Nebuchadnezzar I commissioning hypothesis ties the composition to that king's recovery of Marduk's cult-statue from Elam and to a wider programme of Babylonian theological assertion.footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction on the Nebuchadnezzar I hypothesis.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=introduction

The composite text consulted by modern readers is stitched together from these many witnesses; no single tablet preserves the entire seven-tablet poem.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 3–25, on the composite reconstruction methodology.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 3-25

Tablet V is the most damaged of the seven, with multiple stretches of cosmographic detail recoverable only from parallel manuscripts and partial reconstructions.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 98–113 (Tablet V); commentary pp. 472–478.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 98-113

Tablet VII's recitation of Marduk's fifty names varies in line order across some manuscripts and is partially reconstructed for one or two epithets.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 121–134 (Tablet VII); commentary pp. 482–488.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 121-134

The other six tablets are preserved either fully or to a degree where the narrative is recoverable without significant guesswork.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 50–98 and 113–121, on Tablets I–IV and VI.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 50-98, 113-121

George Smith of the British Museum first published cuneiform fragments of the poem in 1875–76, with a March 1875 announcement in the *Daily Telegraph* and the formal scholarly edition appearing as *The Chaldean Account of Genesis* in 1876; modern scholarship has continuously engaged with the text since then.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. xiii–xx, on the history of scholarship.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. xiii-xx

=== Ritual context: the Akītu festival

Enūma Eliš was recited liturgically on the fourth day of the Babylonian *akītu* festival, the New Year ritual at Babylon, by the high priest before the cult-image of Marduk in the Esagil temple.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 449–465, on the *akītu* recitation; Bottéro 2001, pp. 234–243.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 449-465
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 234-243

The *akītu* festival took place over twelve days at the start of the Babylonian year (1–12 Nisannu).footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 220–243, on the *akītu* festival as a whole.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 220-243

The recitation of Enūma Eliš during the festival was understood not as a commemoration of a past event but as a renewal of cosmic order for the coming year.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 234–243; Lambert 2013, pp. 449–465.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 234-243

The ritual texts that attest to the *akītu* recitation are themselves Late Babylonian and survive principally from temple archives at Babylon and Uruk.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 449–465, on the ritual texts.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 449-465

The performative context determined how the poem was read in antiquity: the recitation re-enacted Marduk's combat with Tiamat and re-installed his cosmic kingship for the year ahead.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 234–243; Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191, on Marduk's kingship as the political-theological centre of the poem.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 234-243
// EVIDENCE: source_id=jacobsen-1976 ; loc=pp. 165-191

The Babylonian king's earthly sovereignty was re-grounded annually through the *akītu* by association with Marduk's cosmic sovereignty.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 234–243; Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 234-243

In the festival's "humiliation" rite on day five, the king of Babylon was momentarily stripped of insignia by the *šešgallu* priest, slapped, and made to swear an oath of innocence — the temporal kingship symbolically suspended before Marduk's cosmic kingship was re-affirmed.footnote:[Bottéro 2001, pp. 234–243.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 234-243

=== Setting and theogonic context

Marduk is the patron god of Babylon (Akkadian *Bāb-ilī*, "the gate of the gods"), originally a city-god of relatively limited regional importance who rose in the second millennium BCE to become the chief god of the Babylonian pantheon.footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction on Marduk's rise; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Marduk.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=introduction
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Marduk

Marduk's elevation in Enūma Eliš is the narrative complement of Babylon's historical elevation as imperial capital: the text and the political claim are co-constituted.footnote:[Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191; Lambert 2013, introduction.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=jacobsen-1976 ; loc=pp. 165-191

In the Mesopotamian theogonic background, Marduk's father is Ea, god of wisdom and the freshwater Apsû.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 79–84); Foster 2005, pp. 439–440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

Ea is also called Nudimmud, an epithet of his used throughout the poem.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, line 81); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Ea.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Ea

Ea in Akkadian context corresponds to Sumerian Enki, but Enūma Eliš is an Akkadian composition and uses the Akkadian theonym Ea throughout.footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction; Foster 2005, p. 439.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=introduction

Marduk's mother is Damkina, the consort of Ea.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 81–84); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Damkina.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Damkina

Enūma Eliš is not a translation of any Sumerian original; no single Sumerian composition corresponds to it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction; Black et al. 2004 (cited as comparative substrate, not as primary).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=introduction

The Sumerian theogonic background — fragmentary god-lists from Nippur and elsewhere — provides theological context for Enūma Eliš but no direct narrative source.footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction; Black et al. 2004 cited comparatively.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=introduction

=== Tablet I: theogony and the binding of Apsû

The poem opens before any naming or organisation: "When on high the heaven had not been named, when below the earth had not been called by name."footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 50 (Tablet I, lines 1–2); Foster 2005, p. 439.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 50

In the primordial state, two waters intermingle: Apsû the freshwater and Tiamat the saltwater, mother and father of all that will come.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 50 (Tablet I, lines 3–8); Foster 2005, p. 439.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 50

Tiamat is presented in Tablet I as a primordial mother before she becomes an antagonist; her enmity is a later development, not her original character.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 50–54 (Tablet I); Jacobsen 1976, pp. 168–172.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 50-54

Mummu is named in Tablet I as the vizier of Apsû.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 50 (Tablet I, line 4); Foster 2005, p. 439.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 50

Successive generations of gods are produced from the mingling of the primordial waters: first Lahmu and Lahamu (a divine pair), then Anšar and Kišar (the celestial and earthly horizons), then Anu (the sky), then Ea-Nudimmud.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 50–54 (Tablet I, lines 9–22); Foster 2005, pp. 439–440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 50-54

Anšar and Kišar are the parents of Anu in this genealogy.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 52 (Tablet I, lines 12–14); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Anšar.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 52
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Anšar

The younger gods make noise that disturbs Apsû in his repose.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 54 (Tablet I, lines 21–28); Foster 2005, p. 440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 54

Apsû resolves to destroy his offspring to restore his quiet.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 54 (Tablet I, lines 39–46); Foster 2005, p. 440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 54

Tiamat objects to her consort's plan; she protests in mother-mode against the destruction of their own offspring.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 54 (Tablet I, lines 41–46); Foster 2005, p. 440; Jacobsen 1976, pp. 168–172.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 54

Mummu encourages Apsû to act despite Tiamat's objection.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 54 (Tablet I, lines 47–54); Foster 2005, p. 440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 54

Ea (Nudimmud) hears of the plot through his wisdom.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 60–66); Foster 2005, p. 440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

Ea recites a sleep-spell against Apsû, drawing on his ritual mastery as god of incantation.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 60–66); Foster 2005, p. 440.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

Ea binds Apsû in sleep, removes his crown and divine aura, and kills him.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 67–70); Foster 2005, pp. 440–441.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

Mummu is bound in the same action.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 71–72); Foster 2005, p. 441.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

Ea takes up residence in Apsû's body, which becomes the Apsû — the cosmic freshwater under the earth — and his dwelling.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 71–78); Foster 2005, p. 441.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

In this newly established Apsû-dwelling, Ea begets Marduk on his consort Damkina.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 56 (Tablet I, lines 79–84); Foster 2005, p. 441.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 56

Marduk's birth is described with a hymn of his exceptional dimensions: he is exalted in stature, his eyes flash fire, four ears sprout from his head, four eyes shine.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 56–58 (Tablet I, lines 85–104); Foster 2005, pp. 441–442.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 56-58

Anu, his grandfather, tests Marduk by giving him the four winds to play with as toys.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 58 (Tablet I, lines 105–110); Foster 2005, p. 442.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 58

Marduk plays with the winds, stirring up dust storms over the deep.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 58 (Tablet I, lines 105–110); Foster 2005, p. 442.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 58

The dust afflicts Tiamat in her chamber, and the gods who side with the elder generation press her to act.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 60 (Tablet I, lines 109–122); Foster 2005, pp. 442–443.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 60

The gods who side with Tiamat invoke the killing of Apsû as the precipitating injustice.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 60–62 (Tablet I, lines 122–134); Foster 2005, pp. 442–443.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 60-62

Tiamat is roused at last; she resolves to make war on the younger gods.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 62 (Tablet I, lines 132–143); Foster 2005, p. 443.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 62

Tiamat creates eleven monsters as her army; the canonical eleven are individually named in the text: Bašmu (Venomous Snake), Ušumgallu (Great Dragon), Mušmaḫḫū (Exalted Serpent), Mušḫuššu (Furious Snake), Laḫmu (Hairy Hero), Ugallu (Great Demon), Uridimmu (Mad Hound), Girtablullû (Scorpion-Man), Umū dabrūtu (Violent Storms), Kulullû (Fish-Man), and Kusarikku (Bull-Man).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 62–64 (Tablet I, lines 132–162); Foster 2005, pp. 443–444; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tiamat (the eleven named creatures).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 62-64
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tiamat

Tiamat anoints Qingu (also written Kingu) as her consort and as general of her army.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 66 (Tablet I, lines 145–162); Foster 2005, p. 444.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 66

Tiamat hands Qingu the Tablet of Destinies (Akkadian *ṭuppi šīmāti*), the cosmic decree-tablet that confers sovereignty on its holder.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 66 (Tablet I, lines 156–162); Foster 2005, p. 444; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tablet of Destinies.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 66
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Tablet of Destinies

By placing the Tablet of Destinies on Qingu's breast, Tiamat formally elevates him to a kingly station among the gods who side with her.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 66 (Tablet I, lines 156–162); Foster 2005, p. 444.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 66

=== Tablet II: the failed champions

Anu and Ea hear of Tiamat's preparations through cosmic intelligence.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 70 (Tablet II, lines 1–11); Foster 2005, p. 446.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 70

Ea reports the danger to his father Anšar.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 70 (Tablet II, lines 11–48); Foster 2005, p. 446.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 70

Anšar dispatches Anu to face Tiamat and bring her to terms.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 72 (Tablet II, lines 67–80); Foster 2005, p. 447.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 72

Anu approaches Tiamat, sees her arrayed for war, and turns back without engaging.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 72 (Tablet II, lines 80–94); Foster 2005, p. 447.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 72

Anšar then dispatches Ea, who likewise turns back at the sight of Tiamat.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 72–74 (Tablet II, lines 95–104); Foster 2005, p. 447.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 72-74

The gods are paralysed; the older champions cannot face Tiamat.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 74 (Tablet II, lines 105–122); Foster 2005, pp. 447–448.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 74

Ea goes to Marduk and prepares him for the role of champion.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 74 (Tablet II, lines 122–138); Foster 2005, p. 448.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 74

Marduk presents himself before Anšar and accepts the challenge — but on a condition.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 76 (Tablet II, lines 139–150); Foster 2005, p. 448.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 76

Marduk's condition is that he be given supreme authority over all the gods: his decree must become unalterable, and his kingship over the assembled gods must be confirmed before he goes out to battle.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 76 (Tablet II, lines 158–162); Foster 2005, pp. 448–449.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 76

The gods must convene in formal assembly to ratify Marduk's elevation; Anšar accepts the condition and convenes the assembly.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 76 (Tablet II, lines 158–162); Foster 2005, pp. 448–449.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 76

=== Tablet III: convocation of the gods

Anšar dispatches his vizier Kakka to summon Lahmu and Lahamu, the elder gods, to the assembly.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 80 (Tablet III, lines 1–25); Foster 2005, pp. 451–452.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 80

Kakka delivers Anšar's message verbatim to Lahmu and Lahamu, recounting Tiamat's preparations and the failure of Anu and Ea to face her.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 80–84 (Tablet III, lines 25–124); Foster 2005, pp. 452–454.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 80-84

The verbatim repetition of long passages between Tablets II and III is a deliberate stylistic feature of Akkadian narrative epic — formal embassy speeches reported word-for-word.footnote:[Lambert 2013, commentary on Tablet III; Foster 2005, p. 451, headnote on the embassy convention.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=Tablet III commentary
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 451

The elder gods Lahmu and Lahamu lament Tiamat's resolve and accept the convocation summons.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 84 (Tablet III, lines 125–134); Foster 2005, p. 454.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 84

The great gods assemble at a banquet in Anšar's hall.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 84–86 (Tablet III, lines 134–142); Foster 2005, p. 454.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 84-86

The gods drink and feast, kissing one another, growing drunk on beer, until they are emboldened.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 86 (Tablet III, lines 134–138); Foster 2005, p. 454.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 86

The gods together confer on Marduk supreme authority and the right to fix destinies.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 86 (Tablet III, lines 138–end); Foster 2005, pp. 454–455.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 86

The decision to test Marduk's fitness for kingship by having him create and destroy a constellation as a sign is taken at the close of Tablet III and acted on at the start of Tablet IV.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 86–90 (Tablet III end / Tablet IV opening); Foster 2005, pp. 455–456.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 86-90

=== Tablet IV: the combat

Marduk is enthroned by the gods, who acclaim his kingship: "Marduk is king!"footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 88 (Tablet IV, lines 1–28); Foster 2005, p. 456.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 88

The gods test Marduk by asking him to make a constellation disappear and reappear by his command alone.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 88 (Tablet IV, lines 19–28); Foster 2005, p. 456.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 88

Marduk speaks; the constellation vanishes. He speaks again; it reappears. The test is passed and his cosmic creative power is publicly demonstrated.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 88 (Tablet IV, lines 25–28); Foster 2005, p. 456.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 88

The gods grant Marduk weapons for the combat: bow, mace, lightning, and a net.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 88–90 (Tablet IV, lines 35–42); Foster 2005, pp. 456–457.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 88-90

Marduk arms himself with the four winds (south, north, east, west) given by Anu.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 90 (Tablet IV, lines 41–48); Foster 2005, p. 457.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 90

Marduk creates seven additional winds — named in the text as the dust-devil, the whirlwind, the hurricane, the ruinous, the four-fold, the seven-fold, and the matchless — augmenting the four cardinal winds Anu had given him.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 90 (Tablet IV, lines 41–48); Foster 2005, p. 457; Dalley 2008, pp. 251–253.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 90
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 251-253

Marduk mounts the storm-chariot drawn by four named horses, rendered variously in the standard translations: Lambert 2013 gives "the Killer, the Relentless, the Trampler, the Swift"; Foster 2005 reads "Killer, Pitiless, Trampler, Fleet".footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 90 (Tablet IV, lines 50–55); Foster 2005, p. 457.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 90

Marduk advances against Tiamat in full battle array.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 90–92 (Tablet IV, lines 65–86); Foster 2005, pp. 458–459.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 90-92

Tiamat opens her mouth wide to swallow Marduk.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 92 (Tablet IV, lines 96–98); Foster 2005, p. 459.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 92

Marduk drives the great winds into Tiamat's open mouth, holding it open so she cannot close it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 92 (Tablet IV, lines 96–104); Foster 2005, p. 459.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 92

The winds inflate Tiamat's belly and distend her body.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 92 (Tablet IV, lines 100–104); Foster 2005, p. 459.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 92

Marduk shoots an arrow into Tiamat's belly; it splits her insides and pierces her heart.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 92 (Tablet IV, lines 101–103); Foster 2005, p. 459.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 92

Tiamat is killed; Marduk treads on her hindquarters and crushes her skull.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 94 (Tablet IV, lines 104–110); Foster 2005, pp. 459–460.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 94

The eleven monsters of Tiamat's army are bound and dispersed.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 94 (Tablet IV, lines 105–122); Foster 2005, p. 460.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 94

Qingu is taken captive; the Tablet of Destinies is removed from his breast and Marduk takes possession of it, sealing it with his own seal.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 94 (Tablet IV, lines 121–122); Foster 2005, p. 460.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 94

Marduk splits Tiamat's body lengthwise into two halves, "like a fish for drying."footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 94–96 (Tablet IV, lines 137–138); Foster 2005, p. 461.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 94-96

The upper half of Tiamat's body becomes the heavens; Marduk stretches it out as a covering.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 96 (Tablet IV, lines 137–146); Foster 2005, pp. 460–461.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 96

Marduk fixes guards over the upper waters so they cannot escape.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 96 (Tablet IV, lines 137–146); Foster 2005, pp. 460–461.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 96

Marduk surveys the regions and establishes the dwellings of the high gods: Anu in the heavens, Enlil in the atmosphere, Ea in the Apsû.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 96–98 (Tablet IV end); Foster 2005, pp. 461–462.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 96-98

This three-tier cosmic division — heaven (Anu), atmosphere (Enlil), and freshwater deep (Ea) — is the canonical Mesopotamian cosmography codified by Enūma Eliš.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 96–98; Black & Green 1992, s.v. cosmology; Bottéro 2001, pp. 220–243.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 96-98
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. cosmology

=== Tablet V: cosmography, calendar, and the heavens

[LACUNA: Tablet V is heavily damaged in its opening twenty-two lines; cosmographic detail on the celestial fixtures is partial. | source: Lambert 2013, pp. 98–113 (manuscript score) | scholarly_reconstruction: Lambert 2013 reconstructs from K.3473 plus duplicates against Aššur and Nineveh exemplars]

[RECONSTRUCTION: The opening of Tablet V — Marduk's establishment of the celestial fixtures (sun, moon, stars, constellations) — is composite-edited from parallel manuscripts. | gap_source: K.3473 (Nineveh) | fill_source: Aššur exemplars and Nineveh duplicates collated by Lambert 2013 | confidence: high]

In Tablet V Marduk establishes the celestial fixtures: he assigns stations to the great gods in the form of constellations.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 98–106 (Tablet V, lines 1–46, with reconstruction notes); Foster 2005, pp. 462–464.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 98-106

Marduk fixes the year of twelve months by setting three constellations for each month.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 100–102 (Tablet V, lines 1–22); Foster 2005, p. 462.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 100-102

Marduk establishes the moon-god Sîn and assigns him the night, defining the lunar phases as the basis of the calendar.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 104 (Tablet V, lines 12–22); Foster 2005, pp. 462–463; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Sîn.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 104
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Sîn

Marduk specifies the lunar cycle: Sîn's monthly phases — crescent, first quarter, full moon, last quarter — give the structure of the month.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 104–106 (Tablet V, lines 12–22); Foster 2005, pp. 462–463.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 104-106

[LACUNA: Tablet V, lines 50–86 (calendar and time-keeping section) is heavily damaged across all surviving manuscripts. | source: Lambert 2013, pp. 106–110 | scholarly_reconstruction: Lambert 2013 partially reconstructs from Aššur duplicates; some details remain unrecoverable]

Marduk establishes the gates of the eastern and western horizons through which the sun-god Šamaš passes daily.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 106–108 (Tablet V, partially reconstructed); Foster 2005, p. 463.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 106-108

Marduk fixes the four cardinal directions and the zenith.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 110 (Tablet V); Foster 2005, p. 463.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 110

Marduk uses parts of Tiamat's body to construct features of the cosmos: from her eyes flow the Tigris and Euphrates rivers; from her saliva he forms the clouds; her ribs become the supports of the sky.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 110–112 (Tablet V, lines 51–62); Foster 2005, p. 463; Dalley 2008, pp. 256–257.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 110-112
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 256-257

The cosmographic claim that the Tigris and Euphrates flow from Tiamat's eyes is one of the most-cited details of the poem and grounds the Mesopotamian alluvium in cosmogony.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 110–112; Bottéro 2001, pp. 220–243.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 110-112

The closing of Tablet V identifies Babylon as the cosmic centre that the gods will build: it is the place where Esagil — Marduk's temple — will stand.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 112 (Tablet V end); Foster 2005, pp. 463–464.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 112

=== Tablet VI: the creation of humanity and the building of Esagil

Marduk announces his intention to make humanity to relieve the gods of their labour.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 114 (Tablet VI, lines 1–10); Foster 2005, p. 464.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 114

Ea proposes the method: humanity will be made from the blood of one rebel god, who must be identified.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 114 (Tablet VI, lines 11–22); Foster 2005, pp. 464–465.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 114

The gods convene to identify the guilty party from the recent rebellion.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 114 (Tablet VI, lines 23–32); Foster 2005, p. 465.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 114

Qingu is identified by the gods as the rebel-leader who incited Tiamat to war.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 114–116 (Tablet VI, lines 23–32); Foster 2005, p. 465.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 114-116

Qingu is bound, brought before Ea, and his arteries severed.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 116 (Tablet VI, lines 32–34); Foster 2005, p. 465.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 116

Ea uses Qingu's blood to fashion humanity, mixing it (in some readings of the lines) with clay or with the gods' tears.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 116 (Tablet VI, lines 33–36); Foster 2005, p. 465; Dalley 2008, pp. 261–262.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 116
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 261-262

Humanity is created to bear the burden of the gods' service: the gods are released from their labour.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 116 (Tablet VI, lines 33–36); Foster 2005, p. 465; Bottéro 2001, pp. 80–87.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 116
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 80-87

The Igigi (the great gods who were doing the labour) are formally released by Marduk.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 116–118 (Tablet VI, lines 35–44); Foster 2005, p. 465.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 116-118

In gratitude, the gods volunteer to build Marduk a permanent dwelling-place.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 118 (Tablet VI, lines 45–60); Foster 2005, pp. 465–466.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 118

The gods build Esagil — Marduk's temple — at Babylon, with Esagil's ziqqurat Etemenanki ("the foundation-house of heaven and earth") rising in seven storeys.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 118–120 (Tablet VI, lines 60–73); Foster 2005, p. 466; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Esagil and Etemenanki.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 118-120
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Esagil and Etemenanki

The construction is followed by a banquet of the gods at the newly completed temple.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 120 (Tablet VI, lines 60–80); Foster 2005, p. 466.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 120

At the dedication banquet, all the gods recite the first nine of Marduk's fifty names.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 120–122 (Tablet VI, lines 121–166, transitioning into Tablet VII); Foster 2005, pp. 466–468.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 120-122

The gods proclaim Marduk's kingship over the cosmos; the older generations (Anu, Anšar, Ea) endorse the proclamation.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 122 (Tablet VI end); Foster 2005, pp. 468–469.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 122

The bow with which Marduk slew Tiamat is exalted as the Bow-Star (the constellation), placed in heaven by Anu as a sign of Marduk's kingship.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 122 (Tablet VI, lines 80–94); Foster 2005, pp. 466–467.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 122

=== Tablet VII: the fifty names of Marduk

Tablet VII is structured as a hymnic recitation of Marduk's fifty names rather than a continuous narrative; it functions as a theological appendix to the cosmogonic action of Tablets I–VI.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 121–134 (Tablet VII); commentary pp. 482–488; Foster 2005, pp. 468–485.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 121-134, 482-488

Each of Marduk's fifty names is presented with an aetiological gloss explaining what aspect of cosmic or divine function the name encodes.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 121–134, with commentary on each name; Foster 2005, pp. 468–485.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 121-134

The fifty names span warrior functions, judicial functions, agricultural and herbalist functions, calendrical functions, and creator functions.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 482–488 (commentary on the fifty names); Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 482-488
// EVIDENCE: source_id=jacobsen-1976 ; loc=pp. 165-191

Several of Marduk's fifty names are theologically significant in their own right: among them are Asari (the agriculturist), Lugaldimmerankia ("king of the gods of heaven and earth"), Asarluḫi (the exorcist), and Tutu (the lord of cult and of building).footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 121–134 with commentary on individual names; Foster 2005, pp. 468–485.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 121-134

The fifty-name list incorporates names that elsewhere belong to other deities — Asarluḫi is otherwise the son of Ea — making the recitation an act of theological absorption: Marduk takes up and unifies the functions of multiple gods.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 482–488; Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191; Black & Green 1992, s.v. Asarluḫi.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 482-488
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Asarluḫi

The names are organised in pairs and triplets, with successive names forming hymnic stanzas; the ordering varies in some manuscripts, particularly the Aššur copies.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 121–134; commentary p. 482.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 121-134

The closing verses of Tablet VII step outside the fifty-names recitation to address the listener directly: the poem is to be transmitted, the names studied and remembered, the recitation preserved.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 134 (Tablet VII closing); Foster 2005, pp. 485–486.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 134

The closing instructions explicitly tie the poem's transmission to the *akītu* recitation: the priests are charged with preserving and reciting it.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 134 and pp. 449–465; Foster 2005, p. 486; Bottéro 2001, pp. 234–243.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 134
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=pp. 234-243

[INFERENCE: The fifty-name structure functions as a hymnic theology of Marduk that absorbs the functions of competing deities into Marduk himself, completing in religious form what the cosmogonic narrative completes in mythic form. | basis: the explicit incorporation of names that elsewhere belong to other gods (Asarluḫi, Tutu); Lambert's commentary p. 482; Jacobsen 1976 pp. 165–191. | risk: this is a synthetic interpretation of how the fifty-names list functions; Lambert is more cautious and treats individual names case by case rather than imposing a single absorption-theology reading on the whole.]

The total of fifty names is itself significant: fifty was the cult-number of Enlil, the second-tier god whose authority Marduk effectively assumes in the poem.footnote:[Lambert 2013, p. 482 (commentary, on the fifty count); Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=p. 482

[INFERENCE: The choice of fifty as the count is therefore not arbitrary but signals Marduk's displacement of Enlil at the head of the active cosmic hierarchy. | basis: Enlil's cult-number was fifty; the *An = Anum* god-list explicitly assigns Enlil's number 50 to Marduk; Lambert (2013, p. 482) and Jacobsen (1976, pp. 165–191) both treat the equation as load-bearing for the poem's theology. | risk: the equation is established from the *An = Anum* god-list and from Lambert's commentary; the poem of Enūma Eliš does not itself state the connection — the inference is from background scholarship into the poem, not from the poem outward.]

=== Variants

[VARIANT: primary=Lambert 2013 standard composite (Babylonian witnesses): Marduk is named throughout as the cosmic king and recipient of the fifty names | alt=Aššur recension (Lambert 2013 apparatus): in some passages Aššur is substituted for Marduk, and Anšar is identified with Aššur to elevate the Assyrian state-god above Marduk | chosen: Lambert composite (Babylonian) | reason: the Babylonian text is the canonical recension; the Aššur substitution is a politically motivated Assyrian reading limited to the Aššur copies and is recorded in Lambert's apparatus rather than in the body text]

The Aššur-substitution variant is documented in Lambert 2013's apparatus and reflects a Middle Assyrian theological assertion: under the Assyrian crown, Aššur takes Marduk's place at the top of the cosmic hierarchy.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 4–25 (manuscript catalogue and apparatus); commentary pp. 449–488.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 4-25

Tablet VII fifty-names list ordering varies in some Aššur and Nineveh copies, with paired epithets reversed in line order in a small number of manuscripts; Lambert 2013 collates these variants in the manuscript score.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 121–134 (Tablet VII manuscript score); commentary p. 482.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=pp. 121-134

=== Summary tally

The seven-tablet structure of Enūma Eliš has the following narrative arc, with the centre of mass on the Tablet IV combat: theogony and Apsû's binding (Tablet I), failed champions and Marduk's nomination (Tablet II), convocation and conferral (Tablet III), combat and cosmic division (Tablet IV), cosmography and calendar (Tablet V), creation of humanity and building of Esagil (Tablet VI), the fifty-names hymnic theology (Tablet VII).footnote:[Lambert 2013, full edition; Foster 2005, pp. 436–486; Dalley 2008, pp. 228–277.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=lambert-2013 ; loc=full edition
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 436-486

The poem's distinctive contribution to Mesopotamian theology is the bundling of cosmogony, theogony, royal-legitimation, and ritual-aetiology into a single composition that functions both as cosmic history and as liturgy.footnote:[Lambert 2013, pp. 449–488 (commentary as a whole); Bottéro 2001, pp. 220–243; Jacobsen 1976, pp. 165–191; Foster 2007, pp. 50–55.]
