# Scope: Akkadian and Babylonian Mythology

## Culture
The Akkadian-language literary tradition of ancient Mesopotamia, taken in the broad scholarly sense — that is, the Babylonian and Assyrian literatures together, as a single linguistic stream that produced its great compositions across roughly two millennia in the cities of Babylon, Nippur, Sippar, Uruk, Borsippa, Aššur, Nineveh, Kalḫu, and the wider Mesopotamian alluvium and northern uplands. The narrators of this tradition called their land *māt Akkadî* ("the land of Akkad"), then *māt Bābili* ("the land of Babylon") and *māt Aššur* ("the land of Aššur"). The literary culture was carried by the cuneiform scribal apparatus (the *ṭupšarrūtu*), centered on temple, palace, and *bīt mummi* training houses.

This book treats Akkadian, Babylonian, and Assyrian narrative literature as one continuous tradition because that is how its ancient practitioners treated it: Babylonian and Assyrian scribes copied the same compositions, glossed them with the same commentaries, and identified themselves with the same scribal lineage. The book does, however, separate the *genres* this literature produced — myth, divine conflict and underworld narrative, heroic epic, royal legend, reflective and practical wisdom, disputation poems and animal fables, visionary and popular narrative, and the short cosmogonic narratives embedded as prologues to incantations — so that each is treated on its own terms rather than flattened into "Mesopotamian myth".

The genre list above is deliberately wider than "myth and heroic epic". Akkadian culture transmitted its knowledge of the world through many narrative forms: a date palm and a tamarisk arguing about which feeds the king better; a fox bargaining for his life with a lion; a father instructing his son in maxims; a brief myth telling how the worm came to live in the tooth. These texts are not ornamental peripheries to "real" mythology — they are how the culture taught agronomy, ethics, social order, and the etiology of everyday suffering. The book takes them in.

## Period
- Earliest in-scope material: Old Akkadian period royal inscriptions of Sargon and Narām-Sîn (ca. 2300 BCE), which are the seed of the later legend cycles. Substantial **literary** attestations begin with the Old Babylonian period (ca. 1900–1600 BCE): OB *Atra-ḫasīs*, OB *Gilgameš*, OB *Anzû*, OB *Adapa* fragments, OB Sargon and Narām-Sîn legends.
- Latest in-scope material: Late Babylonian and Seleucid-period cuneiform tablets (down to ca. 100 BCE), which preserve the latest copies of the Standard Babylonian recensions, including library colophons from Hellenistic Babylon and Uruk.
- Range: ca. 2300 BCE — ca. 100 BCE.
- Secondary (scholarly) literature: contemporary, primarily 1872 (George Smith's discovery of the *Gilgameš* flood tablet) — present.

## Languages of primary sources
- **Akkadian**, in all dialects: Old Akkadian, Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Standard Babylonian (the prestige literary register of the first millennium), Late Babylonian, Old Assyrian, Middle Assyrian, Neo-Assyrian.
- **Sumero-Akkadian bilingual tablets**: the Akkadian text is in scope. The Sumerian column is in scope only where it is integral to the Akkadian literary product as transmitted (e.g., bilingual hymns, the Sumerian preamble of *Lugal-e* in its Akkadian-glossed first-millennium copies). For Sumerian-only compositions, the companion volume *The Myths of Sumer* is the place; this book does not duplicate them.

## Excluded adjacent cultures (contamination risks)
The hard contamination boundary is *language of composition*, not geography. Mesopotamia is densely multilingual, and the same scribes copied multiple traditions; the only stable line is the language the literary text itself was composed in.

- **Sumerian-language literary tradition**: covered in the companion volume *The Myths of Sumer*. Sumerian Gilgameš poems, Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*, the Sumerian Flood Story (*Eridu Genesis*), the Enki and Enlil cycles, the Enmerkar and Lugalbanda cycles, *Lugal-e*, *Angim*, *Ninurta and the Turtle*, and the rest of ETCSL — all out of scope **as Sumerian compositions**. Their Akkadian counterparts (Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*, Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar*, *Atra-ḫasīs*, the Akkadian *Anzû*) are **in scope**: they are independent Akkadian literary works, not translations. Sumerian originals are discussed *as comparative background* in the influences chapter and in chapter footnotes, not retold.
- **Hurrian–Hittite literary tradition (Anatolia)**: distinct languages, distinct corpus. The *Kumarbi Cycle* (*Song of Going Forth*, *Song of Ullikummi*, *Song of Hedammu*), *Illuyanka*, the Hittite *Telipinu* and Anatolian storm-god myths are out of scope. Hurro-Hittite material is, however, central to the influences chapter as the principal vector by which non-Sumerian (and Indo-European-mediated) motifs reached the Akkadian tradition: the Hittite chancery used Akkadian as a lingua franca (Amarna, Boğazköy archives), and Hurrian theogonic patterns plausibly influenced *Enūma Eliš*. The **discussion** of these contacts is in scope; the Hurrian and Hittite **texts themselves** are not retold here.
- **Ugaritic literary tradition (Late Bronze Age Levant)**: West Semitic, alphabetic cuneiform, distinct from Akkadian. The *Baal Cycle*, *Aqhat*, and *Kirta* are out of scope as primary material. Limited comparative reference is permitted in the influences chapter and in footnotes where a parallel illuminates an Akkadian text (e.g., Yamm/Tiamat sea-combat parallels).
- **Elamite literary tradition**: eastern neighbor (southwestern Iran), unrelated language. Out of scope.
- **Aramaic literature**: distinct language family; the Aramaic *Story of Aḥiqar* is out of scope even though it is set at the Assyrian court and influenced the later wisdom tradition.
- **Biblical Hebrew literature**: out of scope as primary material. Genesis 1–11 parallels (Flood, primaeval giants, tower) are mentioned in the influences chapter only insofar as they bear on the *direction* and *mechanism* of borrowing; the Hebrew texts are not retold.
- **First-millennium royal inscriptions, annals, and chronicles (e.g., Ashurbanipal's annals, the Babylonian Chronicle series)**: out of scope as a genre. Excluded unless they contain substantial embedded narrative — the *Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* is in scope precisely because it is a literary epic, not an annalistic king list.
- **Omen series, rituals, incantations, lexical texts, and astronomical/astrological compendia**: out of scope *as genres*. Excluded unless an individual composition contains substantial embedded narrative — the cosmogonic incantation prologues named in §I (e.g., *enūma Anum ibnû šamê*, the *Worm and the Toothache*, the bilingual *Creation of the World by Marduk*) are in scope on this exception. The narrative itself, not the surrounding ritual apparatus, is what is retold.
- **Hellenistic and later receptions** (Berossos's *Babyloniaca* in Greek paraphrase; later Mandaic, Manichaean, or Islamic-era reception): out of scope as primary material; permitted as reception-history sidebars where useful.

## Reconstruction policy
When a primary Akkadian source has a physical lacuna (broken tablet, missing column, lost obverse), content may be reconstructed from:
- **Parallel manuscripts of the same composition** (different exemplars of, e.g., *Atra-ḫasīs* from Sippar, Nippur, Nineveh) — always permitted; the reconstruction marker names the donor manuscript.
- **A different Akkadian composition within the same dialect and period that demonstrably preserves the same scene** — permitted when strictly necessary (e.g., using OB *Anzû* fragments to fill a gap in the Standard Babylonian *Anzû*). The donor text and dialect/period must be named in the marker.
- **Earlier or later Akkadian recensions of the same composition** (e.g., OB *Gilgameš* filling a Standard Babylonian gap) — permitted when no better source exists; the period gap must be named.
- All reconstructed material must be flagged with `[RECONSTRUCTION:]` markers that identify the donor source. Reconstructions must appear explicitly in the claims stage and in the final text — they are never silent fills.

**Not permitted as silent reconstruction**:
- **Sumerian originals filling gaps in Akkadian retellings**. The Sumerian Gilgameš poems are *not* used to fill gaps in the Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*; they belong to a different tradition and are cited only as comparative background. The same applies to the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent* / Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* pair.
- **Hurro-Hittite parallels filling gaps in Akkadian theogonies**. The *Kumarbi Cycle* is comparative material for *Enūma Eliš*, not a donor source.
- **Ugaritic parallels filling gaps in Akkadian combat narratives**. The *Baal Cycle* is comparative material for the Tiamat combat, not a donor source.

In-tradition means: composition in Akkadian (any dialect), attested on tablets from the Mesopotamian scribal stream, ca. 2300 BCE – 100 BCE.

## In-scope corpus (broad)

### I. Cosmogony, creation, and primordial order
- *Enūma Eliš* (Standard Babylonian creation epic)
- *Atra-ḫasīs* (Old Babylonian; Standard Babylonian recensions)
- *Adapa and the South Wind* (Old Babylonian Tell Haddad fragment; Middle Babylonian Amarna fragment; Neo-Assyrian Nineveh fragment)
- Cosmogonic dossier (treated together in a single chapter or as appendix entries depending on attestation): *Enmešarra's Defeat*; *The Toil of Babylon*; *Uraš and Marduk*; *Damkina's Bond*; *The Slaying of Labbu*; *The Founding of Eridu* (Akkadian recensions); *The First Brick*; *The Theogony of Dunnu*.
- **Cosmogonic narratives embedded as incantation prologues** (treated together in the creation chapter or appendix): *enūma Anum ibnû šamê* ("When Anu created the heavens"); the bilingual *Creation of the World by Marduk* (the Sippar incantation prologue); *The Worm and the Toothache* (CT 17 50, the etiology of caries by way of a tiny creation narrative); other named *enūma*-incipit prologues that begin with a cosmogonic clause and then turn to ritual.

### II. Divine conflict, descent, and the underworld
- *The Descent of Ishtar* (Standard Babylonian; the chapter foregrounds the Akkadian composition rather than treating it as a derivative of the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*).
- *Nergal and Ereškigal* (Middle Babylonian Amarna recension; Standard Babylonian Sultantepe recension).
- *Anzû* (Old Babylonian recension; Standard Babylonian recension).
- *Erra and Išum* (first-millennium Babylonian).

### III. Heroic and mythic epics
- *The Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš* (Sîn-lēqi-unninni recension), with reference to the OB and Middle Babylonian forerunners.
- *Etana* (Old Babylonian; Middle Assyrian; Standard Babylonian).

### IV. Royal legends and pseudo-historical epics
- *The Birth Legend of Sargon*.
- *The King of Battle* (*šar tamḫāri*) — including the Amarna fragment.
- *The Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn*.
- *The Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* (Middle Assyrian).

### V. Wisdom, suffering, and dialogic literature
**V.A. Reflective dialogue and complaint** — first-person and dialogic compositions on undeserved suffering, divine justice, and the limits of pious resignation.
- *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*.
- *The Babylonian Theodicy*.
- *Dialogue of Pessimism* (Master and Servant).

**V.B. Practical instruction** — the father-to-son transmission of ethical and social knowledge, distinct in form from the dialogic complaints above.
- *Counsels of Wisdom*.
- *Counsels of a Pessimist*.
- *Šimâ milka* / *Instructions of Šūpê-amēli* (Akkadian recensions: Emar, Ugarit, Hattusa).

### VI. Disputations and animal fables
Akkadian narrative compositions in which personified objects, plants, animals, or trades argue out their relative usefulness, or in which animals act out social dramas. They encode agronomy, animal husbandry, and the social hierarchy of the trades.
- *The Tamarisk and the Date Palm* (Old Babylonian; Middle Assyrian recension).
- *The Ox and the Horse*.
- *The Series of Nisaba and Wheat*.
- *The Fable of the Willow*.
- *The Series of the Fox* (a substantial first-millennium fable cycle).
- *The Poor Man and the Donkey*-type pieces.

### VII. Visionary and popular narratives
- *Underworld Vision of an Assyrian Prince* (Kummâ).
- *The Poor Man of Nippur*.

### Influences chapter (special)
A dedicated chapter, distinct from the introduction, addresses the cultural streams that shaped the Akkadian literary imagination:
- **Sumerian substrate**: lexical, mythological, and structural inheritance — and the question of where Akkadian compositions are translations, where they are adaptations, and where they are independent works on shared themes.
- **Hurro-Hittite contact and Indo-European mediation**: Hurrian theogonic patterns (Anu–Kumarbi–Teshub succession) and their possible influence on *Enūma Eliš*; Indo-Aryan-named Mitanni deities in the Boğazköy treaties as evidence that Indo-European-speaking elites were in direct contact with the Akkadian scribal world; the diplomatic and scribal exchange visible in the Amarna corpus.
- **Amorite, Kassite, and Aramaean integration**: how successive non-Akkadian-speaking ruling groups were absorbed into the Akkadian literary culture rather than displacing it.

The chapter discusses the Sumerian, Hurro-Hittite, and Ugaritic comparanda as **comparative material**; it does not retell those texts. The retelling is reserved for the Akkadian compositions in the corpus above.

## Out-of-scope (explicit)
- Sumerian-language compositions in their Sumerian form (covered in *The Myths of Sumer*).
- Hurro-Hittite literary cycles (*Kumarbi*, *Illuyanka*, *Telipinu*).
- Ugaritic literary cycles (*Baal*, *Aqhat*, *Kirta*).
- Elamite literary tradition.
- Aramaic *Aḥiqar* and other Aramaic narrative literature.
- Biblical Hebrew narrative literature.
- Royal inscriptions, annals, chronicles, king lists (genre exclusion; *Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* is the exception because it is literary epic).
- Omen series, rituals, incantations, lexical texts, astronomical-astrological compendia (genre exclusion; cosmogonic prologues are the exception).
- Berossos's *Babyloniaca* (preserved only in Greek paraphrase) and later Hellenistic, Mandaic, Manichaean, or Islamic-era reception of Mesopotamian material.

## Book register
Asimov-style retelling. Narrative prose in the body. Citations in AsciiDoc footnotes. Inferences, lacunae, reconstructions, dialect/recension identifications, and variants flagged inline with explicit markers. Plain-English register — no unexplained technical terms on first use; every Akkadian or Sumerian term glossed at first occurrence and collected in the glossary. Akkadian transliteration uses the Assyriological standard (*š*, *ṣ*, *ṭ*, macrons for vowel length, circumflex for contracted long vowels, *ḫ* for /x/). Long titles use italics; short ones in roman.

## Variant presentation policy
- If one recension is clearly prevalent in the scholarly corpus (e.g., Sîn-lēqi-unninni's Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*): present it in the body, with substantive variants from earlier recensions in footnotes.
- If two or more recensions are roughly co-equal in scholarly weight (e.g., the Amarna and Sultantepe recensions of *Nergal and Ereškigal*; the OB and SB *Anzû*): present them side by side in the chapter body, each introduced with its source.
- Where dialect or period matters for interpretation (e.g., the difference between OB and SB Atra-ḫasīs framings of the flood), the dialect is named in the body.

# Skill instructions: chapter-draft

name: chapter-draft

# chapter-draft

## For the human

This is the creative stage — and it is much safer now that the facts are already locked. The AI is not simultaneously researching, citing, and writing. It is only writing. The factual skeleton has already been written and verified. The AI's job is to turn a list of numbered, checked facts into a narrative that reads like Asimov at his best.

The main risk is no longer inventing facts (those are locked) but **drift during the prose transformation**: dropping a verified fact because it doesn't fit the narrative flow, softening a claim until its meaning changes, or inserting a "helpful" detail that was never in the verified facts. The next step (`narrative-fidelity`) will catch these, but the writing AI should work to prevent them.

Importantly, the writing AI does **not** receive the original source texts. It only receives the verified claims document. This is deliberate: giving it the sources would tempt it to "improve" the chapter with additional details that were never fact-checked.

## Hard rules
1. **Every factual assertion in the narrative must correspond to a claim in the approved claims document.** No new facts, no new citations, no "I happen to know" additions.
2. **Every claim in the approved document must appear in the narrative.** Do not silently drop claims because they are inconvenient for the prose flow. If a claim is genuinely irrelevant, flag it rather than omitting it.
3. Use exactly the English renderings locked in `glossary.yaml`. Grep the glossary for any foreign term you are about to use in body prose to confirm the locked rendering before writing it.
4. All markers from the claims document — including `[INFERENCE:]`, `[SPECULATION:]`, `[VARIANT:]`, `[LACUNA:]`, `[RECONSTRUCTION:]`, `[APPROPRIATION-FLAG:]` — survive into the narrative byte-for-byte, including all pipe-delimited sub-fields. Do not paraphrase them away or relabel pipe-fields.
5. Citations carry over from the claims document. Do not add, remove, or reassign footnotes.
6. Foreign-language common nouns and book titles in body prose render as AsciiDoc italic (`_term_`), not bold (`*term*`). Convert any asterisk-bold body-prose terms inherited from the claims file to underscore-italic. Footnote bodies, EVIDENCE tokens (`// EVIDENCE: …`), COMPARATIVE-HOOK comments, and marker contents preserve their original markup byte-for-byte — the verbatim-preservation requirement of rule 4/5 is not violated by the body-prose-only italic conversion.

## First-mention gloss policy and the ledger

Each chapter is drafted in isolation, so without coordination every chapter would re-gloss every recurring term in full and the book would read as if no one talked to anyone. The `gloss-ledger.yaml` file at the book root tracks which terms have already been glossed and where, so this stage can decide whether to gloss in full, give a brief reminder, or use the bare term.

For each `glossary.yaml` term you are about to use in body prose, look it up in the ledger and decide:

1. **Not in the ledger** → write the full `first_mention_gloss` from `glossary.yaml` on first appearance in this chapter. Add a ledger entry with `full_gloss_in:` set to the current chapter number, `last_mention_in:` set to the current chapter, and `chapters_used:` containing the current chapter.
2. **`last_mention_in` is the previous chapter (N−1) or the current chapter** → bare term, no gloss. Just update `last_mention_in` to the current chapter and append the chapter to `chapters_used`.
3. **`last_mention_in` is at least 2 chapters back** → write a short *reminder gloss* — one phrase, ad-hoc, not the full functional gloss. For example: "Tiamat, the saltwater chaos-figure" or "the *me* — the cosmic ordinances" rather than the full glossary entry. Update `last_reminder_in` and `last_mention_in` to the current chapter; append to `chapters_used`.

The "at least 2 chapters" threshold is a soft guideline. A very central term used in nearly every chapter does not need a reminder every other chapter — use judgement. A peripheral term that appeared once in chapter 02 and now reappears in chapter 07 should probably get a reminder.

A reminder gloss is shorter and more colloquial than the full first-mention gloss. The full gloss is a careful definition; the reminder is a one-phrase nudge so the reader is not stranded if they jumped chapters.

Only count chapters that you have actually finalized in this run as "previous." If chapter 03 introduces a term that chapter 02 happens to mention but had not yet been drafted at the time chapter 02 was written, that does not retroactively count — fix it by re-drafting from the affected chapter forward.

After writing the chapter, write the updated `gloss-ledger.yaml` back to the book root. Preserve all entries from earlier chapters; add or update only the entries for terms used in this chapter.

## Order discipline

Story chapters are drafted strictly in TOC order — chapter 01 first, then 02, then 03, etc. The intro (chapter 00) is drafted before any of them. This ordering is what makes the gloss ledger reliable: each chapter sees only ledger state from chapters that have actually been finalized.

If you need to re-draft chapter N (e.g. fact-check found a problem), you must also re-draft chapters N+1..end, because their gloss decisions depended on what chapter N established. Before starting, verify: the latest `last_mention_in` value in the ledger should be N−1 (the previous chapter). If it is greater, stop and ask the human — chapters were drafted out of order and the ledger is inconsistent with what you are about to write.

## Style: the Asimov method

Treat the reader as intelligent and uninformed. Gloss proper nouns with functional roles, not poetic epithets. Short paragraphs, one idea each. Plain modern English. Where the source is ambiguous, say so. Where scholars disagree, name it briefly. Use etymology and geography when they illuminate a specific point, never as mandatory ornament.

### Exemplar passage

> The Sumerians believed that before anything else existed, there was a primeval sea. They called her Nammu, and she seems to have been thought of as a kind of cosmic mother — though the texts that survive are maddeningly brief on this point. From Nammu came heaven and earth, conceived of as a single mountain with the sky (An) on top and the earth (Ki) below. An and Ki were the first divine couple, and their union produced Enlil, the god of the air.
>
> Enlil then did something that requires explanation. He separated his father An from his mother Ki, pushing the sky upward and leaving the earth below. This is a motif found in many mythologies — the violent separation of sky and earth by a younger god — but the Sumerian version is unusually matter-of-fact about it. There is no battle, no castration, no cosmic drama. Enlil simply separates them, and the world as we know it comes into being.

### Forbidden words

Do not use, in this register: *shimmering, ancient* (as ornament), *mystical, unfathomable, primordial* (as ornament), *ineffable, sacred* (as filler), *mighty, fearsome, dread, eternal, boundless, infinite, veiled, shrouded*. Avoid openings like "Long ago...", "In the time before time...", "It is said that...". These mark imitation-sacred prose, not Asimov.

## Variant handling (from brief)

- If `variants.classification` is **single-prevalent**: present the prevalent version in the body. Put alternate versions in AsciiDoc footnotes.
- If **co-equal**: present the variants inline in the body, each introduced with its source ("One tradition, preserved in [source], describes... Another, in [source], gives a different account...").

## Narrative transitions

The claims document has no connective tissue — that is your job. You may add:
- **Transitional sentences** that connect one claim to the next ("Having established the cosmic order, the narrative turns to...")
- **Orienting context** that helps the reader follow the story ("At this point in the text, the setting shifts to...")
- **Explanatory asides** that help the reader understand why something matters ("This is worth pausing on, because...")

These are **not** factual claims — they are prose scaffolding. They must not introduce new information about the myth. If a transition requires a factual bridge, that bridge should already be in the claims document as an `[INFERENCE:]` marker.

## Length

The approved claims document is the chapter's length budget. The chapter is whatever tight Asimov prose around those claims yields — no more, no less. There is no target word count, no floor, no ceiling.

Padding is forbidden. Recognize it: filler transitions that restate the prior paragraph, ornamental reverence, "in other words" loops, recap paragraphs, expanded glosses where a bare term suffices, explanatory asides that don't add information. If you catch yourself reaching for any of these to make the chapter feel weightier, stop — the chapter is already the right length.

A chapter that comes in much shorter than another chapter in the same book is not a problem to solve. Two myths can have very different amounts of verified factual content; the prose should reflect that.

## Source overview opening

Every chapter begins with a short prose passage (2–5 paragraphs) that orients the reader in the physical evidence before the story begins. This passage is built from the `=== Source overview` claims in the approved claims document and covers:

- **What we have**: the tablets, fragments, or manuscripts — named by museum number or corpus ID where possible — and where they were found.
- **What condition it is in**: complete, fragmentary, or composite. Where the major gaps are.
- **How we read it**: which critical edition or translation the retelling follows.

This passage sets the Asimov register immediately: the reader understands they are about to hear a story reconstructed from physical artifacts, not received from some mystical tradition. It is the scholarly equivalent of "here is the evidence, now here is the story it tells."

The source overview is separated from the narrative body by a blank line or a section transition (e.g., "The story, as the tablets preserve it, goes as follows."). It is not a dry bibliography — it is narrative prose about artifacts, written in the same Asimov register as the rest of the chapter.

=== Ereškigal's curse and the sprinkling of the water of life

Ereškigal recognises the deception. She utters a powerful curse upon Aṣušu-namir — that the messenger's food shall be the city's leftovers, the messenger's drink shall be the city's drainwater, the messenger shall dwell at the threshold among the city's outcasts; the curse is a fate of perpetual marginality, the lot of those who exist at the edges of social order. But the curse, fierce as it is, does not undo the promise: Ereškigal must release Ishtar.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 503–504 (Ereškigal's curse upon Aṣušu-namir; the marginal-fate imagery); Dalley 2008, pp. 159–160 (the curse-content and its irrevocability).]

=== The ascent: progressive restoration through the seven gates

Ishtar's ascent reverses the descent, gate by gate. At each gate, the gatekeeper returns to her one item of the clothing and jewelry that was stripped from her on the way down, in reverse order: the proud garment first, then the bangles, the girdle of birthstones, the breast-ornaments, the neck-chains, the earrings, and finally the great crown. By the seventh gate she stands once again clothed in the full ornament of her divine office. (The ascent restores in reverse the items listed at the descent; the chapter draws this reversal from the structural symmetry of Foster's translation rather than from a verbatim quotation of the ascent passage.)footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 504 (the ascent through the seven gates; restoration in reverse sequence); Dalley 2008, pp. 160–161 (the gate-by-gate restoration).]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 504 (the ascent and restoration)
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 160-161

But Namtar's escort comes with a condition: a substitute must be brought in Ishtar's place. The underworld must not be left empty; the laws of *Kurnugê* require that when one passes out of it, another must enter in their stead.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 504 (the substitution requirement; the laws of *Kurnugê*); Dalley 2008, p. 161 (the substitute-condition for the ascent).]

=== Tammuz substituted

The substitute Ishtar names is Tammuz — the shepherd-god, her own consort. The text presents Tammuz as having neglected his lover during her descent: instead of mourning her in the standard postures of grief, he has dressed in fine clothes and played the pipe in the company of singers. When Ishtar emerges and sees him so dressed, she designates him as the substitute, and Namtar's demons seize him.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 504–505 (Tammuz's neglect during Ishtar's captivity; his designation as substitute; Namtar's demons take him); Dalley 2008, pp. 161–162 (the substitution scene); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Tammuz.]

Tammuz's sister Belili — wearing her own ornaments, lapis lazuli on her arm — is described as casting them aside in grief at the news. Belili's mourning frames Tammuz's removal in the wider household of the dying-god cult; the figure of the bereaved sister is a load-bearing element of the closing ritual instruction.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 505 (Belili tearing off her ornaments at the news of Tammuz's removal); Dalley 2008, p. 162 (Belili's grief).]

=== Closing ritual: the Tammuz festival

The composition closes with what appears to be a ritual instruction. The closing lines move from narrative into prescription: on a specific day each year — the day of Tammuz's annual descent — the cult community is to lament the dying shepherd-god, the lapis-lazuli pipe is to be sounded for him, and Belili is to take part in the wailing. The closing prescription is partly damaged in some witnesses but the etiological hook is clear: the narrative of Ishtar's descent and Tammuz's substitution authorises a recurring cult observance.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 505 (closing ritual instruction; the Tammuz wailing rite); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 26–29 (the *Descent of Ishtar* and the Tammuz cult in late-period transmission); Bottéro 2001, on the seasonal Tammuz wailing rites in Babylonian and Assyrian practice.]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*The Descent of Ishtar* is one of the most-studied compositions in the Akkadian narrative tradition. It is widely cited in introductions to Mesopotamian religion, in studies of the dying-god typology, and in cross-cultural comparative work on underworld and seasonal-vegetation myths. Its compactness (138 lines) and its tightly-structured narrative arc — descent, stripping, captivity, retrieval, substitution, ritual hook — make it the illustrative type for the Akkadian underworld-descent genre.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 498 (the composition's anthological prominence); Bottéro 2001, on the *Descent of Ishtar* as a representative underworld myth; Foster 2007, pp. 26–29.]
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=p. 498
// EVIDENCE: source_id=bottero-2001 ; loc=passim
// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2007 ; loc=pp. 26-29

Within the Akkadian corpus, the closest internal parallel is *Nergal and Ereškigal* (chapter 14), which shares the underworld setting and the figure of Ereškigal but tells a substantively different story — Nergal's reluctant marriage to the queen of *Kurnugê* — rather than a descent-and-substitution narrative. The two compositions form a paired underworld dossier: *The Descent of Ishtar* gives the goddess's descent and seasonal-substitution structure; *Nergal and Ereškigal* gives the underworld's complementary marital and political structure.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 498 and pp. 506 ff.
