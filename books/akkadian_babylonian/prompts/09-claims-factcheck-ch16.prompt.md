# Scope: Akkadian and Babylonian Mythology

## Culture
The Akkadian-language literary tradition of ancient Mesopotamia, taken in the broad scholarly sense — that is, the Babylonian and Assyrian literatures together, as a single linguistic stream that produced its great compositions across roughly two millennia in the cities of Babylon, Nippur, Sippar, Uruk, Borsippa, Aššur, Nineveh, Kalḫu, and the wider Mesopotamian alluvium and northern uplands. The narrators of this tradition called their land *māt Akkadî* ("the land of Akkad"), then *māt Bābili* ("the land of Babylon") and *māt Aššur* ("the land of Aššur"). The literary culture was carried by the cuneiform scribal apparatus (the *ṭupšarrūtu*), centered on temple, palace, and *bīt mummi* training houses.

This book treats Akkadian, Babylonian, and Assyrian narrative literature as one continuous tradition because that is how its ancient practitioners treated it: Babylonian and Assyrian scribes copied the same compositions, glossed them with the same commentaries, and identified themselves with the same scribal lineage. The book does, however, separate the *genres* this literature produced — myth, divine conflict and underworld narrative, heroic epic, royal legend, reflective and practical wisdom, disputation poems and animal fables, visionary and popular narrative, and the short cosmogonic narratives embedded as prologues to incantations — so that each is treated on its own terms rather than flattened into "Mesopotamian myth".

The genre list above is deliberately wider than "myth and heroic epic". Akkadian culture transmitted its knowledge of the world through many narrative forms: a date palm and a tamarisk arguing about which feeds the king better; a fox bargaining for his life with a lion; a father instructing his son in maxims; a brief myth telling how the worm came to live in the tooth. These texts are not ornamental peripheries to "real" mythology — they are how the culture taught agronomy, ethics, social order, and the etiology of everyday suffering. The book takes them in.

## Period
- Earliest in-scope material: Old Akkadian period royal inscriptions of Sargon and Narām-Sîn (ca. 2300 BCE), which are the seed of the later legend cycles. Substantial **literary** attestations begin with the Old Babylonian period (ca. 1900–1600 BCE): OB *Atra-ḫasīs*, OB *Gilgameš*, OB *Anzû*, OB *Adapa* fragments, OB Sargon and Narām-Sîn legends.
- Latest in-scope material: Late Babylonian and Seleucid-period cuneiform tablets (down to ca. 100 BCE), which preserve the latest copies of the Standard Babylonian recensions, including library colophons from Hellenistic Babylon and Uruk.
- Range: ca. 2300 BCE — ca. 100 BCE.
- Secondary (scholarly) literature: contemporary, primarily 1872 (George Smith's discovery of the *Gilgameš* flood tablet) — present.

## Languages of primary sources
- **Akkadian**, in all dialects: Old Akkadian, Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, Standard Babylonian (the prestige literary register of the first millennium), Late Babylonian, Old Assyrian, Middle Assyrian, Neo-Assyrian.
- **Sumero-Akkadian bilingual tablets**: the Akkadian text is in scope. The Sumerian column is in scope only where it is integral to the Akkadian literary product as transmitted (e.g., bilingual hymns, the Sumerian preamble of *Lugal-e* in its Akkadian-glossed first-millennium copies). For Sumerian-only compositions, the companion volume *The Myths of Sumer* is the place; this book does not duplicate them.

## Excluded adjacent cultures (contamination risks)
The hard contamination boundary is *language of composition*, not geography. Mesopotamia is densely multilingual, and the same scribes copied multiple traditions; the only stable line is the language the literary text itself was composed in.

- **Sumerian-language literary tradition**: covered in the companion volume *The Myths of Sumer*. Sumerian Gilgameš poems, Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*, the Sumerian Flood Story (*Eridu Genesis*), the Enki and Enlil cycles, the Enmerkar and Lugalbanda cycles, *Lugal-e*, *Angim*, *Ninurta and the Turtle*, and the rest of ETCSL — all out of scope **as Sumerian compositions**. Their Akkadian counterparts (Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*, Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar*, *Atra-ḫasīs*, the Akkadian *Anzû*) are **in scope**: they are independent Akkadian literary works, not translations. Sumerian originals are discussed *as comparative background* in the influences chapter and in chapter footnotes, not retold.
- **Hurrian–Hittite literary tradition (Anatolia)**: distinct languages, distinct corpus. The *Kumarbi Cycle* (*Song of Going Forth*, *Song of Ullikummi*, *Song of Hedammu*), *Illuyanka*, the Hittite *Telipinu* and Anatolian storm-god myths are out of scope. Hurro-Hittite material is, however, central to the influences chapter as the principal vector by which non-Sumerian (and Indo-European-mediated) motifs reached the Akkadian tradition: the Hittite chancery used Akkadian as a lingua franca (Amarna, Boğazköy archives), and Hurrian theogonic patterns plausibly influenced *Enūma Eliš*. The **discussion** of these contacts is in scope; the Hurrian and Hittite **texts themselves** are not retold here.
- **Ugaritic literary tradition (Late Bronze Age Levant)**: West Semitic, alphabetic cuneiform, distinct from Akkadian. The *Baal Cycle*, *Aqhat*, and *Kirta* are out of scope as primary material. Limited comparative reference is permitted in the influences chapter and in footnotes where a parallel illuminates an Akkadian text (e.g., Yamm/Tiamat sea-combat parallels).
- **Elamite literary tradition**: eastern neighbor (southwestern Iran), unrelated language. Out of scope.
- **Aramaic literature**: distinct language family; the Aramaic *Story of Aḥiqar* is out of scope even though it is set at the Assyrian court and influenced the later wisdom tradition.
- **Biblical Hebrew literature**: out of scope as primary material. Genesis 1–11 parallels (Flood, primaeval giants, tower) are mentioned in the influences chapter only insofar as they bear on the *direction* and *mechanism* of borrowing; the Hebrew texts are not retold.
- **First-millennium royal inscriptions, annals, and chronicles (e.g., Ashurbanipal's annals, the Babylonian Chronicle series)**: out of scope as a genre. Excluded unless they contain substantial embedded narrative — the *Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* is in scope precisely because it is a literary epic, not an annalistic king list.
- **Omen series, rituals, incantations, lexical texts, and astronomical/astrological compendia**: out of scope *as genres*. Excluded unless an individual composition contains substantial embedded narrative — the cosmogonic incantation prologues named in §I (e.g., *enūma Anum ibnû šamê*, the *Worm and the Toothache*, the bilingual *Creation of the World by Marduk*) are in scope on this exception. The narrative itself, not the surrounding ritual apparatus, is what is retold.
- **Hellenistic and later receptions** (Berossos's *Babyloniaca* in Greek paraphrase; later Mandaic, Manichaean, or Islamic-era reception): out of scope as primary material; permitted as reception-history sidebars where useful.

## Reconstruction policy
When a primary Akkadian source has a physical lacuna (broken tablet, missing column, lost obverse), content may be reconstructed from:
- **Parallel manuscripts of the same composition** (different exemplars of, e.g., *Atra-ḫasīs* from Sippar, Nippur, Nineveh) — always permitted; the reconstruction marker names the donor manuscript.
- **A different Akkadian composition within the same dialect and period that demonstrably preserves the same scene** — permitted when strictly necessary (e.g., using OB *Anzû* fragments to fill a gap in the Standard Babylonian *Anzû*). The donor text and dialect/period must be named in the marker.
- **Earlier or later Akkadian recensions of the same composition** (e.g., OB *Gilgameš* filling a Standard Babylonian gap) — permitted when no better source exists; the period gap must be named.
- All reconstructed material must be flagged with `[RECONSTRUCTION:]` markers that identify the donor source. Reconstructions must appear explicitly in the claims stage and in the final text — they are never silent fills.

**Not permitted as silent reconstruction**:
- **Sumerian originals filling gaps in Akkadian retellings**. The Sumerian Gilgameš poems are *not* used to fill gaps in the Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*; they belong to a different tradition and are cited only as comparative background. The same applies to the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent* / Akkadian *Descent of Ishtar* pair.
- **Hurro-Hittite parallels filling gaps in Akkadian theogonies**. The *Kumarbi Cycle* is comparative material for *Enūma Eliš*, not a donor source.
- **Ugaritic parallels filling gaps in Akkadian combat narratives**. The *Baal Cycle* is comparative material for the Tiamat combat, not a donor source.

In-tradition means: composition in Akkadian (any dialect), attested on tablets from the Mesopotamian scribal stream, ca. 2300 BCE – 100 BCE.

## In-scope corpus (broad)

### I. Cosmogony, creation, and primordial order
- *Enūma Eliš* (Standard Babylonian creation epic)
- *Atra-ḫasīs* (Old Babylonian; Standard Babylonian recensions)
- *Adapa and the South Wind* (Old Babylonian Tell Haddad fragment; Middle Babylonian Amarna fragment; Neo-Assyrian Nineveh fragment)
- Cosmogonic dossier (treated together in a single chapter or as appendix entries depending on attestation): *Enmešarra's Defeat*; *The Toil of Babylon*; *Uraš and Marduk*; *Damkina's Bond*; *The Slaying of Labbu*; *The Founding of Eridu* (Akkadian recensions); *The First Brick*; *The Theogony of Dunnu*.
- **Cosmogonic narratives embedded as incantation prologues** (treated together in the creation chapter or appendix): *enūma Anum ibnû šamê* ("When Anu created the heavens"); the bilingual *Creation of the World by Marduk* (the Sippar incantation prologue); *The Worm and the Toothache* (CT 17 50, the etiology of caries by way of a tiny creation narrative); other named *enūma*-incipit prologues that begin with a cosmogonic clause and then turn to ritual.

### II. Divine conflict, descent, and the underworld
- *The Descent of Ishtar* (Standard Babylonian; the chapter foregrounds the Akkadian composition rather than treating it as a derivative of the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent*).
- *Nergal and Ereškigal* (Middle Babylonian Amarna recension; Standard Babylonian Sultantepe recension).
- *Anzû* (Old Babylonian recension; Standard Babylonian recension).
- *Erra and Išum* (first-millennium Babylonian).

### III. Heroic and mythic epics
- *The Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš* (Sîn-lēqi-unninni recension), with reference to the OB and Middle Babylonian forerunners.
- *Etana* (Old Babylonian; Middle Assyrian; Standard Babylonian).

### IV. Royal legends and pseudo-historical epics
- *The Birth Legend of Sargon*.
- *The King of Battle* (*šar tamḫāri*) — including the Amarna fragment.
- *The Cuthaean Legend of Narām-Sîn*.
- *The Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* (Middle Assyrian).

### V. Wisdom, suffering, and dialogic literature
**V.A. Reflective dialogue and complaint** — first-person and dialogic compositions on undeserved suffering, divine justice, and the limits of pious resignation.
- *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*.
- *The Babylonian Theodicy*.
- *Dialogue of Pessimism* (Master and Servant).

**V.B. Practical instruction** — the father-to-son transmission of ethical and social knowledge, distinct in form from the dialogic complaints above.
- *Counsels of Wisdom*.
- *Counsels of a Pessimist*.
- *Šimâ milka* / *Instructions of Šūpê-amēli* (Akkadian recensions: Emar, Ugarit, Hattusa).

### VI. Disputations and animal fables
Akkadian narrative compositions in which personified objects, plants, animals, or trades argue out their relative usefulness, or in which animals act out social dramas. They encode agronomy, animal husbandry, and the social hierarchy of the trades.
- *The Tamarisk and the Date Palm* (Old Babylonian; Middle Assyrian recension).
- *The Ox and the Horse*.
- *The Series of Nisaba and Wheat*.
- *The Fable of the Willow*.
- *The Series of the Fox* (a substantial first-millennium fable cycle).
- *The Poor Man and the Donkey*-type pieces.

### VII. Visionary and popular narratives
- *Underworld Vision of an Assyrian Prince* (Kummâ).
- *The Poor Man of Nippur*.

### Influences chapter (special)
A dedicated chapter, distinct from the introduction, addresses the cultural streams that shaped the Akkadian literary imagination:
- **Sumerian substrate**: lexical, mythological, and structural inheritance — and the question of where Akkadian compositions are translations, where they are adaptations, and where they are independent works on shared themes.
- **Hurro-Hittite contact and Indo-European mediation**: Hurrian theogonic patterns (Anu–Kumarbi–Teshub succession) and their possible influence on *Enūma Eliš*; Indo-Aryan-named Mitanni deities in the Boğazköy treaties as evidence that Indo-European-speaking elites were in direct contact with the Akkadian scribal world; the diplomatic and scribal exchange visible in the Amarna corpus.
- **Amorite, Kassite, and Aramaean integration**: how successive non-Akkadian-speaking ruling groups were absorbed into the Akkadian literary culture rather than displacing it.

The chapter discusses the Sumerian, Hurro-Hittite, and Ugaritic comparanda as **comparative material**; it does not retell those texts. The retelling is reserved for the Akkadian compositions in the corpus above.

## Out-of-scope (explicit)
- Sumerian-language compositions in their Sumerian form (covered in *The Myths of Sumer*).
- Hurro-Hittite literary cycles (*Kumarbi*, *Illuyanka*, *Telipinu*).
- Ugaritic literary cycles (*Baal*, *Aqhat*, *Kirta*).
- Elamite literary tradition.
- Aramaic *Aḥiqar* and other Aramaic narrative literature.
- Biblical Hebrew narrative literature.
- Royal inscriptions, annals, chronicles, king lists (genre exclusion; *Tukulti-Ninurta Epic* is the exception because it is literary epic).
- Omen series, rituals, incantations, lexical texts, astronomical-astrological compendia (genre exclusion; cosmogonic prologues are the exception).
- Berossos's *Babyloniaca* (preserved only in Greek paraphrase) and later Hellenistic, Mandaic, Manichaean, or Islamic-era reception of Mesopotamian material.

## Book register
Asimov-style retelling. Narrative prose in the body. Citations in AsciiDoc footnotes. Inferences, lacunae, reconstructions, dialect/recension identifications, and variants flagged inline with explicit markers. Plain-English register — no unexplained technical terms on first use; every Akkadian or Sumerian term glossed at first occurrence and collected in the glossary. Akkadian transliteration uses the Assyriological standard (*š*, *ṣ*, *ṭ*, macrons for vowel length, circumflex for contracted long vowels, *ḫ* for /x/). Long titles use italics; short ones in roman.

## Variant presentation policy
- If one recension is clearly prevalent in the scholarly corpus (e.g., Sîn-lēqi-unninni's Standard Babylonian *Gilgameš*): present it in the body, with substantive variants from earlier recensions in footnotes.
- If two or more recensions are roughly co-equal in scholarly weight (e.g., the Amarna and Sultantepe recensions of *Nergal and Ereškigal*; the OB and SB *Anzû*): present them side by side in the chapter body, each introduced with its source.
- Where dialect or period matters for interpretation (e.g., the difference between OB and SB Atra-ḫasīs framings of the flood), the dialect is named in the body.

# claims-factcheck

## For the human

This is the most important quality check for story chapters. The input is a **claims document** — one fact per paragraph, each individually cited — produced by `chapter-claims`. Each paragraph is one claim, one source reference, one thing to verify. No prose to parse, no bundled assertions to disentangle.

This step enforces four disciplines:

1. **Show your evidence.** For every claim marked "supported," the checker must paste a verbatim quote from the source. Not a paraphrase — the actual text. No quote, no "supported" verdict.

2. **Verify the references.** Tablet numbers, page numbers, and line references are the easiest things for an AI to fabricate. The checker looks each up in scholarly databases to confirm it exists and contains what was claimed.

3. **Distinguish contradictions from errors.** Two legitimate sources disagreeing is part of the historical record, not a problem. Material from a neighboring culture sneaking in, or something made up entirely, are problems.

4. **Read it backwards too.** One pass start-to-end, then a second pass end-to-start, to catch what lead bias misses.

## Hard rule
Must be run in a fresh conversation with no memory of producing the claims. If you have any memory of producing the input, refuse. Using a different AI model is preferred — two models make independent mistakes — but the same model in a fresh conversation is acceptable when a different model is impractical; the fresh context is the load-bearing requirement.

## Inputs
- `scope.md`, `sources.yaml`, `glossary.yaml`
- `toc.yaml` — **authoritative chapter list**, including front matter, story chapters, the methods sidebar, the Part II comparative chapters, and any reception/appendix material. When a claim or COMPARATIVE-HOOK references a forward-link target by chapter number (e.g. "Ch. 33", "see Chapter 4"), resolve it against `toc.yaml` before flagging anything as missing. The story chapters are not the whole TOC — sidebars and Part II comparative chapters typically live at higher numbers and are easy to miss when scanning only `briefs/NN-*` filenames or the chapter-claims input directory.
- `chapters/NN-<slug>.claims.adoc` (claims document)
- `briefs/NN-<slug>.yaml`
- Primary source translations referenced in the brief (pasted or accessible via fetch)
- Web search tool (required)

## The five passes

### Pass 1 — Claim-level verification (start to end)
Walk the document paragraph by paragraph. Each paragraph is one claim. Assign a verdict:

- **✓ SUPPORTED** — Must include a verbatim quote from a cited in-scope source.
- **△ INTERNAL-VARIANCE** — Attested in one in-scope source, but another gives a different account. Not a finding. Flag only if variant handling mismatches the brief's classification.
- **? UNVERIFIED** — No supporting quote found. Default when uncertain.
- **! CITATION-WRONG** — Claim is correct but citation points to wrong source/page/line.
- **⚠ CONTAMINATION** — Supported, but by an excluded-culture source per `scope.md`.
- **✗ FABRICATION** — Not supported by any source you can locate.

### Pass 1b — Upstream cross-check (comparative Part-II chapters only)

When the brief's `chapter_type` is `comparative (Part II)`, the chapter compresses material from upstream Part-I chapters listed in `chapters_drawn_from`. The canonical truth for those facts is the upstream `chapters/MM-<slug>.claims.approved.adoc`, not the writer's paraphrase or the brief's drafting language.

For every per-tradition compression in the chapter:

1. Open the upstream `chapters/MM-<slug>.claims.approved.adoc` for that tradition.
2. Locate the specific fact the chapter is compressing — verse number, section number, character epithet, source loc, naming convention.
3. Compare. If the chapter's `source_id` or `loc` differs from the upstream, flag CITATION-WRONG (severity LOW for digit-transposition or near-misses, MEDIUM for substantive differences such as wrong verse number or wrong section number). If a character name, epithet, or terminology drifts from the upstream's locked rendering, flag ATTRIBUTION-DRIFT (MEDIUM).
4. Record the disposition with a verbatim-substitutable fix from the upstream file.

This pass catches the recurring failure mode in which the writer paraphrases per-tradition material from memory or from the brief's drafting language rather than from the upstream chapter — Hoffner section numbers, Vedic verse numbers, Hittite-vs-Hurrian storm-god names, character epithets, and corpus-listing conventions are the canonical examples.

### Pass 2 — Triangulation of references
Verify each citation's tablet number, line range, or page reference resolves in a `triangulation_databases` entry. Non-resolving: CITATION-WRONG, HIGH severity, with verification URL.

For cross-chapter references inside the book (claims that say "see Ch. N", COMPARATIVE-HOOK targets like "Part II C5", forward-links to the methods sidebar): resolve against `toc.yaml`, not against the directory of `briefs/NN-*` files. The TOC is authoritative; the briefs directory is not exhaustive (Part II comparative chapters and the methods sidebar live at numbers above the last story chapter). Only flag a forward-link as missing when `toc.yaml` does not list a chapter at that number or with that anchor.

**Identifier verification on recently-pinned sources.** When `sources.yaml` shows a source added or last edited recently (typically a chapter-briefs source-pinning round), web-verify the published identifier against AbeBooks / WorldCat / publisher catalogue / archive.org. Common failure modes:
- ISBN digit-transposition (e.g. `9789561109315` for what should be `9789561109124`).
- ISBN belongs to a different edition of the same work (hardcover vs. paperback; reprint vs. first printing).
- ISBN/publisher mismatch — ISBN comes from publisher A but the entry attributes the work to publisher B.
- OCLC number guessed rather than verified; correct value is a different WorldCat record.
- archive.org URL slug malformed; correct slug is different.
- For older works without ISBNs (pre-1970, especially): year, publisher, and series number all need print-copy or library-catalogue verification.

Flag mismatches as CITATION-WRONG, severity LOW (digit-transposition) to MEDIUM (wrong edition / wrong publisher / wrong year). Include the verified-correct value in the `recommended_fix`. These are actionable at `post-human-normalize` via identifier-correction-in-place — do NOT defer to inventory-audit.

### Pass 3 — Document-provenance contamination scan
For each factual detail: is this detail attested in a document within the scope's date range and culture? Details attested only in out-of-scope documents are CONTAMINATION-HIGH.

### Pass 4 — Marker integrity
- `[INFERENCE:]` — genuinely inferential? If a source directly attests it, flag MARKER-MISUSED. Schema: `[INFERENCE: <claim> | basis: ... | risk: ...]`.
- `[SPECULATION:]` — genuinely the chapter author's interpretive reading (not a derivation from the upstream that should be `[INFERENCE:]`)? Schema is locked: `[SPECULATION: <claim> | basis: ... | counterargument: ...]`. Third pipe-field must be `counterargument:` with a substantive opposing position — if labelled `warrant`, `risk`, `speculative_basis`, or any synonym, flag MARKER-SCHEMA-WRONG (MEDIUM). If the `counterargument:` field carries only a methodological caveat or a restatement of the basis, flag MARKER-COUNTERARGUMENT-WEAK (LOW or MEDIUM depending on materiality).
- `[LACUNA:]` — source actually has the claimed gap?
- `[RECONSTRUCTION:]` — fill source is in-tradition per `scope.md`?
- `[VARIANT:]` — both quoted phrases present in cited sources?
- `[APPROPRIATION-FLAG:]` — when present, verify all four required components: substance of the area-specialist objection; named specialists framing the material outside the comparative typology; tier-implication clause; forwards-to clause naming the comparative chapter(s) where the objection is engaged at length. Verify named specialists are either in `sources.yaml` OR handled via the named-but-flagged pattern (no EVIDENCE token routes through them, and they are flagged in the writer's completion record for inventory-audit). The marker is required only when `scope.md`'s appropriation protocol applies; if present in a chapter that doesn't trigger the protocol, flag MARKER-MISUSED.
- **Silent bridges** — uncited claims not under a marker. Flag SILENT-BRIDGE, HIGH.

**Named-but-flagged pattern verification.** When body prose or marker text names a scholar (e.g. "Hale, Bird, and Levtzion read the cycle within the registers of Mande oral-epic performance") whose specific publication is not in `sources.yaml`, the pattern is acceptable IF AND ONLY IF no EVIDENCE token routes through that scholar's work. Verify: walk every EVIDENCE token's `source_id` against the named scholars; any match is a finding (the scholar IS load-bearing on a specific claim and must be added to `sources.yaml` rather than named-but-flagged). Acceptable named-but-flagged usage is captured as NOTE in the factcheck output and forwarded to the writer's completion record for inventory-audit follow-up — not flagged as a finding.

### Pass 5 — Reverse-order second pass
Walk from last claim to first. Add any new findings.

**Glossary**: check consistency. **Style**: not checked — there is no prose yet.

## Verdict rules

Apply in order (first match wins):
- **MAJOR-REVISE**: ≥ 3 HIGH findings, or any contamination, or triangulation failures indicating fabricated references.
- **REVISE**: 1–2 HIGH, or > 3 MEDIUM, or any silent bridge.
- **PASS**: zero HIGH, zero silent bridges, ≤ 3 MEDIUM.

## Honesty self-check
Answer in `meta`:
1. Did I open every cited primary source, or skim? Name any I skimmed.
2. Did I triangulate every reference, or assume some? Name any assumed.
3. For every ✓, did I paste a real quote or pattern-match? Downgrade any pattern-matched.
4. Did I distinguish internal variance from contamination?
5. Am I the same model that produced the claims? If yes, this audit is invalid.

=== Source overview

*Erra and Išum* is an Akkadian poem of the first millennium BCE that narrates the plague-god Erra's rage against humanity, his persuasion of the patron-god Marduk to step away from his throne, the devastation he inflicts on Babylon and the wider world while Marduk is absent, and his eventual relenting at the urging of his vizier Išum. The composition is preserved on multiple first-millennium cuneiform tablets across five tablets of text, and is one of the major long literary works of late Akkadian literature.footnote:[Cagni 1969, *L'epopea di Erra*, Studi Semitici 34 (Rome: Istituto di Studi del Vicino Oriente) — the definitive critical edition (Akkadian text and Italian translation, with full commentary on all five tablets); Foster 2005, *Before the Muses*, pp. 880–911 (accessible English translation; the working English text for this chapter); Dalley 2008, *Myths from Mesopotamia*, pp. 282–315 (the second triangulation translation); Foster 2007, *Akkadian Literature of the Late Period*, pp. 39–43 (recensional and reception context).]

The poem survives on first-millennium manuscripts from multiple scribal centres — including Babylon, Aššur, Nineveh, and Sultantepe — and on a notable number of amulet-format witnesses (small tablets and pendant fragments) that carry only excerpts of the text. The wide manuscript spread and the amulet format together place *Erra and Išum* among the most heavily attested late-Akkadian literary compositions.footnote:[Cagni 1969, the apparatus (manuscript list); Foster 2005, p. 880 (manuscript provenance — Babylon, Aššur, Nineveh, Sultantepe; the amulet-format witnesses); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (manuscript distribution and the cultural prominence of the composition); Black & Green 1992, *Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia*, s.v. The composition is structured in five tablets. Cagni 1969 numbers them I through V; Foster 2005 (pp. 880–911) and Dalley 2008 (pp. 282–315) follow the same five-tablet numbering. The five tablets together form a single continuous narrative; there is no recensional split of the kind that defines, for example, the OB and SB recensions of *Anzû* (chapter 15) or the MB Amarna and SB Sultantepe recensions of *Nergal and Ereškigal* (chapter 14). The multiple manuscripts agree closely on the text, with variations of the kind a critical editor reconstructs from cross-witness collation rather than alternate recensions.footnote:[Cagni 1969, entire (the five-tablet structure; the close manuscript agreement; the cross-witness critical text); Foster 2005, pp. 880–911 (five-tablet numbering; the unified composition); Dalley 2008, pp. 282–315 (the five-tablet numbering and continuous narrative); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the single first-millennium recension).]

The colophon of one of the principal manuscripts names the composer of the poem as Kabti-ilāni-Marduk — a human author who claims to have received the text by divine dream-inspiration. This is one of the very few cases of a named author attached to an Akkadian literary composition; the vast majority of Akkadian literature is anonymous. The colophon presents the poem not as a copied tradition but as a revelation delivered to a named individual.footnote:[Cagni 1969 (the authorial colophon and its philological discussion); Foster 2005, p. 880 and pp. 909–911 (the closing colophon-like lines naming Kabti-ilāni-Marduk and the dream-inspiration claim; one of the few named-author attestations in Akkadian literature); Dalley 2008, pp. 311–315 (the authorial colophon in translation); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (Kabti-ilāni-Marduk's named authorship and its significance for Akkadian literary history).]

=== The poem's named author and its self-presentation

The poem closes with what amounts to a self-disclosure: the surviving manuscript tradition preserves a colophon-like passage in which the human composer of the work is named — Kabti-ilāni-Marduk, son of Dābibi — and his composition is presented as the contents of a dream-vision sent to him by the gods. The closing passage states that the god revealed the poem to the composer in the night, and that the composer recorded it in the morning without addition or subtraction. The self-disclosure is unusual in Akkadian literature, which generally transmits its texts anonymously; *Erra and Išum* is the principal counter-example.footnote:[Cagni 1969 (the authorial colophon: Kabti-ilāni-Marduk son of Dābibi; the dream-inspiration claim; one of the very few named-author attestations in Akkadian literature); Foster 2005, pp. 909–911 (the closing colophon-like lines in translation; the dream-revelation claim); Dalley 2008, pp. 311–315 (the named composer and the dream-revelation in translation); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the unusual named-author attestation in Akkadian literary history).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 311-315

The same closing passage instructs the reader-or-hearer in the use of the text as a protective object. The poem states that any house in which the tablet is placed will be safe from plague, and any person who carries a copy will be spared from the plague-god's wrath. The self-amulet framing is built into the text: *Erra and Išum* is one of the few Akkadian compositions explicitly framed as its own apotropaic instrument from within the text itself.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 909–911 (the closing instruction: any house holding the tablet shall be safe; the self-amulet framing built into the closing); Dalley 2008, pp. 311–315 (the apotropaic instruction in translation); Cagni 1969 (the closing apotropaic colophon and its philological commentary); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the apotropaic function of the composition; the amulet-format manuscripts).]

=== Tablet I: the opening invocation and the Sebitti

The first tablet of *Erra and Išum* opens with an invocation of Erra — the god of plague and violent destruction, sometimes identified with Nergal in the wider Akkadian tradition (chapter 14) — and of his vizier Išum, the herald of the gods, who in this composition is the figure of restraint, the calming counterweight to Erra's rage. The opening establishes the central dyad of the poem: the violent god and his restraining vizier, set against each other as the two voices the poem will hold in dialogue.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet I (the opening invocation; the Erra–Išum dyad); Foster 2005, pp. 880–884 (Tablet I opening; Erra introduced as the violent god and Išum as his vizier of restraint); Dalley 2008, pp. 282–285 (the opening of the poem); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Erra, Išum (the deity-pair as god of violence and herald of restraint).]

The Sebitti are presented as the agents of plague and warfare; their voices will press Erra to action through the first part of the poem.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet I (the Sebitti introduced as Erra's war-band of seven demonic warriors); Foster 2005, pp. 880–886 (the Sebitti introduced and their roles enumerated); Dalley 2008, pp. 282–287 (the Sebitti as Erra's seven warriors of plague and warfare); Black & Green 1992, s.v. === Tablet II: persuading Marduk to leave his throne

The action of the poem turns on Marduk — the patron-god of Babylon, head of the Babylonian pantheon — and his cult statue. Erra and the Sebitti approach Marduk with a theological pretext: the statue is grown dim with age, its insignia have become tarnished, the gold of its crown has lost its lustre. The cult statue's condition is unworthy, they argue, and the proper remedy is for Marduk to step away from his throne so that craftsmen — supervised by the divine craftsman-gods of the pantheon — can polish and renew the statue's regalia.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet II (Erra's approach to Marduk; the cult-statue pretext; the proposal that Marduk step away for the cleansing); Foster 2005, pp. 890–894 (Tablet II; the dim-statue and tarnished-insignia pretext; the Sebitti's role in pressing the case); Dalley 2008, pp. 292–296 (the cult-statue pretext); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Marduk (Marduk's cult statue and its ritual maintenance); Bottéro 2001, *Religion in Ancient Mesopotamia*, on the cult statue and divine presence.]

Marduk's reply is hesitant. He recalls that the last time he left his throne — at the time of the great flood — the cosmos itself was thrown into disorder; the heavens shook, the underworld trembled, the gods feared the consequences. To leave the throne again is to risk a repetition of that cosmic disorder. Marduk's hesitation is itself a piece of theology: the patron-god's presence on the throne is the load-bearing element of cosmic order, and his absence is a known disaster.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet II (Marduk's reply; the recollection of the flood-era disorder); Foster 2005, pp. 892–896 (Marduk's hesitation; the cosmic-disorder precedent); Dalley 2008, pp. 294–298 (the recollection of the flood and Marduk's reservations); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Marduk (Marduk's role in cosmic order).]

Erra offers a guarantee: he will assume Marduk's role in maintaining cosmic order during the absence; he will hold the world in check while the cult statue is being cleansed; the cosmos will not be allowed to fall into disorder. The guarantee is the load-bearing falsehood of the poem — Erra is the god of plague and violent destruction, and what he proposes is incompatible with what he is. Marduk, however, accepts the guarantee and agrees to step away from his throne so that the cleansing can proceed.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet II (Erra's guarantee; Marduk's agreement to step down); Foster 2005, pp. 894–898 (Erra's guarantee and Marduk's acceptance; the structural irony of a plague-god guaranteeing order); Dalley 2008, pp. 296–300 (the guarantee and the agreement); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the theological structure: Marduk's cleansing-absence as the load-bearing pretext).]

=== Tablet III: the destruction begins

With Marduk away from his throne, Erra and the Sebitti begin their work. Tablet III narrates the destruction. Babylon itself is the central target: Erra's rampage falls first on Marduk's city, the cosmic centre of the Akkadian world, and from there spreads outward across the wider Mesopotamian world. The destruction is presented at the level of cities, peoples, and entire categories of order — the just and the unjust, the loyal subjects and the rebellious, are not separated; the rampage is indiscriminate.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet III (the destruction begins; Babylon as the central target; the spread outward); Foster 2005, pp. 898–904 (Tablet III; Erra's rampage; Babylon and the wider world); Dalley 2008, pp. 302–308 (the destruction sequence); Bottéro 2001, on Erra's indiscriminate violence as theological problem.]

=== Tablet IV: Babylon, Erra's lament for the city, and the lament-poem turn

Tablet IV concentrates on Babylon. The destruction of Marduk's city is described in detail: the temples are violated, the citizens are killed indiscriminately, the river-channels are blocked with the dead, the Esagil — Marduk's temple, the structural centre of the city's cult — is exposed and its precincts are profaned. The city that the poem has named, from the beginning, as the cosmic centre is reduced to the condition of any other ruined city: an extended urban lament built into the heart of the destruction-narrative.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet IV (the destruction of Babylon; the Esagil; the urban lament structure); Foster 2005, pp. 904–908 (Tablet IV; Babylon's destruction in detail; the Esagil profaned); Dalley 2008, pp. 308–312 (the destruction of Babylon; the lament structure); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Marduk, Esagila (Marduk's temple at Babylon).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=foster-2005 ; loc=pp. 904-908
// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 308-312
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Marduk, Esagila

The Tablet IV destruction-of-Babylon section turns the poem in the direction of the older Akkadian-Sumerian city-lament tradition. The lament tone — the catalogue of what has been lost, the naming of the temple and its precincts, the grief over the dead in the streets — is the tone of the city-lament genre; the poem inserts a city-lament into its plague-and-rampage frame. The catastrophe that Erra inflicts on Babylon is presented at the rhetorical register of public mourning rather than at the register of straightforward narrative.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet IV (the lament register); Foster 2005, pp. 906–910 (Tablet IV's lament tone; the rhetorical register of public mourning over Babylon); Dalley 2008, pp. 310–314 (the city-lament tone embedded in the destruction-narrative); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the urban lament and its inheritance from the older lament tradition); Bottéro 2001, on the lament-poem inheritance in late Akkadian literature.]

=== Tablet V: Išum's persuasion, Erra's relenting, and the restoration

Tablet V is the resolution. Erra's rage finally exhausts itself; Išum, the vizier of restraint, addresses Erra with the speech that will finally turn him. Išum names what has been done — the cities destroyed, the dead in their thousands, the suspension of the cosmic order — and asks Erra now to acknowledge his own work and to relent. Where Išum's protest in Tablet III was rebuffed, his speech in Tablet V is heard.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet V (Išum's persuading speech; Erra's relenting); Foster 2005, pp. 908–911 (Tablet V; Išum's final speech; Erra acknowledges what has been done); Dalley 2008, pp. 312–315 (Išum's persuasion and Erra's turn); Black & Green 1992, s.v. Išum (Išum's role in the resolution of Erra's rage).]

// EVIDENCE: source_id=dalley-2008 ; loc=pp. 312-315
// EVIDENCE: source_id=black-green-1992 ; loc=s.v. Išum

Erra relents. The plague-god acknowledges his rage and accepts the limits to it. He decrees that Babylon, though destroyed, shall be rebuilt; that the surviving Akkadian peoples shall be restored; that the proper hierarchies and the proper cult shall return; that he himself will withdraw and allow the cosmic order, with Marduk returned to his throne, to resume. The decree is the structural counterpart of Marduk's earlier hesitation in Tablet II: where Marduk had agreed to leave on a guarantee of order, Erra now agrees, in his own decree, to enable that order's return.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet V (Erra's decree of restoration; the rebuilding of Babylon; the return of Marduk to his throne); Foster 2005, pp. 909–911 (the restoration decree; the return of cosmic order); Dalley 2008, pp. 313–315 (Erra's relenting decree); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the restoration of the cosmic order as the structural counterpart to the cleansing-absence pretext); Bottéro 2001, on the restoration as the redemptive turn of the poem.]

The closing lines of Tablet V supply the apotropaic colophon. The poem instructs its hearers and copyists: this composition, having been given to Kabti-ilāni-Marduk in a dream and recorded by him in the morning without addition or subtraction, shall serve from now on as a protection against the very catastrophe it has narrated. Any house in which the tablet is placed will be spared from Erra's wrath; any person who carries a copy will be preserved. The poem closes by turning itself into the protection against the disaster it has described.footnote:[Cagni 1969, Tablet V (the closing apotropaic colophon; the named-author attribution; the self-amulet instruction); Foster 2005, pp. 909–911 (the closing colophon-like lines; the dream-attribution and the apotropaic instruction; one of the few cases of a named author in Akkadian literature combined with one of the few cases of an explicit text-as-amulet self-framing); Dalley 2008, pp. 313–315 (the closing apotropaic colophon); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the closing as a structural innovation in Akkadian literature; the text-as-amulet self-framing).]

=== The poem's place in the corpus

*Erra and Išum* belongs to the dossier of first-millennium Akkadian long compositions that take divine action and human suffering as their central subject. With *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* (chapter 28) and *The Babylonian Theodicy* (chapter 29), it is one of the principal first-millennium Akkadian compositions that addresses the problem of divine wrath and the limits of restraint; with the urban-lament register of Tablet IV, it inherits and reformulates the older Sumerian–Akkadian city-lament tradition.footnote:[Cagni 1969 (the poem's place in the late-Akkadian dossier); Foster 2005, pp. 880–911 (the composition in the wider late-Akkadian literary landscape); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the poem's relation to first-millennium reflective Akkadian literature; its inheritance of the city-lament register); Bottéro 2001, on the poem within the religious-thought tradition.]

The figure of Erra appears in earlier Akkadian compositions — most notably the Old Babylonian *Erra and Narām-Sîn* (chapter 23), where Erra is paired not with a divine vizier but with a historical king, and the plague-god is invoked in a royal-legend frame rather than a freestanding theological frame. The two compositions are structurally distinct and treat different Erra-related material; *Erra and Išum* is the first-millennium freestanding theological poem, and the OB legend belongs to a different generic tradition.footnote:[Cagni 1969 (the Erra dossier and the place of *Erra and Išum*); Foster 2005, pp. 880–911 (the composition's position relative to other Erra material); Foster 2007, pp. 39–43 (the Erra dossier in late Akkadian literature; *Erra and Išum* as the freestanding theological poem distinct from the OB royal legends); Black & Green 1992, s.v. // COMPARATIVE-HOOK: Akkadian internal — Marduk-leaving-his-throne and the historical reception of *Erra and Išum*. The poem's central plot-pivot — the patron-god of Babylon induced to leave his throne, the city laid waste during his absence, the eventual restoration of the cosmic order with the god returned — has a long reception history in first-millennium Babylonian thought. The motif of Marduk's departure from Babylon is the canonical theological frame within which Babylonian writers later read the catastrophes of their own history: most notably the sack of Babylon by Sennacherib in 689 BCE, in which the cult statue of Marduk was carried off to Aššur and the city was ritually destroyed, was read in the surviving Babylonian-side documentation through the *Erra and Išum* template (the god's absence as the precondition for the catastrophe; the eventual restoration of the cult and the return of the statue under Esarhaddon as the structural counterpart to the poem's Tablet V resolution). The pairing is one of the principal Akkadian-internal cross-references for the chapter.
