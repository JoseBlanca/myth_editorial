== A Note on How This Book Was Made

This book is unusual in its authorship. The stories it retells -- Marduk and Tiamat, Indra and Vṛtra, Thor and Jörmungandr, and a great many more -- are ancient, set down in clay, papyrus, palm leaves, oral tradition, and parchment over the course of four millennia. The prose that retells them was written, almost from first word to last, by artificial intelligence. The human role was to choose the project, settle a small number of difficult framing decisions at the outset, approve what the machines produced at a handful of review points, and decide when the book was done.

The project itself is an experiment, and this book in particular is a particular kind of experiment. Its sister volumes each treat a single culture; this one is a cross-cultural comparison, tracing one motif -- the combat with a chaos-monster -- across some fifteen traditions, and then following the scholarly arguments about which of those traditions belong together, which were borrowed, and which the comparison flatters or distorts. The question behind it was whether a pipeline designed to produce careful single-culture retellings could also handle the harder work of comparison: weighing area specialists against comparativists, recording where the chaoskampf framing has been challenged or rejected, and refusing to flatten traditions into a template that does not fit them.

The work runs as a pipeline of discrete stages, each stage a separate job handed to an AI in a fresh conversation. The motif's attestations were catalogued in a first stage and audited in a second; each chapter was then built in three further stages -- a list of numbered factual claims anchored line by line to specific tablets, hymns, or codices, a check of those claims against the original translations, and a final draft that turns the verified claims into continuous narrative prose. A last stage confirms that the narrative reflects the claims faithfully and adds nothing that was not there. Between major stages the human author reviewed a short audit report, corrected any findings he wished to correct, and released the next stage. For the non-Eurasian traditions in particular, an explicit appropriation protocol required at least two area specialists to be cited by name before any comparative claim was made.

The tech stack on this book differs from its sister volume on Sumer. All stages were run by Claude -- there was no separate research phase done by another model -- but the cross-model discipline that the pipeline was designed around was, on this book, mostly held. The writing was done by Claude Opus 4.7. The line-by-line fact-checking of every chapter's numbered claims was done by Claude Sonnet 4.6, in a fresh conversation, against the same primary sources the writer had been given. The narrative-fidelity check that follows each chapter's draft -- the test that the prose adds nothing the claims do not warrant -- was likewise done by Sonnet 4.6 for the thirty-two single-tradition chapters of Part I. For the eight thematic comparative chapters of Part II, and for two late Part I chapters, the fidelity check was done by Opus 4.7 in a fresh conversation rather than by a different model. Isolation between conversations was held throughout; isolation between models was held for most of the book but not all of it. The reader should weigh the comparative chapters of Part II with that asymmetry in mind.

The machinery that binds all this together is a small Python program that assembles prompts from a library of instruction files -- one per stage -- and passes them from one AI to the next. Every factual statement in the book carries a hidden machine-readable token pointing to its source. A final assembly stage builds the bibliography from those tokens automatically, rather than by parsing the prose, which eliminates an entire class of citation errors. Inferences, lacunae, reconstructions, and variant readings are flagged inline with explicit markers; in the chapters on traditions where area specialists have rejected the chaoskampf framing -- the Rainbow Serpent, Nanabozho and Mishipeshu, Mwindo, Yu the Great -- the book records that rejection rather than overriding it. The book itself is written in AsciiDoc and rendered to EPUB and PDF through Asciidoctor.

What the human author whose name appears on the cover actually did was this: choose the topic; decide that this would be a cross-cultural book rather than a single-culture one and that the comparative chapters would be the spine of the book rather than a capstone; settle the inclusion question at scope-lock -- which fifteen stories went into the CORE tier, which thirty into RELATED, and which contested or contrastive cases needed dedicated chapters with their own framing; read the audit reports at the gates between stages; correct a small number of findings; press the button for the next stage; and, after the chapters were drafted, retitle the book from "Chaoskampf Across Cultures" to "The Slayer and the Dragon: Chaoskampf Across Cultures" because the original title was searchable but unwelcoming. The scholarly judgement, the prose style, the sentence rhythm, the marshalling of Watkins and West and Ballentine and López-Ruiz across thirty-two stories -- all of that came from the machines.

The reader should treat the book accordingly. The scholarship it draws on is real, and each factual claim is tied to a published source that the reader can look up. The stories it tells are the stories on the tablets and in the manuscripts. The appropriation warnings are the warnings the area specialists themselves issued, recorded rather than smoothed over. But the voice that tells them is not a human voice, and the comparative judgements that hold the book together -- what counts as a chaoskampf and what only resembles one -- are judgements made by an AI within the constraints of the brief. The reader should feel free to weigh the result on its own terms.
