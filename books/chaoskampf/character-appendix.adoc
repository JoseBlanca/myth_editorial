[appendix]
== Characters

A reference guide to the named characters who appear in this book. Physical descriptions are included only where attested in the in-scope sources; where the sources are silent, this is noted explicitly. Within each section, entries are ordered alphabetically by the form locked in the book's glossary. Within each "Appears in" list, chapters are ordered by the narrative chronology of the relevant mythology — cosmogonic before theogonic, theogonic before heroic, heroic before eschatological — not by chapter number.

=== Deities

[[char-adad]]
==== Adad

Akkadian storm-god, summoned by Anu in the _Anzû Epic_ as the first of three failed champions before Ninurta is named.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 561, Tablet I.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — first figure summoned by Anu to fight Anzû; refuses on the grounds that the Tablet of Destinies makes the rebel unassailable.

*Relevance.* Adad is the first element of the threefold-refusal pattern (Adad, Gerra, Šara) that establishes the need for a champion of a different kind — the structural feature that Annus argues passes from the _Anzû Epic_ into _Enūma Eliš_.footnote:[Annus 2001, introduction.]

[[char-ahura-mazda]]
==== Ahura Mazdā

The Avestan creator-god ("Wise Lord"; Middle Persian Ohrmazd), supreme benevolent power of Zoroastrian dualistic cosmology and structural opponent of Aŋra Mainyu.footnote:[Benveniste & Renou 1934, passim, on Ahura Mazdā as supreme creator-god in Zoroastrian dualistic cosmology.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-13-thraetaona-azidahaka, _Thraētaona vs. Aži Dahāka_>> — supplies the cosmological frame within which Thraētaona's combat against the dragon-creature of Aŋra Mainyu is intelligible as participation in the larger struggle of order against opposition.
* <<ch-37-C4-eschatological-displacement, _C4. Eschatological Displacement_>> — under whose sovereignty the cosmos is renovated at Frashokereti after Kərəsāspa's final killing of the freed Aži Dahāka.

*Relevance.* Ahura Mazdā is the supreme creator-god of Zoroastrian theology, on whose side every Avestan combat against creatures of Aŋra Mainyu is fought.footnote:[Benveniste & Renou 1934, passim; Watkins 1995, Part V ch. 45, on the dualistic alignment of the Aži Dahāka combat with the wider Ahura Mazdā / Aŋra Mainyu opposition.] His pairing with Aŋra Mainyu is one of the clearest dualistic-cosmological structures in the ancient Near East and supplies the theological grid within which the *xᵛarənah*-theology of Iranian sovereignty operates.footnote:[Benveniste & Renou 1934, on *Yašt* 19's _xᵛarənah_-theology under Ahura Mazdā's sovereignty.]

[[char-aigipan]]
==== Aigipan

"Goat-Pan" of Apollodorus's Typhonomachy, who with Hermes recovers Zeus's severed sinews from the Corycian cave; majority scholarly view identifies Aigipan as Pan under a goat-cult epithet.footnote:[Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

*Physical description.* No physical description bearing on the in-scope discussion survives.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-11-zeus-typhon, _Zeus vs. Typhon_>> — Hermes's partner in the recovery operation against Delphyne in Apollodorus's expansion.

*Relevance.* Aigipan is one of the Apollodoran expansion's most distinctive features, an unusual pairing of trickster-god with pastoral god in goat-form for a divine recovery operation.footnote:[Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

[[char-an]]
==== An

The Sumerian sky-god, named in _Lugal-e_ as the father, with Earth (Ki), of the demon Asag and invoked in the closing doxology of the poem.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 24–47, on Asag as son of An and Ki; lines 669–671, on the closing invocation of An.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-01-ninurta-asag, _Ninurta vs. Asag_>> — the parental sky-god whose union with Earth produces Asag, and one of the two senior deities invoked in the closing prayer for Ninurta's devotee.

*Relevance.* An is the highest-ranking deity of the Sumerian theogony.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 669–671, on An invoked alongside Bau as senior deity.] In _Lugal-e_ his role is genealogical and liturgical rather than narratively active: he supplies the parentage of the adversary and is named in the closing blessing.

[[char-anat]]
==== ʿAnat

Ugaritic warrior-goddess, sister and combat-ally of Baal in the Baal Cycle.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II introduction, on ʿAnat as warrior-goddess and Baal's ally; KTU 1.3 ii commentary on her combat scene.]

*Physical description.* In KTU 1.3 ii ʿAnat is described in a slaughter-scene wading in blood up to her thighs, with severed heads hung at her belt and severed hands at her girdle.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II, KTU 1.3 ii commentary, on the slaughter-scene description.] No further full-figure description is preserved.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-07-baal-yamm-lotan, _Baal vs. Yamm and Lôtan_>> — restrains Baal at the embassy from Yamm, slaughters at her shrine, intercedes for the palace-building, and lists a seven-headed serpent among her victories at KTU 1.3 iii.

*Relevance.* ʿAnat is one of the most active divine figures in the Baal Cycle and one of the most extensively narrated warrior-goddesses in any Late Bronze Age West Semitic corpus.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II introduction, on ʿAnat's prominence.] Her catalogue of victories at KTU 1.3 iii names a seven-headed serpent and is one of the loci that feeds the Lôtan-Leviathan reception argument.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II, KTU 1.3 iii commentary, on the seven-headed-serpent in ʿAnat's catalogue.]

[[char-angra-mainyu]]
==== Aŋra Mainyu

The Avestan "evil spirit" (Middle Persian Ahriman), cosmic adversary of Ahura Mazdā in Zoroastrian dualistic theology and the power who fashions Aži Dahāka as a destructive instrument against the good creation.footnote:[Benveniste & Renou 1934, passim, on Aŋra Mainyu as cosmic adversary; Watkins 1995, Part V ch. 46, on the *Bundahišn*'s account of Aži Dahāka's Ahrimanic origin.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-13-thraetaona-azidahaka, _Thraētaona vs. Aži Dahāka_>> — the cosmic source of Aži Dahāka and the structural opponent against whose creation Thraētaona's combat is directed.
* <<ch-37-C4-eschatological-displacement, _C4. Eschatological Displacement_>> — whose opposition is finally eliminated at Frashokereti.

*Relevance.* Aŋra Mainyu is the load-bearing dualistic counterpart to Ahura Mazdā in Zoroastrian theology.footnote:[Benveniste & Renou 1934, passim, on the dualistic structure.] His role as the source of demonic creatures gives the Avestan combat-corpus its distinctive structural alignment: every dragon-killing is a participation in the wider cosmic struggle.

[[char-ansar]]
==== Anšar

Elder Babylonian sky-god of _Enūma Eliš_, son of Lahmu and Lahamu, father of Anu, who commissions Marduk as champion of the gods.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 437, Tablet I theogony; Lambert 2013, Tablets I–IV.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-03-marduk-tiamat, _Marduk vs. Tiamat_>> — receives Ea's report of Tiamat's mobilisation, dispatches Ea and then Anu against her without success, and finally accepts Marduk's condition that his decree be supreme before sending him to fight.

*Relevance.* Anšar is the structural pivot of _Enūma Eliš_'s second tablet: the elder god who commissions the champion and whose authorisation makes Marduk's elevation possible.footnote:[Lambert 2013, Tablet II, on Anšar's commissioning role.] His distinctive gesture — striking his thigh and biting his lip in formulaic dismay — marks the assembly's despair before Marduk's emergence.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 444, Tablet II.]

[[char-anu]]
==== Anu

Akkadian sky-god (Sumerian An), son of Anšar and Kišar in _Enūma Eliš_; the senior sky-deity who convenes assemblies and confers regalia.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 437, Tablet I; Lambert 2013, introduction.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-03-marduk-tiamat, _Marduk vs. Tiamat_>> — fashions the four winds for the infant Marduk, fails as champion against Tiamat, and at the close of the epic confers his own number, fifty, upon Marduk.
* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — convenes the divine assembly after Anzû's theft of the Tablet of Destinies and successively summons Adad, Gerra, and Šara as champions.
* <<ch-05-kumarbi-ullikummi, _The Kumarbi Cycle_>> — the second sovereign of the Kingship in Heaven succession, deposed by his cupbearer Kumarbi who bites off and swallows his genitals.

*Relevance.* Anu is the standing sky-father across the Akkadian and Hurro-Hittite combat-myth corpora, a figure of formal sovereignty whose office is repeatedly transferred or assumed by the combat's victor.footnote:[Lambert 2013, introduction; Hoffner 1998, §14, on Anu's standing across the corpora.] In _Enūma Eliš_ his bestowal of the number fifty on Marduk completes the displacement of the older theology.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 485, Tablet VII.]

[[char-anzu]]
==== Anzû

Lion-headed eagle-monster of the Standard Babylonian _Anzû Epic_, originally a minor servitor in Enlil's chamber, who steals the Tablet of Destinies and is slain by Ninurta.footnote:[Annus 2001, introduction; Foster 2005, pp. 555–578.]

*Physical description.* Anzû's bodily form is a lion-headed eagle — a predator bird with a lion's head grafted onto its raptor body. The identification is confirmed both by the descriptive epithets in the epic and by an extensive iconographic corpus on cylinder seals, amulets, and temple sculpture from the third millennium BCE onward.footnote:[Annus 2001, introduction, on the lion-headed eagle iconography; Foster 2005, pp. 555–556, headnote on Anzû's physical form.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — born at the Apsû, attends Enlil at his bath, seizes the Tablet of Destinies, flees to the mountains, deflects Ninurta's first arrow by decree, and is killed in the second combat after his pinion-feathers are plucked.
* <<ch-01-ninurta-asag, _Ninurta vs. Asag_>> — listed in the Sumerian form Anzud in Šarur's catalogue of Ninurta's prior trophies.
* <<ch-03-marduk-tiamat, _Marduk vs. Tiamat_>> — appears, demoted, in Tiamat's eleven-monster brood in _Enūma Eliš_ Tablet I.

*Relevance.* The _Anzû Epic_ is read in current scholarship as the structural and thematic antecedent of _Enūma Eliš_, with the Tablet of Destinies, the threefold-refusal pattern, and the closing name-list passing forward into the Marduk composition.footnote:[Annus 2001, introduction, on the Anzû-to-Enūma-Eliš genealogy; Lambert 2013, introduction.] Anzû's reuse as a subordinate monster of Tiamat's brood is one of the clearest signals of that literary genealogy: the older epic's protagonist becomes the new composition's footnote.

[[char-apep]]
==== Apep

The chaos-serpent of Egyptian solar theology (Greek Apophis), Ra's nightly adversary in the _Duat_ and the personification of _isfet_.footnote:[Faulkner 1972, spells 7, 39, 108; te Velde 1967, chs. 3–4.]

*Physical description.* Across the six attestation strata Apep is morphologically stable: a great serpent of vast size, of unspecified-but-monstrous coils, capable of spitting venom, of swallowing the solar barque, and of regenerating after each apparent dispatch.footnote:[te Velde 1967, chs. 3–4, on Apep's morphological stability across the corpus.] The Amduat hour 7 narrates him coiling across the channel of the _Duat_ on the sandbanks; Book of the Dead spell 108 places him on the western horizon-mountain.footnote:[te Velde 1967, ch. 3, on the seventh-hour combat and on the western-horizon localisation in spell 108.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-16-ra-apep, _Ra vs. Apep_>> — the central nightly adversary across the Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead (spells 7, 39, 108), Amduat (hour 7), Book of Gates (gate 4), and the late-Ptolemaic _Book of Overthrowing Apep_ on the Bremner-Rhind Papyrus.
* <<ch-36-C3-cyclical-maintenance, _C3. Cyclical Maintenance_>> — type-specimen, alongside Illuyanka, of the cyclical-maintenance subtype.

*Relevance.* Apep is the most extensively documented adversary in the Egyptian corpus, with continuous attestation from the Pyramid Texts (c. 2400 BCE) to the Bremner-Rhind Papyrus (c. 310 BCE), more than two millennia.footnote:[te Velde 1967, ch. 3, on the chronological range of the Apep material; Faulkner 1972, on the Book of the Dead spells.] His distinctive feature within the book's typology is regeneration: he is unkillable-because-recurrent, the daily-combat structure being constitutive rather than incidental.footnote:[te Velde 1967, chs. 3–4, on the regenerative capacity as articulating the recurrence-structure.]

[[char-apollo]]
==== Apollo

Olympian god of prophecy, music, and plague, who slays the Delphic serpent (an unnamed female *drakaina* in the Homeric Hymn, named Python in Ovid) at the foundation of the Delphic oracle.footnote:[Fontenrose 1959, chs. 1–3; Ogden 2013, ch. 2; Homeric Hymn to Apollo; Ovid _Metamorphoses_ 1.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope textual treatment of the dragon-combat passages discussed in this book.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-12-greek-dragons-after-typhon, _Greek dragon-combats after Zeus-Typhon_>> — slays the Delphic serpent with bow and arrows at Pytho; the killing founds the Delphic oracle (place-name Pytho derived from *pythein*, "to rot," from the dragon's body), and the Septerion festival re-enacts the killing every eight years.
* <<ch-09-revelation-red-dragon, _Michael vs. the Red Dragon_>> — figure of Yarbro Collins's Leto-Apollo-Python primary-substrate reading of Revelation 12.
* <<ch-36-C3-cyclical-maintenance, _C3. Cyclical Maintenance_>> — the Septerion festival's eight-yearly cycle supplies a Greek cyclical-maintenance reflex.

*Relevance.* Apollo is the Greek cult-foundation dragon-slayer, the figure on whom the Delphic oracle's founding depends.footnote:[Fontenrose 1959, chs. 1–3.] His epithets *Pythios* and *Pythoktonos* — "of Pytho" and "Python-slayer" — are the cult-titles that record the combat in the lived ritual language of Delphic worshippers.footnote:[Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

[[char-apsu]]
==== Apsû

Primordial fresh-water (Sumerian Abzu), male counterpart of Tiamat and consort in the opening generation of _Enūma Eliš_; killed by Ea in the prologue.footnote:[Lambert 2013, Tablet I; Foster 2005, pp. 436–439.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources; Apsû is named as a primordial water alongside Tiamat at the beginning of the epic and is described in the action only by his sleeping body, which Ea binds, strips of crown and divine radiance, and kills.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 439, Tablet I.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-03-marduk-tiamat, _Marduk vs. Tiamat_>> — primordial parent at the opening of the epic; resolves to destroy the noisy younger gods and is overcome by Ea's incantation, his corpse becoming the floor of Ea's new dwelling.

*Relevance.* The killing of Apsû is the first combat in _Enūma Eliš_ and a structural rehearsal for Marduk's later combat with Tiamat.footnote:[Forsyth 1987, ch. 2, on Apsû's killing as rehearsal.] His name passes to the cosmic fresh-water region at which Marduk is later born.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 439–440, Tablet I.]

[[char-aruru]]
==== Aruru

Senior Sumerian mother-goddess of _Lugal-e_, who reminds Ninurta that the fates of Asag's defeated stone-warriors remain to be fixed.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 411–434.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-01-ninurta-asag, _Ninurta vs. Asag_>> — opens the lapidary-aetiology sequence by prompting Ninurta to judge the stones.

*Relevance.* Aruru's intervention is the structural hinge between the combat and the long sequence of stone-judgements that occupies the second half of _Lugal-e_.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 411–434, on Aruru's prompting.]

[[char-athirat]]
==== Athirat

Ugaritic mother-goddess, El's consort and mother of the gods, whose intercession is necessary to secure El's authorisation for Baal's palace.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II, KTU 1.4 i–iii commentary on Athirat's intercession.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-07-baal-yamm-lotan, _Baal vs. Yamm and Lôtan_>> — receives gifts from Baal and ʿAnat in KTU 1.4 i–iii, then journeys to El at the Source of the Two Rivers and conveys the petition that secures the palace's authorisation.

*Relevance.* Athirat occupies the position of senior matron of the Ugaritic divine assembly, the mediating figure whose assent the palace-building petition requires before El will grant it.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II introduction.]

[[char-ashtart]]
==== Athtart

Ugaritic goddess of the Baal Cycle, who in KTU 1.2 iv intervenes at Yamm's defeat with a rebuke or warning to Baal regarding the disposition of the defeated rival.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I, KTU 1.2 iv commentary on Athtart's intervention.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-07-baal-yamm-lotan, _Baal vs. Yamm and Lôtan_>> — among the goddesses who restrain Baal from striking Yamm's messengers and intervenes at the moment of Yamm's defeat.

*Relevance.* Athtart appears alongside ʿAnat as one of the active warrior-goddesses of the assembly, though her role is much smaller than ʿAnat's; Smith treats her intervention at the moment of Yamm's defeat as one of the tablet's interpretive cruxes.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I, KTU 1.2 iv commentary.]

[[char-baal]]
==== Baal

Ugaritic storm-god (Ugaritic Baʿlu / Haddu), son of Dagan, combat-champion of the Baal Cycle, and the god whom Yahweh polemically replaces in the Hebrew Bible.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I introduction, on Baʿlu / Haddu in Ugaritic and the storm-god type.]

*Physical description.* No full-figure physical description survives in the in-scope sources. KTU 1.4 vii reports that Baal's voice — the storm-god's thunder — sounds through the window of his palace on Ṣapānu after the window is opened, an audible rather than visual mark of the figure.footnote:[Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II, KTU 1.4 vii commentary on the opening of the window and Baal's thunder.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-07-baal-yamm-lotan, _Baal vs. Yamm and Lôtan_>> — refuses Yamm's demand for his hand-over, fights Yamm with the named maces Yagrush and Ayyamur forged by Kothar-wa-Khasis, takes the kingship that El had initially conferred on Yamm, and (in Mot's retrospective taunt at KTU 1.5 i) is named as having defeated Lôtan.
* <<ch-08-yahweh-leviathan-rahab, _Yahweh vs. Leviathan and Rahab_>> — the figure whose storm-and-sea combat the Hebrew Bible polemically transfers to Yahweh.
* <<ch-35-C2-combat-as-sovereignty, _C2. Combat as Sovereignty_>> — load-bearing case for the rival-sovereignty reading of the wider combat-myth corpus.
* <<ch-39-C6-near-eastern-mediterranean-transmission, _C6. The Near Eastern → Mediterranean Transmission_>> — the Ugaritic input to the Phoenician-mediated transmission of the storm-god-vs-sea pattern into early Greek poetry.

*Relevance.* Baal is the load-bearing test case for Ballentine's rival-sovereignty reading of the combat-myth corpus.footnote:[Ballentine 2015, ch. 2, on Baal-Yamm as the central test case.] The Ugaritic Cycle's framing of his combats as divine-court politics — sovereignty granted, refused, transferred — supplies the interpretive grammar within which the related Hebrew, Greek, and Mesopotamian materials can be read.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I introduction; Smith & Pitard 2009, Vol. II introduction.]

[[char-bau]]
==== Bau

Sumerian healing-goddess invoked alongside An in the closing prayer of _Lugal-e_.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 669–671.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-01-ninurta-asag, _Ninurta vs. Asag_>> — invoked at the close of the poem in the prayer for the worshipper who maintains Ninurta's cult.

*Relevance.* Bau's appearance in _Lugal-e_ is liturgical rather than narrative; she is paired with An in the closing benediction.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 669–671.]

[[char-belet-ili]]
==== Bēlet-ilī

Akkadian "mother of the gods" (also Mami), named in the _Anzû Epic_ as Ninurta's mother.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 562–563, Tablet I; Annus 2001, Tablet I.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — addressed by Ea on Anu's behalf, summons her son Ninurta, arms him, and dispatches him against Anzû.

*Relevance.* Bēlet-ilī is the maternal commissioning figure of the _Anzû Epic_, the conduit between the divine assembly's call for a champion and the warrior-son who will answer it.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 562–563, on the commissioning role.]

[[char-birdu]]
==== Birdu

Messenger-god in the _Anzû Epic_, dispatched after the slaying to announce Ninurta's victory to Enlil.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 575–576, Tablet III; Annus 2001, Tablet III.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — enters Enlil's presence to announce Ninurta's recovery of the Tablet of Destinies.

[[char-coatlicue]]
==== Coatlicue

Mexica earth-goddess ("She-of-the-Serpent-Skirt"), mother of Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and the Centzon Huitznahua, who conceives Huitzilopochtli at Coatepec from a ball of feathers descending from the sky.footnote:[Anderson-Dibble 1950–82, _Florentine Codex_ Book 3; Matos Moctezuma 1988, interpretive chapters; Carrasco 1999, ch. on Coatepec.]

*Physical description.* The great Coatlicue statue in the Museo Nacional de Antropología in Mexico City — an Aztec-period andesite sculpture approximately 2.52 metres tall — depicts the goddess with a skirt of intertwined serpents, a necklace of human hands and hearts, two outward-facing serpent-heads as her own face (formed where her decapitated head would otherwise be), and clawed feet.footnote:[Matos Moctezuma 1988, interpretive chapters on the Coatlicue statue and its iconographic features.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-24-huitzilopochtli-coatepec, _Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and Coatepec_>> — sweeping at Coatepec, conceives Huitzilopochtli through the descending feather-ball; afraid as Coyolxauhqui and the Centzon Huitznahua approach, she is reassured by the unborn god's voice from within her womb.

*Relevance.* Coatlicue is the maternal pole of the Coatepec narrative; the Aztec andesite statue and the _Florentine Codex_ Book 3 narrative are independently witnessing presentations of the same earth-and-death-and-fertility goddess at the centre of the Mexica state cult.footnote:[Matos Moctezuma 1988, interpretive chapters; Carrasco 1999, ch. on Coatepec.]

[[char-coyolxauhqui]]
==== Coyolxauhqui

Mexica moon-goddess ("Bells-on-Her-Cheeks"), Coatlicue's daughter, leader of the Centzon Huitznahua's plot against their mother, dismembered by Huitzilopochtli at Coatepec.footnote:[Anderson-Dibble 1950–82, _Florentine Codex_ Book 3; Matos Moctezuma 1988; Carrasco 1999.]

*Physical description.* The Coyolxauhqui Stone, a circular Aztec andesite monolith excavated at the foot of the southern staircase of the Templo Mayor in 1978, depicts the dismembered moon-goddess at the base of Coatepec — head severed, arms and legs disarticulated and arranged radially around her nude torso.footnote:[Matos Moctezuma 1988, interpretive chapters on the Coyolxauhqui stone, on its iconographic depiction of the moon-goddess with severed head and disarticulated limbs arranged radially around her nude torso, and on its placement at the foot of the southern staircase.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-24-huitzilopochtli-coatepec, _Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and Coatepec_>> — leads the conspiracy of the Centzon Huitznahua against Coatlicue and is decapitated and dismembered by the newly born Huitzilopochtli, her body rolled down the slopes of Coatepec.
* <<ch-34-C1-combat-as-cosmogony, _C1. Combat as Cosmogony_>> — the defeated lunar pole of the cosmogonic combat through which the proper celestial order is established.

*Relevance.* Coyolxauhqui's dismembered figure is the load-bearing iconographic moment of the Mexica state-cult: the Coyolxauhqui Stone at the foot of the Templo Mayor's southern staircase is the architectural-iconographic re-enactment of the killing at the precise location where the narrative-moment occurs in the architecture.footnote:[Matos Moctezuma 1988, interpretive chapters; Carrasco 1999, ch. on Coatepec.]

[[char-damkina]]
==== Damkina

Akkadian goddess, consort of Ea and mother of Marduk in _Enūma Eliš_.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 440, Tablet I; Lambert 2013, Tablet I.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

[[char-di-jun]]
==== Di Jun

High god of the early-Chinese textual tradition, father of the ten suns and commissioner of Hou Yi to address the suns-crisis.footnote:[Birrell 1993, ch. on solar myths.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-22-hou-yi, _Hou Yi and the Suns_>> — sends Hou Yi down from the heavens to deal with a crisis caused by his own children, the ten suns.

*Relevance.* Di Jun's commission of Hou Yi places the archer-hero's authority for the killing in the heavens rather than in the human sage-king alone.footnote:[Birrell 1993, ch. on solar myths.]

[[char-ea]]
==== Ea

Akkadian god of wisdom and the deep fresh waters of the Apsû (Sumerian Enki), counsellor to the assembly and to the warrior-champion across the Mesopotamian and Hurro-Hittite combat corpora.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 555–578, on Ea in the _Anzû Epic_; Lambert 2013, Tablets I–VII; Hoffner 1998, §15, on Ea's role in the Kumarbi Cycle.]

*Physical description.* No physical description survives in the in-scope sources.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-03-marduk-tiamat, _Marduk vs. Tiamat_>> — kills Apsû with an incantation, fathers Marduk in the Apsû with Damkina, fails as the assembly's first champion against Tiamat, nominates his son, and at Marduk's behest opens Qingu's blood for the creation of humans.
* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — proposes Ninurta as champion after the threefold refusal, devises the pinion-feather strategy that defeats Anzû, and is the addressee of Šarur's report from the battlefield.

[[char-asag]]

*Physical description.* The text describes Asag's hide as of an order that does not yield to the ordinary instruments of combat — Šarur's third report warns that conventional weapons cannot penetrate the adversary.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 57–69, on Šarur's third report concerning Asag's hide.] On Asag's emergence the gods are reported as cowering and cosmic order shakes, and Šarur's later plea frames him as "storm-like and uncontrollable… a kind of weather-system with hostile intent."footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 168–186 and 265–280.] After his death his body is reduced to a heap of rubble and dust, the substance of stones.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 281–299.] No further morphological detail survives in the in-scope sources.

* <<ch-09-revelation-red-dragon, _Michael vs. the Red Dragon_>> — appears as the second sign in heaven in Rev 12, attempts to devour the male child at his birth, fights Michael and his angels, is cast down to the earth, pursues the woman into the wilderness, pours a river of water from his mouth that the earth swallows, and turns to make war on the rest of her children.
* <<ch-10-christian-reception, _St. George, St. Michael, and the Christian Reception_>> — central iconographic figure of the late-antique-and-medieval Michaelic and Georgian dragon-fight imagery.
* <<ch-37-C4-eschatological-displacement, _C4. Eschatological Displacement_>> — alongside Jǫrmungandr and Aži Dahāka, the type-case of the eschatological subtype.
* <<ch-42-reception-coda, _Reception coda_>> — the figure whose afterlife runs through Milton's Satan into modern reception of "war in heaven" imagery.

*Relevance.* The Red Dragon's seven heads invoke the Lôtan / Leviathan iconography; the "ancient serpent" gloss invokes both Genesis 3 and the Hebrew Bible Leviathan; and the assimilation to "the Devil and Satan" completes the Second Temple satanological identification.footnote:[Yarbro Collins 1976, ch. 3; Aune 1997–98.] Yarbro Collins's primary-substrate argument identifies the closest plot-shape match in the Greek Leto-Apollo-Python myth.

[[char-spartoi]]
==== Spartoi

"Sown Men" of the Greek tradition, armed warriors springing from the dragon-of-Ares's teeth that Kadmos sows at Thebes; only five survive their mutual combat to become the founding patrilineal lineages of Theban aristocracy.footnote:[Apollodorus _Bibliotheca_ 3.4.1; Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

*Physical description.* No continuous physical description survives beyond the narrative datum that they spring from the soil fully grown and battle-ready and immediately fall to fighting one another.footnote:[Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-12-greek-dragons-after-typhon, _Greek dragon-combats after Zeus-Typhon_>> — emerge from the sown teeth at Thebes, kill one another in mutual combat, and the surviving five become the Theban founding aristocracy.

*Relevance.* The Spartoi are the genealogical product of Kadmos's dragon-killing — the city's population literally generated from the slain dragon's body — making the Kadmos episode a city-foundation case in the strong sense.footnote:[Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

[[char-tiamat]]
==== Tiamat

Primordial salt-sea goddess of _Enūma Eliš_, mother of the first gods, cast as monstrous adversary after her elevation of Qingu and slain by Marduk; the cosmos is built from her split body.footnote:[Lambert 2013, Tablets I–V; Foster 2005, pp. 436–467.]

*Physical description.* In the opening tablets Tiamat is named as the primordial salt-sea, undifferentiated into gods or lands, mingled with the fresh-water Apsû. After her mobilisation she brings forth a host of monstrous champions with venom for blood. At the moment of combat the text describes her opening her mouth wide; Marduk drives the Imhullu wind into the open mouth so she cannot close her lips, her belly is distended by the inrushing winds, and she is rendered immobile. Marduk's arrow splits her belly and her heart.footnote:[Foster 2005, p. 458, Tablet IV.] After the killing he splits her body lengthwise like a flat fish, raises the upper half as the sky, makes mountains from her thighs, opens her eyes for the sources of the Tigris and Euphrates, makes springs of her nostrils, piles mountains on her head, bores wells through her skull, coils her tail to form the cosmic bond between heaven and earth, and arches her crotch as architectural support for the heavens.footnote:[Foster 2005, pp. 459–464, Tablets IV–V; Lambert 2013, Tablets IV–V.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-03-marduk-tiamat, _Marduk vs. Tiamat_>> — the cosmogonic mother whose death and dismemberment build the world: sky, mountains, rivers, springs, cosmic bond, architectural support.
* <<ch-08-yahweh-leviathan-rahab, _Yahweh vs. Leviathan and Rahab_>> — referenced in the Gunkel-thesis discussion of the *tehôm* / Tiamat cognancy at Genesis 1:2.
* <<ch-34-C1-combat-as-cosmogony, _C1. Combat as Cosmogony_>> — the type-specimen of the cosmogonic subtype.

*Relevance.* Tiamat is the figure on which Hermann Gunkel's 1895 Chaoskampf hypothesis was built, and the linguistic cognancy of her name with Hebrew *tehôm* (the deep of Genesis 1:2) is the cornerstone of the Gunkel argument.footnote:[Gunkel 1895, Teil I; Day 1985, pp. 49–52.] Ballentine's rival-sovereignty reading rests on the textual datum that Tiamat is not amorphous chaos: she organises an army, appoints a champion, distributes a token of authority, and conducts a war.footnote:[Ballentine 2015, ch. 1.]

[[char-tren-tren]]
==== Tren-Tren

Mapuche mountain serpent paired with the marine serpent Kai-Kai; their combat raises mountain and flood in the Mapuche cosmogonic narrative.footnote:[Koessler-Ilg 1954; Foerster 1993; Course 2011.]

*Physical description.* No continuous physical description bearing on the in-scope discussion of the chapter survives in the synthesis.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-27-mapuche-kai-tren, _Kai-Kai vs. Tren-Tren_>> — raises the land in opposition to Kai-Kai's flood, generating the Mapuche landscape.

*Relevance.* Tren-Tren is the mountain pole of the Mapuche paired-serpent cosmogony; current Mapuche-studies area specialists (Foerster, Bacigalupo, Course, Faron) read the pair within the Mapuche cosmographic frame rather than as a chaoskampf case.footnote:[Foerster 1993; Bacigalupo 2007; Course 2011.]

[[char-typhon]]
==== Typhon

The hundred-headed, serpent-limbed earth-born monster of Hesiod's _Theogony_ (variant name Typhoeus), child of Gaia and Tartarus; Zeus's last and greatest adversary, the closing combat of the _Theogony_'s succession-cycle.footnote:[West 1966, Theog. 820–880; Ogden 2013, ch. 2.]

*Physical description.* Hesiod's Theog. 824–828 describes the hundred heads of Typhoeus as serpent-heads springing from the monster's shoulders, with flickering tongues and fiery eyes. Theog. 829–835 describes his polyphonic voice — each of the hundred heads emits a different sound (the bellowing of bulls, the roaring of lions, the barking of hounds, the hissing of serpents), and the combined polyphony is itself uncanny.footnote:[West 1966, on Theog. 824–828, 829–835.] Hesiod's body description has serpents springing from the shoulders in place of arms or legs. Apollodorus's later expansion describes the body as enormous, surpassing all mountains in height, with serpent-coils for legs, dragon-heads erupting from the hands, and fire flashing from the eyes.footnote:[Ogden 2013, ch. 2, on the Apollodoran morphology.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-11-zeus-typhon, _Zeus vs. Typhon_>> — earth-born child of Gaia and Tartarus, struck by Zeus's thunderbolt at Theog. 853–858, scorched, cast down into Tartarus (later under Mount Etna in the Apollodoran tradition); in Apollodorus, severs Zeus's sinews at the Corycian cave and is finally defeated after Hermes and Aigipan recover them.
* <<ch-35-C2-combat-as-sovereignty, _C2. Combat as Sovereignty_>> — the Hesiodic kingship-combat case.
* <<ch-39-C6-near-eastern-mediterranean-transmission, _C6. The Near Eastern → Mediterranean Transmission_>> — the Greek pole of the Kumarbi-to-Hesiod transmission case, with the Apollodoran sinew-theft sequence as the principal point of Hurro-Hittite parallel.

*Relevance.* Typhon is the standing Greek serpent-monster, whose hundred serpent-heads and earth-born origin make him the Greek instance of the wider Indo-European storm-god-and-water-dragon complex.footnote:[West 2007, ch. 6.] His combat closes the _Theogony_'s succession-cycle and confirms Zeus's settled rule.

[[char-ullikummi]]
==== Ullikummi

Hurro-Hittite stone monster, Kumarbi's progeny by a great rock, who grows on the right shoulder of Upelluri to threaten Teshub's kingship.footnote:[Hoffner 1998, §15 (Song of Ullikummi).]

*Physical description.* Ullikummi is described in the Song of Ullikummi as a stone child of stone — a stone column rising from the sea up toward the heavens, growing at a prodigious rate measured in cubits and acres of growth per day. His stone constitution makes him deaf and blind (Šauška's seduction-attack therefore cannot reach him) and invulnerable to organic-combat methods.footnote:[Hoffner 1998, §15, on the stone-column morphology, the prodigious growth, and the deaf-and-blind constitution.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-05-kumarbi-ullikummi, _The Kumarbi Cycle_>> — conceived by Kumarbi on a great rock, planted on Upelluri's right shoulder, grows from the sea up to the gates of heaven where Teshub resides; resists Teshub's first storm-attack and Šauška's seduction; severed from Upelluri by the primordial copper cutter at Ea's identification, then defeated in the renewed combat.
* <<ch-39-C6-near-eastern-mediterranean-transmission, _C6. The Near Eastern → Mediterranean Transmission_>> — principal evidence for the comparative argument that Hesiod's Typhon descends from this Hurrian template.

*Relevance.* Ullikummi is the cycle's flagship adversary, the most cosmographically distinctive monster in the surviving Hurro-Hittite corpus: a stone column rising from sea to heaven, planted in the world's foundation itself.footnote:[Hoffner 1998, §15, on the cosmographic distinctiveness.] West reads the Ullikummi-Typhon parallel as the most strongly anchored Near-Eastern-to-Greek transmission case.footnote:[West 1997, ch. 6.]

[[char-upelluri]]
==== Upelluri

Hurro-Hittite primordial giant who bears the world; on whose right shoulder Ullikummi is planted and grows.footnote:[Hoffner 1998, §15.]

*Physical description.* Upelluri is presented as a primordial cosmographic figure: the giant on whose shoulders the heavens and earth were originally separated and rest.footnote:[Hoffner 1998, §15, on Upelluri as cosmographic foundation.] No further morphological description survives. His own awareness is dulled by his immense size and his cosmogonic age — he does not at first perceive the burden of the growing monster on his shoulder.

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-05-kumarbi-ullikummi, _The Kumarbi Cycle_>> — bears the world; Ullikummi is planted on his right shoulder; Ea travels to him to investigate the structural origin of the threat, and Upelluri's reply expresses his cosmographic obliviousness.

*Relevance.* Upelluri is the cosmographic foundation of the Kumarbi Cycle's universe, structurally functioning as a feature of the cosmos rather than as an active character.footnote:[Hoffner 1998, §15.]

[[char-vishaps]]
==== vishaps

Armenian "dragons," named in Khorenatsi's late paraphrase of the Vahagn hymn; also the name given to second-millennium-BCE stone stelae (vishapakar) associated with the cult.footnote:[Russell 1987.]

[[char-vrtra]]
==== Vṛtra

Vedic serpent-demon, the obstructing figure whose body blocks the cosmic waters; slain by Indra in RV 1.32 and across the Indra-cycle.footnote:[Jamison & Brereton 2014, RV 1.32.]

*Physical description.* RV 1.32.5 describes Vṛtra in mountainous-and-mountain-comparable terms: the serpent-adversary is "lying" or "sprawled" on the slopes, with a body that the hymn's imagery treats as itself a mountain or mountain-form.footnote:[Jamison & Brereton 2014, RV 1.32.5, on the body-as-mountain imagery.] The killing-blow with the *vajra* splits this body — *bhid-* "to split" being used so that the splitting of the mountain is at once the splitting of the obstructing serpent's body. RV 1.32.7 describes the slain Vṛtra lying broken across the channel as the released waters flow over and past his fallen body. RV 1.32.9 describes Vṛtra's mother Dānu lying above him as a cow above her calf.footnote:[Jamison & Brereton 2014, RV 1.32.7 and 1.32.9.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-14-indra-vrtra, _Indra vs. Vṛtra_>> — slain by Indra with the Tvaṣṭṛ-forged *vajra*; the killing splits the mountain, releases the seven rivers, separates heaven and earth, produces dawn.
* <<ch-15-vedic-reflexes, _Vedic and post-Vedic reflexes_>> — referenced as the Vedic adversary whose combat-pattern Trita Āptya's older stratum precedes and Krishna-Kāliya's bhakti-era reworking transforms.
* <<ch-34-C1-combat-as-cosmogony, _C1. Combat as Cosmogony_>> — discussed as a near-case in the cosmogonic subtype.
* <<ch-38-C5-ie-hero-slay-serpent-formula, _C5. The IE HERO SLAY SERPENT Formula_>> — the *ahi* of the áhann áhim formula, anchor case for Watkins's reconstruction.

*Relevance.* Vṛtra is the standing Vedic dragon-adversary, and the áhann áhim formula at RV 1.32.1–2 is the philological anchor of Watkins's reconstructed Indo-European HERO SLAY SERPENT line.footnote:[Watkins 1995, chs. 41–48; Jamison & Brereton 2014, RV 1.32 headnote.] His name derives from Vedic *vṛ-* "to obstruct," lexically transparent in its own language: Vṛtra is "the obstructor" of the cosmic waters.footnote:[Benveniste & Renou 1934, on the etymology.]

[[char-yamata-no-orochi]]
==== Yamata no Orochi

Eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent of Izumo, slain by Susanoo; its tail contains the sword Kusanagi, one of the Three Sacred Treasures of the Japanese imperial regalia.footnote:[Philippi 1968, _Kojiki_ book 1 chs. 19–20; Naumann 1996.]

*Physical description.* The _Kojiki_ describes Yamata no Orochi as eight-headed and eight-tailed, an enormous serpent extending across mountain valleys; the sword Kusanagi is found within the eighth tail when Susanoo dismembers the body.footnote:[Philippi 1968, _Kojiki_ book 1 chs. 19–20.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-23-susanoo-orochi, _Susanoo vs. Yamata no Orochi_>> — intoxicated with eight vats of sake by Susanoo, dismembered, and its tail discovered to contain the sword Kusanagi.
* <<ch-42-reception-coda, _Reception coda_>> — the figure whose iconographic afterlife runs through the Japanese Orochi tradition into the modern kaiju lineage (Godzilla and successors).

*Relevance.* The Yamata no Orochi episode is read by current Japanese-studies area specialists as sword-etiology and metallurgical allegory — the eight-headed serpent figuring iron-smelting along the rivers of Izumo, the sword's emergence from the tail figuring the metallurgical emergence of the bladed weapon.footnote:[Naumann 1996.] The chaoskampf framing is applied from outside in twentieth-century Western comparativist scholarship.

[[char-yamm]]
==== Yamm

*Physical description.* No physical description of Yamm survives in the in-scope sources beyond the narrative datum that he stumbles after Yagrush's first blow at his shoulders and falls under Ayyamur's strike at his skull.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I, KTU 1.2 iv commentary.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-08-yahweh-leviathan-rahab, _Yahweh vs. Leviathan and Rahab_>> — referenced as the Ugaritic prototype of the sea-as-adversary motif transferred polemically to Yahweh's defeat of the sea (Hebrew *yam*).
* <<ch-35-C2-combat-as-sovereignty, _C2. Combat as Sovereignty_>> — the load-bearing rival-sovereignty case of Ballentine's reading.

*Relevance.* Yamm is Ballentine's load-bearing case for the rival-sovereignty rather than chaos-vs-order reading of the combat-myth corpus: his name is the common noun for "sea" without primordiality marker, El initially confirms his kingship, his epithet "Judge River" gives him a juridical office incompatible with primordial chaos, and the post-combat narrative installs Baal in a palace rather than fashioning the cosmos from his body.footnote:[Ballentine 2015, ch. 2.]

=== Named Weapons

[[char-kusanagi]]
==== Kusanagi

"Grass-cutter," sword found in Yamata no Orochi's eighth tail when Susanoo dismembers the serpent; one of the Three Sacred Treasures of the Japanese imperial regalia.footnote:[Philippi 1968, _Kojiki_ book 1 chs. 19–20.]

*Appears in:*

[[char-mjollnir]]
==== Mjǫllnir

Thor's hammer (anglicised Mjöllnir), forged by the dwarves Brokkr and Eitri (or Sindri) in the smiths' contest of _Skáldskaparmál_ ch. 35, the storm-god's combat-weapon.footnote:[Faulkes 1987, _Skáldskaparmál_ ch. 35.]

*Physical description.* The hammer is described in _Skáldskaparmál_ ch. 35 as so potent that it never fails of its target and returns to the wielder's hand after striking; it has a short haft (a flaw introduced in the contest by Loki disguised as a fly biting Brokkr) which requires the iron gauntlets and the belt of strength *Megingjǫrð* to wield.footnote:[Faulkes 1987, _Gylfaginning_ ch. 21; _Skáldskaparmál_ ch. 35.]

*Appears in:*

*Relevance.* Mjǫllnir is the cosmic combat-weapon of the Norse storm-god, in the same structural position as Indra's *vajra* and Baal's Ayyamur and Yagrush.footnote:[West 2007, ch. 6.]

[[char-sarur]]
==== Šarur

Ninurta's animate talking mace, scout and messenger across both _Lugal-e_ and the _Anzû Epic_; one of the most cross-textually recurring "characters" of the Mesopotamian combat corpus.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2 lines 24–47, 96–118, 228–243; Foster 2005, pp. 568–569.]

*Physical description.* No physical description of Šarur as an object beyond the designation "mace" survives in the in-scope sources; the figure's distinctiveness is precisely that he is a weapon that speaks, flies, scouts, argues, counsels, and praises.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2, on Šarur as a speaking and flying mace; Foster 2005, on Šarur as messenger in the Anzû Epic.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-01-ninurta-asag, _Ninurta vs. Asag_>> — alerts Ninurta to Asag's emergence, recites the trophy-list of Ninurta's prior victories, urges retreat, flies back to Enlil at the E-kur as intercessor, returns with the conditional charge, delivers the targeted-killing instruction (pierce the liver), and after the slaying sings the victor's praise-song.
* <<ch-02-ninurta-anzu, _Ninurta vs. Anzû_>> — accompanies Ninurta to the mountain, returns to Ea after the failed first attack as messenger, carries Ea's pinion-feather plan back to Ninurta, and is the speaking subject who carries the warrior's report in direct speech.

*Relevance.* Šarur is the most distinctive structural feature of the Sumerian and Akkadian Ninurta material: an animate weapon that crosses-textually as a recurring speaking character, the "camera" of _Lugal-e_'s scouting sequences and the messenger of the _Anzû Epic_'s tactical exchange between warrior and counsellor.footnote:[ETCSL 1.6.2, on Šarur's animate-weapon role; Foster 2005, on the cross-text continuity.]

[[char-vajra]]
==== vajra

Indra's thunderbolt-mace, forged by Tvaṣṭṛ; the cosmic combat-weapon of the Rigveda.footnote:[Jamison & Brereton 2014, on the *vajra*.]

*Physical description.* The *vajra* is a forged divine artefact, the lightning-strike mythologised as a material implement; no further morphological description is supplied in the in-scope textual treatment beyond its being the weapon Tvaṣṭṛ forges and Indra wields in RV 1.32.2.footnote:[Jamison & Brereton 2014, RV 1.32.2.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-14-indra-vrtra, _Indra vs. Vṛtra_>> — instrument of the killing-stroke against Vṛtra in RV 1.32.6 and across the Indra-cycle.

*Relevance.* The *vajra* is the Vedic cosmic combat-weapon, in the same structural position as Mjǫllnir and Baal's named maces; through its forged-implement-of-the-lightning logic the combat acquires its concrete material image.footnote:[Watkins 1995, chs. 41–48.]

[[char-yagrush-ayyamur]]
==== Yagrush and Ayyamur

Baal's two named maces ("Driver" and "Chaser"), forged by Kothar-wa-Khasis at KTU 1.2 iv, addressed in second-person speech and instructed in their work.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I, KTU 1.2 iv commentary.]

*Physical description.* The maces are presented in the surviving Ugaritic text not as inert weapons but as named partners in the combat — Yagrush commanded to leap from Baal's hand and strike Yamm at the shoulders, Ayyamur to strike at the skull.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I, KTU 1.2 iv commentary on the targeted deployment.]

*Appears in:*

* <<ch-07-baal-yamm-lotan, _Baal vs. Yamm and Lôtan_>> — paired named maces of the combat with Yamm in KTU 1.2 iv; first mace's strike does not finish the combat, second mace's blow brings Yamm down.

*Relevance.* The named-mace pair is the load-bearing detail of the Ugaritic combat: weapons addressed in the second person and instructed in their work, structurally parallel to Ninurta's Šarur and a probable areal feature of Late Bronze Age combat-myth narration.footnote:[Smith 1994, Vol. I, KTU 1.2 iv commentary on the named-mace pair as a Ugaritic literary feature.]
